The bookLe livre

One July night in Moscow, while the Tsar gives a ball, the telegraph line to Siberia goes dead: a Tartar army has crossed the frontier, egged on by a Russian traitor, Ivan Ogareff, and the Tsar's own brother is cut off in Irkutsk. The Tsar sends a courier, Michael Strogoff, who must carry one letter across a country at war without once revealing who he is, not even to his own mother. On the road he meets Nadia, travelling alone to join her exiled father, and two rival war correspondents, an Englishman and a Frenchman. A race across the whole of Siberia, complete.

Une nuit de juillet, à Moscou, pendant que le tsar donne un bal, la ligne télégraphique de Sibérie se tait : une armée tartare a franchi la frontière, poussée par un traître russe, Ivan Ogareff, et le propre frère du tsar est coupé du monde à Irkoutsk. Le tsar envoie un courrier, Michel Strogoff, qui doit porter une lettre à travers un pays en guerre sans jamais révéler qui il est, pas même à sa propre mère. En chemin, il rencontre Nadia, qui voyage seule pour rejoindre son père exilé, et deux correspondants de guerre rivaux, un Anglais et un Français. Une course à travers toute la Sibérie, en texte intégral.

Chapter I — A Ball at the New Palace

"Sire, another dispatch."

"Where from?"

"From Tomsk."

"The wire is cut beyond that town?"

"It has been cut since yesterday."

"Send a telegram to Tomsk every hour, General, and keep me informed."

"Yes, Sire," replied General Kissoff.

These words were exchanged at two o'clock in the morning, just as the ball at the New Palace was at the height of its splendour.

All evening the bands of the Preobrazhensky and Pavlovsky regiments had played without a pause, working through polkas, mazurkas, schottisches and waltzes chosen from the very best of their repertoire. Couples of dancers multiplied endlessly through the magnificent reception rooms of the palace, which stood only a few steps from the "old house of stones", where so many terrible dramas had been played out in the past, and whose echoes woke that night to throw back the tunes of quadrilles.

The Grand Marshal of the Court, moreover, was ably assisted in his delicate duties. The grand dukes and their aides-de-camp, the chamberlains in waiting and the officers of the palace were themselves directing the dances. The grand duchesses, covered in diamonds, and the ladies-in-waiting, in full court dress, set a gallant example to the wives of the high military and civil officials of the ancient "city of white stones". So when the signal for the polonaise rang out, and guests of every rank joined in that stately procession which, at such ceremonies, has all the importance of a national dance, the mingling of long gowns tiered with lace and uniforms glittering with decorations made a sight beyond description, under the light of a hundred chandeliers multiplied tenfold by the mirrors.

It was dazzling.

And the great ballroom, the finest of all the rooms in the New Palace, made a setting worthy of the splendour of that procession of great personages and magnificently dressed women. The rich vaulted ceiling, its gilding already mellowed by the patina of time, seemed spangled with points of light. The brocade of the curtains and door hangings fell in superb folds and flushed with warm crimson tones, which broke sharply at the angles of the heavy fabric.

Through the panes of the great round-arched windows, the light that flooded the rooms, softened by a faint mist on the glass, showed outside like the glow of a fire, in sharp contrast with the night that, for a few hours, wrapped the glittering palace. This contrast drew the attention of those guests whom the dancing did not claim. When they paused in the window recesses, they could make out a few bell towers, blurred in the darkness, raising their huge outlines here and there. Below the carved balconies they could see many sentries pacing silently, rifles carried level on their shoulders, their pointed helmets plumed with a crest of flame in the glare of the light pouring from the windows. They could hear, too, the tread of the patrols beating time on the stone flags, perhaps more accurately than the dancers' steps on the parquet floors. Now and then the sentries' call was passed from post to post, and sometimes a trumpet call, mingling with the music of the orchestra, threw its clear notes into the general harmony.

Lower still, below the façade, dark masses stood out against the great cones of light cast by the windows of the New Palace. They were boats going down a river whose waters, pricked with the flickering gleam of a few lanterns, lapped at the foot of the terraces.

The chief figure at the ball, the man who was giving it, and whom General Kissoff had addressed by a title reserved for sovereigns, was simply dressed in the uniform of an officer of the Guards Rifles. This was no affectation on his part, but the habit of a man who cared little for show. His dress thus stood out against the superb costumes that mingled around him, and it was in this same plain uniform that he usually appeared among his escort of Georgians, Cossacks and Lezgins, dazzling squadrons splendidly arrayed in the brilliant uniforms of the Caucasus.

He was tall, with an affable air and a calm face, though his brow was troubled. He moved from group to group, but he spoke little, and seemed to pay only vague attention either to the cheerful talk of the young guests or to the graver words of the high officials and the members of the diplomatic corps who represented the leading states of Europe at his court. Two or three of these shrewd statesmen—readers of faces by profession—thought they had noticed on their host's features some sign of anxiety whose cause escaped them, but not one of them would have ventured to question him about it. In any case, the officer of the Guards Rifles clearly intended that his secret worries should not disturb the ball in the slightest; and since he was one of those rare sovereigns whom almost an entire world has grown used to obeying, even in thought, the pleasures of the ball did not flag for a moment.

Meanwhile General Kissoff was waiting to be dismissed by the officer to whom he had just handed the dispatch from Tomsk, but the officer remained silent. He had taken the telegram and read it, and his brow grew darker still. His hand even went, of its own accord, to the hilt of his sword, then rose to his eyes and covered them for an instant. It was as though the brilliance of the lights hurt him, and he sought darkness in order to see more clearly into himself.

"So," he went on, after drawing General Kissoff into a window recess, "since yesterday we have been cut off from the Grand Duke, my brother?"

"Cut off, Sire, and it is to be feared that dispatches will soon be unable to cross the Siberian frontier at all."

"But the troops of the Amur and Yakutsk provinces, and those of Transbaikalia, have received orders to march on Irkutsk at once?"

"That order went out in the last telegram we were able to get through beyond Lake Baikal."

"And the provinces of Yeniseisk, Omsk, Semipalatinsk and Tobolsk: we have been in direct communication with them ever since the invasion began?"

"Yes, Sire, our dispatches are reaching them, and we are certain that at this hour the Tartars have not advanced beyond the Irtysh and the Ob."

"And of the traitor Ivan Ogareff there is no news?"

"None," replied General Kissoff. "The Director of Police cannot say for certain whether or not he has crossed the frontier."

"Have his description sent at once to Nizhny Novgorod, Perm, Yekaterinburg, Kasimov, Tyumen, Ishim, Omsk, Elamsk, Kolyvan and Tomsk, to every telegraph station the wire can still reach!"

"Your Majesty's orders will be carried out at once," replied General Kissoff.

"Not a word of this to anyone!"

With a sign of respectful assent, the general bowed, melted at first into the crowd, and soon left the reception rooms without his departure being noticed.

Jules Verne (1828–1905)

Jules Verne needs no introduction; his English translations do. Many of the versions still in circulation were made in the nineteenth century, in haste, and they show it. Michel Strogoff (1876) is his great Siberian adventure, and the one in which he keeps both feet on the ground: no submarine, no balloon, no rocket. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

Jules Verne n'a pas besoin de présentation ; ses traductions anglaises, si. Beaucoup des versions encore en circulation datent du XIXe siècle, faites à la hâte, et cela se voit. Michel Strogoff (1876) est sa grande aventure sibérienne, celle où il garde les deux pieds sur terre : ni sous-marin, ni ballon, ni fusée. Les classiques en anglais moderne donnent en entier, dans l'anglais d'aujourd'hui, les classiques français que le lecteur anglophone ne connaît qu'en traductions anciennes ou abrégées. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
