The Eaglet

L'Aiglon

A drama in six acts (1900)

by Edmond Rostand · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Edmond Rostand · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

ForthcomingÀ paraître

The bookLe livre

Napoleon's son, kept at the Austrian court under a borrowed title, tutored to forget his father and watched by Metternich, who understands exactly what he is holding. The boy knows the whole of the legend and none of the world. Six acts move him from the drawing room at Baden to the field of Wagram, where he meets the only army he will ever command — the dead. It ran for months in 1900 with Bernhardt in the title role, and it is the biggest thing Rostand wrote after Cyrano.

Le fils de Napoléon, retenu à la cour d'Autriche sous un titre d'emprunt, éduqué pour oublier son père et surveillé par Metternich, qui sait exactement ce qu'il tient. L'enfant connaît toute la légende et rien du monde. Six actes le mènent du salon de Baden au champ de Wagram, où il rencontre la seule armée qu'il commandera jamais : les morts. La pièce tint des mois à l'affiche en 1900 avec Bernhardt dans le rôle-titre, et reste la plus vaste entreprise de Rostand après Cyrano.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

ACT ONE — THE WINGS THAT GROW

At Baden, near Vienna, in 1830.

The drawing room of the villa Marie-Louise is occupying. A vast room with the crystal balloon of an Empire chandelier hanging in the middle of it. Pale panelling, walls painted in fresco, a Pompeian green. A frieze of sphinxes running round the ceiling.

Left, two doors. The downstage one is the door of Marie-Louise's bedroom. The upstage one opens onto the ladies-in-waiting's apartments. — Right, downstage, another door; upstage, in a niche, an enormous faience stove, heavily ornamented. — Upstage, between two windows, a wide french window through which one sees the balusters of a flight of steps forming a balcony, going down into the garden. A view over the park at Baden: limes and firs, deep walks, lanterns hung from arched brackets. A magnificent day in the first days of September.

Precious furniture has been brought into this ordinary rented villa. Left, near the window, a fine lemonwood cheval glass loaded with bronzes; downstage, a vast mahogany table covered with papers; against the wall, a lacquer-topped whatnot filled with books. — Right, toward the back, a small Érard piano of the period, a harp; further down, a Récamier day-bed beside a large round table. Armchairs and X-stools. A great many flowers in vases. On the wall, framed engravings of the members of the imperial family of Austria; portraits of the Emperor Francis, of the Duke of Reichstadt as a child, and others.

Curtain up: at the back of the room, a group of very elegant women. Two of them, sitting at the piano with their backs to the audience, are playing a duet. — Another is at the harp. They are sight-reading. Laughter; interruptions.

A footman shows in, by way of the steps, a modest-looking young woman accompanied by an officer of Austrian cavalry, a magnificent hussar in blue and silver. The two newcomers, seeing that nobody has noticed them, stand a moment in a corner of the room. — At this point the Count de Bombelles comes in by the door on the right, drawn by the music. He heads for the piano, beating time. But he catches sight of the young woman, stops, smiles, and goes quickly to her.

SCENE I

THÉRÈSE, TIBURCE, BOMBELLES, MARIE-LOUISE, THE LADIES-IN-WAITING.

THE LADIES, at the keyboard, all talking at once and laughing like mad things.

She misses every flat! — It's a scandal!

— I'll take the bass. — One, two! — Harp! — La... la!... — Pedal!

BOMBELLES, to Thérèse

It's you?

THÉRÈSE

Good morning, Monsieur de Bombelles.

A LADY, at the keyboard

Mi... sol...

THÉRÈSE

I start as reader today.

ANOTHER LADY, at the keyboard

The flat!

THÉRÈSE

And thanks to you. Thank you.

BOMBELLES

It's perfectly simple, Thérèse:

you're a relative of mine and you're French.

THÉRÈSE, presenting the officer

Tiburce.

BOMBELLES

Ah, your brother!

(He holds out his hand, and points Thérèse to an armchair.)

Sit down a moment.

THÉRÈSE

Oh — I'm very nervous!

BOMBELLES, smiling

And about what, in heaven's name?

THÉRÈSE

About coming close to everything that's left on this earth

of the Emperor!...

BOMBELLES, sitting down beside her

Really? That's what it is, my dear?

TIBURCE, in an irritated tone

Our people used to detest Bonaparte!

THÉRÈSE

I know... but to see —

TIBURCE, a little disdainfully

His widow!...

THÉRÈSE, to Bombelles

And perhaps... his son?

BOMBELLES

Certainly.

THÉRÈSE

A person would have to have no more

soul than reading, and not be French,

and not be my age, in short, to be able

not to tremble, Monsieur, at the moment of seeing them.

— Is she beautiful?

BOMBELLES

Who?

THÉRÈSE

The Duchess of Parma!

BOMBELLES, surprised

But —

THÉRÈSE, quickly

She's unhappy, and that's a very great charm!

BOMBELLES

But I don't understand! You've seen her?

THÉRÈSE

Oh, no!

TIBURCE

No! We've barely been shown into this room.

BOMBELLES, smiling

Yes, but —

TIBURCE, eyeing the women at the piano

We were afraid of disturbing these ladies,

whose laughing was adding scales to the harpsichord!

THÉRÈSE

I'm waiting for Her Majesty, here, in my corner.

BOMBELLES, getting up

What?

But she's the one playing the bass at this moment!

THÉRÈSE, getting up, seized

The Emp —

BOMBELLES

I'll go and tell her.

(He goes to the piano and speaks low to one of the women playing.)

MARIE-LOUISE, turning round

Ah, is this the little one?...

A very touching story... yes... you told it to me...

A brother who —

BOMBELLES

Son of an émigré, still an émigré.

TIBURCE, coming forward, offhandedly

The Austrian uniform suits me well enough;

then there's the fox-hunting, which I adore.

MARIE-LOUISE, to Thérèse

There he is — the bad boy who eats up

the little you have left!

THÉRÈSE, trying to excuse Tiburce

Oh, my brother —

MARIE-LOUISE

A good-for-nothing,

who ruined you! But you make excuses for him; that's very good.

— Thérèse de Lorget, I find you charming.

(She takes her hands and sits her down beside her on the day-bed. Bombelles and Tiburce withdraw upstage, talking.)

So here you are among these ladies. I flatter myself

I'm pleasant enough company... a little sad, since —

— Alas!

(Silence.)

THÉRÈSE, moved

I'm so troubled that I can't

express —

MARIE-LOUISE, wiping her eyes

Yes, it was a very great loss!

Too few people knew that beautiful soul!

THÉRÈSE, trembling

Oh, indeed!

MARIE-LOUISE, turning round, to Bombelles

I've just written to have his horse kept!

(To Thérèse.)

Since the general's death —

THÉRÈSE, astonished

The general's?

MARIE-LOUISE, wiping her eyes

He kept that title.

THÉRÈSE

Ah! I understand!

MARIE-LOUISE

... I weep!

THÉRÈSE, with feeling

Isn't that title his finest glory?

MARIE-LOUISE

One can't know at first everything one is losing:

I lost everything when I lost General Neipperg!

THÉRÈSE, dumbfounded

Neipperg?

MARIE-LOUISE

I came to Baden to distract myself.

It's very good. Close to Vienna. An hour. — Ah, God, my dear,

my nerves!... They say that since I've grown thinner

I look like the Duchesse de Berry.

Vitrolles told me so. Now I curl my hair

the way she does. — Why didn't God take me too?

(Looking round her.)

It's small, but it isn't bad, this villa.

— Metternich is our guest, passing through. — He's here.

He leaves tonight. — Life at Baden isn't sad.

We have the Sandors, and Thalberg the pianist.

We get Montenegro to sing in Spanish;

then Fontana howls us something out of Figaro;

the Archduchess comes with the English

ambassadress; and one goes out in the landau... But it all slides

off my grief! — Ah, if that poor general —

— Are you thinking of coming to the ball tonight?

THÉRÈSE, who is watching her with growing amazement

But —

MARIE-LOUISE, impetuously

At the Meyendorffs'. Strauss is coming from Vienna.

— Bombelles, she must come, mustn't she?

THÉRÈSE

Might I ask Your Majesty

for news of the Duke of Reichstadt?

MARIE-LOUISE

His health

is good. He coughs a little... But the air is so soft

at Baden!... A young man! He's coming to the serious hour:

the first appearances in society! — And when I think, heavens,

that he's a lieutenant-colonel already!

But would you believe — it's an enormous grief to me! —

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
