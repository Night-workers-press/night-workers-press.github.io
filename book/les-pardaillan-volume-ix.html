From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I

RUE SAINT-HONORÉ

✦ ✦ ✦

A bright spring morning, caressed by wild breezes perfumed by the blossoming trees of the nearby gardens of the Louvre…

It was the hour when housewives go out to market. The Rue Saint-Honoré teemed with a motley, bustling crowd. Itinerant hawkers bearing their wares on trays, mendicant monks and blind men from the Quinze-Vingts with satchels slung over their shoulders, all came and went, deafening the passers-by with their "cries" launched in shrill voices, shaking their bells and rattles.

At the entrance to the Rue de Grenelle (now Rue J.-J. Rousseau), which was quieter, there stood a very plain litter, without armorial bearings, its leather curtains hermetically sealed. Behind the litter, a few paces back, rode an escort of a dozen strapping fellows armed to the teeth: fearsome-looking cutthroats of formidable aspect, despite the richness of their dark-hued costumes. All mounted on sturdy roans, all silent, stiff in their luxuriously caparisoned saddles, like equestrian statues, their eyes fixed on a horseman — another formidable equestrian statue — who kept to the right of the litter, close to the curtain. This man was an enormous colossus, a giant such as one rarely sees, with broad shoulders capable of bearing staggering loads without faltering, and who must have been gifted with extraordinary strength. He was assuredly a gentleman, for he cut a grand figure beneath his violet velvet costume of opulent simplicity, which he wore with an imposing elegance. Just as the ten formidable cutthroats — whose dreaded chief he undoubtedly was — kept their eyes fixed on him, ready to obey his slightest gesture, so he, indifferent to all that passed around him, kept his gaze constantly riveted on the curtain beside which he stood. He too, quite evidently, held himself ready to obey an order that might at any moment be issued from within that mysteriously curtained litter.

Finally, to the left of the litter, on foot, stood a woman: poorly dressed like a woman of the people, of irreproachable cleanliness, with a pallid complexion, a slimy smile, of indeterminate age — perhaps forty, perhaps sixty. She paid no attention to the litter against which she stood pressed. Her half-closed eye, with a peculiar fluttering squint, kept glancing constantly toward the Rue Saint-Honoré, attentively monitoring the ceaseless to-and-fro of the throng.

Suddenly she pressed her lips against the curtain and, in a low voice, issued this warning:

— Here she is, madame — it is Muguette, or Brin de Muguet, as they call her.

A corner of the heavy curtain lifted imperceptibly. Two wide, deep eyes, of a haunting gentleness, appeared between the folds and gazed with burning attention at the girl the old woman had just designated by this poetic name of Brin de Muguet.

She was a girl of barely seventeen, an adorable apparition of radiant youth, charm, and beauty. Slender, supple, she was enchanting in her coquettish, almost luxurious gown of vivid hue, revealing ankles of aristocratic delicacy and a dainty little foot elegantly shod. Beneath the turned-down collar trimmed with lace, from which emerged a neck of admirably pure line, a broad silk ribbon held before her a small wicker tray on which bouquets of flowers were displayed in a disorder that bespoke impeccable taste. Her eye mischievous, her smile touched with a hint of malice, her complexion of a dazzling whiteness that could have put to shame the fine lilies she carried before her, her step assured, quick, light, infinitely graceful, she moved through the throng with remarkable ease. And in a harmonious, singularly captivating voice, she cried her wares:

— Adorn yourselves with flowers!… Here comes Brin de Muguet with lilies and roses!… Adorn yourselves, fair ladies and gentle lords!

And the crowd greeted her — she who gave herself this fresh, sprightly flower-name: Brin de Muguet — with tender smiles and manifest sympathy. And seeing the eagerness with which the "fair ladies and gentle lords" — who were often no more than honest bourgeois or simple folk — bought her flowers without haggling, it was no less manifest that this little street flower-girl was the darling of the crowd, a small personage enjoying to the highest degree that inconstant, fragile thing called popularity. It is certain that this pretty name — Brin de Muguet — which seemed made for her, so wonderfully did it suit her — this name that some shortened to simply Muguette, fluttered on every lip with a kind of moved affection. It is equally certain that she must have been doing excellent business, for her tray emptied rapidly while the little leather purse hanging from her belt, into which she tucked her takings as she went, swelled steadily.

Behind Brin de Muguet, at a respectful distance, without her appearing to notice, a young man followed her every movement with the patience of a hunter lying in wait — or of a man in love. He was a very young man — barely twenty — slender, supple as a living blade of steel, proud, very elegant in his slightly worn grey velvet costume, making the enormous spurs of his long soft-leather boots — which moulded a slim, sinewy leg to mid-thigh — ring loudly. One of those dazzling physiognomies in which one could see a piquant blend of manly boldness and childlike timidity. He held in his hand a splendid white lily and, from time to time, he raised it to his lips with a sort of religious fervour, on the pretext of breathing in its scent. He had certainly bought this flower from the little street flower-girl. Seeing the passion-charged looks he fixed on her from afar, there could be no mistaking it: he was in love. A timid lover who, in all certainty, had not yet dared declare himself.

The mysterious invisible lady, who kept attentive watch behind the slightly raised curtains of her litter, did not notice this young man. Her great dark eyes of haunting gentleness — all that we can see of her for the moment — remained stubbornly fixed on the graceful young girl, studying her with a sureness of gaze that, with eyes such as those, must have been remarkable. After a fairly long examination, she let fall through the curtain, in a voice of strangely penetrating gentleness:

— This young girl seems to be very well known and much loved by the populace.

— If she is well known! exclaimed the old woman. I should think so, my lady! When I returned to Paris, a fortnight ago, I heard nothing on all sides but talk of Muguette or Brin de Muguet. I was far from suspecting it was her. When I encountered her by chance a few days later, I was so taken aback that I could not bring myself to approach her. And by the time I tried, she had vanished.

— And you are certain she is the same child who was entrusted to you as a newborn by Landry Coquenard?

— The very same Landry Coquenard who was then the confidential man, the willing tool of signor Concino Concini, who was not yet at the time… but enough… Yes, madame, it is she!… She is Concini's daughter!…

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
