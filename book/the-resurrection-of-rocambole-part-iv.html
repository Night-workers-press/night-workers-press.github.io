Chapter One

It was nearly midnight, and they had been talking since nine o'clock around a tea table in the drawing room of the Countess Artoff. The Countess Artoff was none other than that beautiful, unfortunate Baccarat, whom love had driven to repentance, and who for a long time, under the name of Madame Charmet, had been a guardian angel to the poor. One day God had taken pity on that broken heart, and one last ray of love had warmed all those ruins. Count Artoff—young, handsome, intelligent, worth millions—had loved Louise Charmet, already purified by repentance, and had offered her his hand. That had been eleven years ago. But happiness has the privilege of granting a second youth to those whose first youth was spent amid life's storms. Baccarat was forty; she looked scarcely twenty-eight, so lovely was she still. She might open the doors of her salon to all the most beautiful women in Paris—she remained their queen in beauty all the same. That evening, though, a fair, pale creature sitting beside her on a sofa rivaled the Countess Artoff in beauty, charm, and brilliance. This was the fair Vasilika Wasserenoff, the woman of mysterious vengeance, the implacable enemy of her young cousin Yvan Potenieff. The gathering was a large one. Count Kouroff was there, to whom Vasilika had promised her hand. Then three or four old friends of Baccarat's, among them Viscount Fabien d'Asmolles, husband of that Blanche de Chamery whose brother Rocambole had once, for a time, pretended to be. They had first talked about poor Yvan Potenieff.

"He's mad," Vasilika had said.

"Are you quite sure of that, madame?" the Countess Artoff had answered.

"Certainly I'm sure. Mad as a hatter. The Madeleine he talks about never existed."

Baccarat had looked at the countess with a doubtful air.

"Aren't you the one being deceived?" she had said.

Then she had hastened to add:

"That Monsieur de Morlux of yours, the man who's made himself inseparable from your cousin and brought him to France—I don't care for him at all."

"Oh?" Vasilika had said.

And, stealing a glance, she had shot a look of suspicious hatred at Baccarat. She sensed that the Countess Artoff had seen through her. But suddenly poor Yvan Potenieff—on whom Doctor Lambert was lavishing cold shower after cold shower in perfectly good faith—was no longer the topic of conversation. Why? Because a new figure had just come in and spoken a name that rang out like a thunderclap in the memory of most of the people present. This was a young man of twenty-seven or twenty-eight, a lawyer just beginning to practice, who haunted the courthouse, kept up with every piece of judicial news, and made it both his business and his pleasure to deliver, aloud, in half a dozen drawing rooms, a running chronicle of the courts. This young man's name was Paul Michelin. He had thirty thousand francs a year, was a good-looking fellow, and pleaded his cases for nothing. That evening Monsieur Paul Michelin had walked into the Countess Artoff's drawing room saying:

"You haven't heard?"

"Heard what?" they asked him, seeing his rather startled expression.

"Rocambole's been arrested."

At that name, Baccarat and Fabien d'Asmolles exchanged a pained look.

"Who is Rocambole?" asked the fair Countess Vasilika.

"Madame," replied Maître Paul Michelin, "Rocambole is a mysterious figure people talked about a great deal ten or fifteen years ago. He was the leader of a notorious gang of criminals known as the Knaves of Hearts."

"Charming name," said the countess.

"It seems that Rocambole, who had spent six rather peaceful years at the Toulon penal colony, woke up one morning feeling the need to leave it."

"Well, tell us the story—it sounds most entertaining," said the Countess Vasilika.

"Gladly, madame," Paul Michelin replied.

He had no idea that he was about to talk about Rocambole in front of people who, for the most part, had known the man very well. As for the beautiful Russian, she was not at all sorry to see the conversation turned aside, with the Countess Artoff thoroughly thrown off the scent where Yvan Potenieff was concerned. Monsieur Paul Michelin needed no urging. He recounted, in his best style, the well-known story of Rocambole—that is, the legend served up at the criminal court. But what he could not say, and what the courts had never known, was that the former leader of the Knaves of Hearts had been known to the whole of Paris as the Marquis de Chamery. Baccarat and Fabien d'Asmolles, who had felt a sharp pang of alarm at first, watching the young lawyer plunge headlong into his story, had ended up reassuring each other with a glance.

"Truly," said the beautiful Russian, "this man escaped from the penal colony?"

"Oh, in the most extraordinary way."

And the lawyer rattled off, all in one breath, the story of that escape, which he had read seven or eight months earlier in the Gazette des Tribunaux. Then he added:

"At the time, two versions of the story went around."

"Let's hear them," said the Countess Artoff, with studied indifference.

"It seems Rocambole didn't escape from the Toulon penal colony alone."

"Oh?"

"He had three companions. Instead of escaping the usual way, overland, they escaped by sea, seizing a longboat. The sea was so rough that night that word went round the next day that the four escaped convicts had drowned. That story held for a long time—but six months later..."

"There was news of Rocambole?" the Countess Vasilika asked eagerly.

"Yes, madame."

"How so?"

"About six weeks ago, a theft of a hundred thousand francs was committed against a man you certainly know."

"Who?"

"Viscount Karle de Morlux."

"Certainly we know him," said the fair Vasilika. "He's the one who brought my unfortunate cousin back from Russia. So—someone stole a hundred thousand francs from him?"

"Yes, madame."

A scornful smile crossed Baccarat's lips then, though until that moment she had sat silent and indifferent.

"And they accused Rocambole," she said.

"Naturally."

"So he hadn't drowned after all?"

"Apparently not."

"And how exactly did this theft take place?"

Monsieur Paul Michelin, who drew his information from a reliable source—namely the Gazette des Tribunaux—recounted what had been written and printed at the time about the broken doors, the forced writing desk, the footprints in the garden, and the ladder propped against the wall. But at that point Baccarat cut him off.

"Really, my dear Paul," she said, "can you tell us such nonsense with a straight face?"

"I beg your pardon?" said the lawyer, somewhat stung.

"What you're describing is a common highway robbery, my friend."

"Well?"

"And you're pinning it on Rocambole..."

"His name was mentioned at the time..."

Baccarat shrugged.

"My poor friend," she said, "Rocambole was far too clever a man for that. He wouldn't have bothered lowering himself to steal a shameful hundred thousand francs out of a writing desk."

"Did you know him yourself, then, madame?" Paul Michelin asked.

"Perhaps..." Baccarat replied, with a mysterious air that left the young lawyer thunderstruck. "And," she added, "I could tell you a good many things... But go on, my friend, we're listening..." she finished, in a tone that made it clear she would not say another word on the subject.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
