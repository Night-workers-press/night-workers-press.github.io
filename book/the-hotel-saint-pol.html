Dix heures du soir, dans ce coin de Paris qui va de la rue Saint-Antoine à la Seine, et un immense silence pèse sur une enceinte crénelée assez vaste pour loger dix mille hommes d'armes : une forêt de tours et de flèches où la musique des fêtes et les orgues de huit chapelles chantent tour à tour la gloire de Satan et celle de Dieu. Charles VI est fou, Isabeau ne l'est pas, et la cour est une forteresse avec une fête dedans.

CHAPTER I

THE QUEEN

Ten o'clock at night is about to strike…

In the hazy atmosphere of the summer night, in that corner of Paris which stretches from the rue Saint-Antoine to the Seine, it was a striking sight—that formidable crenellated enclosure upon which a vast silence weighed…

It is a giant fortress where ten thousand men-at-arms may be lodged, a forest of towers, of belfries, of spires, of pinnacles, a fantastical city where the music of festivities and the organs of eight chapels sing by turns the glory of Satan and that of God, while the roaring of the lions, from the depths of their cages, answers the watch-cry of the sentinels—a true feudal town, in short, where, across six gardens and fourteen courtyards, there spread at their ease the palace of the king, the palace of the queen, the mansion of the Archbishops, the lodging of Pont-Périn, the mansion of Saint-Maur, the palace of Beautreillis—sumptuous Gothic edifices dominating with their campaniles twenty other buildings scattered within the walls.

This immense domain bears a name that everywhere is murmured amid mysterious tales and extravagant legends:

It is called L'HÔTEL SAINT-POL.

Ten o'clock at night is about to strike…

In the queen's palace, all is silent…

At the far end of the magnificent bedchamber, in ceremonial dress—the cotte-hardie worked with silver, the veil of lace falling from the hennin—stand the three maids of honor, motionless.

Before a tall mirror of steel, her wrists encircled with emeralds, her hands glittering with rings after the fashion she has imported, her eyes of a black-blue, her hair a burning blond, dazzling in the radiance of her eighteen springs—Isabeau of Bavaria, the Queen of France.

She listens, she waits, she listens again; she is nervous, impatient, she sighs—and her whole body shudders when ten o'clock strikes… at last! Then she turns:

"For this night, you have leave. Escorts will conduct you home: to the Passavant lodging, Laurence; to the Hôtel de Coucy, Blanche; and you, Colette, to the Hôtel de Saveuse. Go."

As she speaks thus, her voice trembles as a voice trembles when a gust of madness ravages the brain. And her mask of strange beauty convulses under the strain of some terrible emotion.

The maids of honor bow in a slow curtsy, and when they rise, one of them—that girl, there beside the bed, so beautiful, so pure, so touching, Laurence d'Ambrun—has turned as pale as the wax of the torches that light the scene. Surely she is a soul in distress. There is despair in the bearing of this young girl, and her eyes reflect some grief without remedy… The queen pales in her turn. And in an altered voice, in which there vibrates one knows not what menacing suspicion:

"You have death upon your face, d'Ambrun!… Why?… Answer me!…"

"A faintness, Majesty," answers Laurence with effort. "If you deign to permit it, I will remain at the palace."

"Go home!" growls the queen. "Go home and rest this week," she resumes, more calmly. "Go, my dear. Tomorrow I will send you my healer at the Passavant lodging."

Laurence stammers a dismal word of thanks, and goes out with her companions.

"Oh!" she thinks, distraught, "she sends us away! It is the Comte de Nevers she awaits! I know it! I am sure of it… misfortune is upon me! Misfortune and… the chastisement!… Lord, Lord, punish me alone, and save the innocent one!…"

They cross the hall of Mathebrune, adjoining the private apartment: deserted!—then the hall of Théseus: deserted!—then the monumental gallery with its double colonnade: deserted! The queen's palace has been emptied!…

And while Blanche de Coucy and Colette de Saveuse, obeying the order, descend the majestic staircase of Egyptian granite, cross the courtyards and leave the Hôtel Saint-Pol, Laurence d'Ambrun remains there, leaning against a column of the gallery, her hands clasped, in despair. And in an accent of frenzy, she murmurs:

"He is going to come!… It is over!… Farewell to my last hope!… He loves the queen!…"

For some minutes Isabeau has listened to the sounds that grow distant, that fade, that die away. Then, sure of her solitude, she springs forward; with her undulating, supple step, she follows the same path as the maids of honor; but at the edge of the vast and superb staircase that descends straight to the vestibule, she stops as at the edge of a lake of shadow.

And suddenly there appears the one she awaits!

He is a young man of twenty-five, of a harsh beauty, of a rough elegance, broadly built, the brow violent, the lip disdainful, the cruel eye charged with mistrust. He mounts to Isabeau, sets one knee upon the flagstones, and murmurs:

"Jean sans Peur, Comte of the March of Nevers, awaits the orders of his queen!"

"Rise!" commands Isabeau.

And when he has obeyed, she hesitates, she trembles, her throat tightens, her breast heaves, her lips burn, and all at once:

"Why, in these eight days that you have been in Paris—at the lists, at the hunt, at the festivals given in your honor, everywhere—is it I, always I, whom you watch?"

In curt words, Nevers replies:

"Why, at the festivals and everywhere and always, is it you—you alone—who seize my thought, my gaze, and my soul?"

Jean sans Peur, avidly, contemplates her, studies her. His gaze, with its glints of steel, shines with a frightful cunning. And he too is in the full flush of youth! And if the queen's delirium is one of those phenomena that overturn all psychology, his own passion is more hideous still, for it is self-love driven to frenzy, for it is ambition burning, devouring! Himself! Himself! There is none but himself! He will break, destroy, crush everything in his path!…

"Madame," he murmurs, "ah! Madame, you do not condemn me, then?…"

"Condemn you! I!"

The cry has burst from the queen's lips—the cry that delivers her up like a wanton of the Champ-Flory, the cry that proclaims the downfall of her honor as a woman, of her dignity as a wife. And these are the first words she has ever exchanged alone with this man! And it is only eight days since, for the first time, she saw Jean sans Peur, absent from Paris these three years! And this is their first meeting!…

Dazzled, stammering shapeless vows, he has opened his arms and advances… but then Isabeau slips away!

She covers him with a look grave to the point of menace, and growls:

"I want you for myself, for myself alone, wholly—strength and thought, mind, soul, and body. Take care, Nevers! Take care before you answer the supreme question! Swear that you have in your life no bond of love."

"None!" answers Jean sans Peur.

"If there were one, swear to sever it… hear me well! It is not a mere rupture I demand: between you and me, I will have nothing 'living'… nothing!…"

Nevers raises his hand and swears!

In the far reaches of the gallery passes a moan… so faint that they do not hear it. And it is Laurence d'Ambrun who gasps:

"Farewell! Farewell to hope!…"

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
