The bookLe livre

Greece, the spring of 1856. Hermann Schultz, a young German botanist with a head full of Latin names, goes up Mount Parnes to look for plants and is taken prisoner by Hadgi-Stavros, the King of the Mountains: a courteous old brigand who keeps his money with a London bank, runs his gang as a joint-stock company and sends his shareholders an annual report. Captured with him are two Englishwomen, the formidable Mrs Simons and her daughter Mary Ann, held for a ransom of a hundred thousand francs. The funniest brigand story in French, as quick and dry now as it was in 1857.

Grèce, printemps 1856. Hermann Schultz, jeune botaniste allemand à la tête pleine de noms latins, monte sur le Parnès chercher des plantes et tombe aux mains d'Hadgi-Stavros, le Roi des montagnes : un vieux brigand courtois qui place son argent dans une banque de Londres, gère sa bande comme une société par actions et adresse à ses actionnaires un rapport annuel. Avec lui sont prises deux Anglaises, la redoutable Mrs Simons et sa fille Mary Ann, dont la rançon est fixée à cent mille francs. L'histoire de brigands la plus drôle de la langue française, aussi vive et sèche qu'en 1857.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — Mr Hermann Schultz

On the 3rd of July this year, at about six in the morning, I was watering my petunias and suspecting no harm when a tall young man walked in: fair-haired, beardless, wearing a German cap and adorned with gold spectacles. A roomy overcoat of hard-wearing worsted flapped mournfully about his person, like a sail against the mast when the wind drops. He wore no gloves. His shoes of undyed leather stood on soles so massive and so wide that each foot had a little pavement all round it. In his side pocket, somewhere about the region of the heart, a large porcelain pipe stood out in relief, its outline dimly traced under the shiny cloth. It did not even occur to me to ask this stranger whether he had studied at a German university. I put down my watering can and greeted him with a fine Guten Morgen.

"Sir," he said in French, though with a deplorable accent, "my name is Hermann Schultz. I have just spent some months in Greece, and your book has travelled everywhere with me."

This preamble filled my heart with a sweet joy. The stranger's voice sounded more melodious to me than the music of Mozart, and I turned on his gold spectacles a look that sparkled with gratitude. You would not believe, dear reader, how fond we are of those who have taken the trouble to decipher our scrawl. For my part, if I have ever wished to be rich, it was so that I could settle an income for life on everyone who has read me.

I took him by the hand, this excellent young man. I sat him down on the best bench in the garden, for we have two. He told me that he was a botanist, sent out on a mission by the Hamburg Botanic Garden. While completing his herbarium he had observed the country, its animals and its people as best he could. His artless descriptions and his views, narrow but sound, reminded me a little of good old Herodotus. He spoke ponderously, but with a candour that inspired trust, and he stressed his words with the emphasis of deep conviction. He was able to give me news, if not of the whole city of Athens, at least of the leading figures I had named in my book. In the course of our talk he expressed a few general ideas which struck me as all the more judicious because I had developed them before he did. By the end of an hour we were intimate friends.

I do not know which of us was the first to say the word brigandage. Travellers who have roamed through Italy talk painting; those who have visited England talk industry: every country has its speciality.

"My dear sir," I asked this treasure of a stranger, "did you meet any brigands? Is it true, as people say, that there are still brigands in Greece?"

"It is only too true," he answered gravely. "I spent a fortnight in the hands of the terrible Hadgi-Stavros, who is known as the King of the Mountains, so I can speak from experience. If you have the leisure, and a long story does not frighten you, I am ready to give you the details of my adventure. You may make of it whatever you please: a novel, a short story or rather (for it is history) an extra chapter for that little book in which you have heaped up so many curious truths."

"You are really too kind," I said, "and both my ears are at your command. Let us go into my study. It will be cooler there than in the garden, and yet the scent of the mignonette and the sweet peas will still reach us."

He followed me with very good grace, and as he walked he hummed a Greek folk song, one of the old ballads of the klephts, the mountain outlaws:

Down to the plains comes a klepht with eyes of jet;

His gilded musket rings at every stride;

He tells the vultures: "Keep close by my side,

I'll serve you the Pasha of Athens yet!"

He settled himself on a divan, tucked his legs under him like an Arab storyteller, took off his overcoat to be cooler, lit his pipe and began to tell his story. I sat at my desk and took it down in shorthand as he dictated.

I have always been a trusting soul, especially with people who pay me compliments. Even so, my amiable visitor told me such astonishing things that more than once I wondered whether he was having a joke at my expense. But his speech was so assured, and his blue eyes gave me so limpid a look, that each flash of scepticism died the instant it was kindled.

He talked without a break until half past twelve. If he stopped two or three times, it was to relight his pipe. He smoked with perfect regularity, in even puffs, like the chimney of a steam engine. Whenever I happened to glance at him, I saw him sitting calm and smiling in the midst of a cloud, like Jupiter in the fifth act of Amphitryon.

Someone came to tell us that lunch was served. Hermann sat down opposite me, and the faint suspicions that had been trotting about in my head could not stand up to his appetite. I told myself that a good stomach seldom goes with a bad conscience. The young German was too good a guest at table to be a faithless narrator, and his voracity vouched for his veracity. Struck by this thought, I confessed, as I offered him the strawberries, that for a moment I had doubted his good faith. He answered with an angelic smile.

I spent the whole day alone with my new friend, and not once did I feel the hours drag. At five in the afternoon he put out his pipe, got back into his overcoat and shook my hand, saying goodbye. I answered:

"Until we meet again!"

"No: goodbye," he said, shaking his head. "I am leaving today on the seven o'clock train, and I dare not hope ever to see you again."

"Leave me your address. I have not yet given up the pleasures of travel, and I may pass through Hamburg one day."

"Unfortunately I do not know myself where I shall pitch my tent. Germany is a large country; there is no saying that I shall remain a citizen of Hamburg."

"But if I publish your story, I must at least be able to send you a copy."

"Please do not trouble. As soon as the book is out it will be pirated in Leipzig, by Wolfgang Gerhard, and I shall read it. Goodbye."

Once he had gone, I carefully reread the story he had dictated. I found a few improbable details in it, but nothing that flatly contradicted what I myself had seen and heard during my stay in Greece.

Edmond About (1828–1885)

Edmond About had lived in Athens as a young scholar, and his first book about modern Greece offended the whole city. Le Roi des montagnes (1857) is his answer and his masterpiece: a comedy of money, pride and gunpowder from which nobody escapes, not the Greeks, not the English, not the Germans, not his own countrymen. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

Edmond About avait vécu à Athènes en jeune savant, et son premier livre sur la Grèce moderne avait offensé toute la ville. Le Roi des montagnes (1857) est sa réponse et son chef-d'œuvre : une comédie d'argent, d'orgueil et de poudre à laquelle personne n'échappe, ni les Grecs, ni les Anglais, ni les Allemands, ni ses propres compatriotes. Les classiques en anglais moderne donnent en entier, dans l'anglais d'aujourd'hui, les classiques français que le lecteur anglophone ne connaît qu'en traductions anciennes ou abrégées. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
