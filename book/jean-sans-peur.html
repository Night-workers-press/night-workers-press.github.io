CHAPTER I

Passavant's Journey

The chevalier de Passavant had halted, then, beyond the walls of Paris, a prey to a discouragement that broke within him every spring of vital force. With his way of viewing men and things in absolute terms, with his slight acquaintance with that real life which fashions events and beings in half-tones, he magnified the catastrophe beyond all measure.

There is but one thing in the world that cannot be set right: and that is death. All the rest is mended, patched, plastered over anew, for human thought clings above all else to the finding of a lodging, and there is no effort of which it is not capable in order to make shift even with a hovel. When everything cracks within our soul, when our thought finds itself driven out of the palaces it had raised for itself, it consents to concessions, and makes do with a cottage. Passavant did not know this. What, for that matter, did he know? Not much — and he was right glad to know nothing at all.

And so, to have missed the king's rendezvous was, to him, a catastrophe. He held himself dishonoured. He did not know that, even had he lacked the lawful excuses he was able to plead, Odette, had he rejoined her, would have pardoned him with a single look.

Passavant resolved, therefore, to return into Paris. He remounted his beast and resolutely turned his back on Paris, heading toward the north. So it goes with the most formal resolutions of a man whom a passion drives; readers, you are double creatures. Look into yourselves, and you will often surprise this phenomenon at work.

Passavant set himself in the saddle, saying: "I have not a maille. I am accused of a hideous murder. I am hunted by the vengeance of the queen, of Jean de Bourgogne, of his rabid henchmen. I am despised by the king who saved me, by that fair damsel who trusted in me. Well, then! I go back to Paris to have myself killed."

At the same time, he was taking the road to Dammartin.

Passavant took care to give himself no explanation of this apparent nonsense, which was, at bottom, of an irreproachable logic.

At Dammartin, he learned that, in assuring himself he had not a maille, he had uttered a cruel truth. It was harshly confirmed to him by the tuggings of his stomach. He was hungry.

He drew up before the perron of the inn of Saint-Éloi. He was sniffing the good smells that escaped from it, and contemplating rather piteously the pretty girl who, leaning against the doorway, considered him with a sympathy as little disguised as could be. Seeing that the chevalier said not a word, she opened the attack:

— This is the best inn in the country, my handsome captain. What is it, then, that you seek?

— The road to Villers-Cotterets, said Passavant, at all hazards.

— Ah! said she. It lies that way. — And she stretched out her arm. — But surely you cannot go all the way to Villers-Cotterets without dining?

— That is just what I was saying to myself, my pretty girl. But…

He set foot to the ground and seemed to consider attentively the image of the blessed Éloi, which swung in the sour breath of the north wind. It was cold. Through the open door he saw the bright blaze that beckoned to him. His heart was bruised. The fair eyes of the tavern-keeper promised him the consoling balm. What would you have had him do? What you would have done in his place: he went in, while a chubby-cheeked lad led his horse to the stable.

Before he had well collected himself, the chevalier found himself seated at table beside the great hearth. He felt himself invaded by well-being. He obeyed all the more readily his need to think of nothing, in that, very soon, the pretty girl was setting before him the rich omelette she had just tossed in the pan — rich, we say, in colour and in perfume, which is a richness like any other. The quarter of venison that followed was received by the chevalier with the gratitude of a stomach that cries for help. The mushrooms freshly gathered in the surrounding woods and sautéed in the pan among fine shallots, thyme, and rosemary, seemed to him an escort worthy of the handsome slice of roebuck likewise borrowed from the forest domains. A flask of grey wine helped the chevalier to see life a little less cruel. An idea that passed all at once through his head completed the restoring of his good humour. Here it is, in its simplicity:

— What is this silver clasp doing, that fastens the ribbon of my chaperon? Can I not do without it? To the devil with the ribbons of the chaperon and the silver clasp! Holà, my pretty girl, hear me. I have not the least denier. Good… Do not grow gloomy, and continue, I pray you, that clear regard of yours which comforts me. In place of écus, will you accept this clasp for the price of my dinner and my horse's?

The tavern-keeper examined the clasp. She chanced to be tolerably honest — we speak of the tavern-keeper — and she said:

— For the price of this clasp, my gentleman, you have a right, you and your beast, to another dinner such as the one you have just made.

— Well then! cried the chevalier joyously, put into one of my holsters a good measure of oats, into the other a pâté, some bread, a flask… and we shall be quits.

— All that shall be done, my captain.

Half an hour later, Passavant set out again. The pretty girl of the inn came to offer him the stirrup-cup, her eyes lowered, a smile at the corner of her lips.

When he reached Villers-Cotterets, the inn of the blessed Éloi had faded from his memory. He did not stop in that town where once Roselys had been exposed beneath the porch of the church, and, upon a direction that was given him, continued on his way toward the feudal castle which the duc d'Orléans had but lately completed, and where the king of France had sought a refuge — at least, so he believed. It was growing dark. The black sky was full of snows held in reserve. It was cold. Beneath his gloves of buckskin the chevalier felt the numbness of the frost. It was mournful. His heart sought life, and he saw about him nothing but the image of death.

While he mused thus, the reins upon his horse's neck, there befell him what befalls every horseman who wastes his time in musing: he lost his way. The horse climbed a steep slope, and came out at last upon a broad plateau where there rose, like the columns of a cathedral, ancient beeches stripped of their foliage. Alone, here and there, a few oaks still crowned themselves with leaves tinged with purple. Passavant stopped near a stack of wood that some wood-cutters were setting in order tidily.

— Where am I? he asked.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
