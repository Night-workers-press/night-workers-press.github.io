From the opening pagesLes premières pages

CHAPTER I Fairy or Witch

At the moment when this tale begins, France, in 1314, had for its king Louis X, called le Hutin. The queen, Marguerite de Bourgogne, and her two sisters, Jeanne and Blanche, secretly led a life of debauchery, and the Tour de Nesle became their favorite place of pleasure.

At that time the king was counseled in affairs of State by his uncle Charles, Comte de Valois, and by Monseigneur Enguerrand de Marigny, formerly ambassador to the court of Burgundy. The latter had once been the lover of the queen, who bore him a daughter, Myrtille; but the girl has always been ignorant of her father's high office, and she does not know her mother. The Comte de Valois, uncle of the king, had seduced a young lady of the court of Burgundy, Anne de Dramans. A son, Jehan, was born, whom Marguerite de Bourgogne, out of amorous hatred, resolved to have destroyed. The man charged with this mission, Lancelot Bigorne, hesitated at the final moment and saved the child.

Years have passed since then: Jehan — Buridan — has become a man, Myrtille a young woman.

The two young people love each other without suspecting the heavy past that lies behind them. The hatred which Enguerrand de Marigny and the Comte de Valois bear one another stands as an obstacle to their love. As for Marguerite de Bourgogne, she disdains the love of Philippe d'Aulnay, a friend of Buridan's. It is Buridan she loves; but he remains faithful to Myrtille, and the queen's hatred is tenacious: they shall both die.

Yet Louis X suspects that he is betrayed. He goes to the Tour de Nesle, finds papers there, seizes them... But Philippe d'Aulnay springs forward, snatches the compromising papers from the king's hands, sets them ablaze at a torch... Buridan is at his side.

Philippe d'Aulnay has saved the honor of the woman he loves, but he remains a prisoner of the king's archers, while Buridan and his friends take refuge in the Cour des Miracles.

Led by Bigorne, the king finds his uncle, the Comte de Valois, in the lower hall of the Tour de Nesle.

Valois, on seeing his nephew and king enter, uttered a cry of joy and rushed toward Louis, who clasped him in his arms, saying:

"So it must be I who come to pluck you from the hands of the Philistines?"

"Ah! Sire," cried Valois, "were I to live for centuries, never shall I forget that it is to you I owe my liberty and perhaps my life. That life, my dear Sire — dispose of it as you will; it belongs to you henceforth!"

As he spoke thus, in a surge of reaction after the hours of anguish and terror he had just endured, the Comte de Valois burst into sobs. At that moment he was sincere in his gratitude, and a kind of exaltation came over him at the thought that the king himself loved him enough to have taken the trouble to come and deliver him in person.

"But," he said, after the embraces and effusions that followed the first moment, "but, Sire, how could you have known?..."

"But tell me first, my worthy uncle," the king returned in his turn, "how could you have let yourself be taken, like a fox run down by the pack?"

"Sire," said Valois, "I will confess to you that before all else I am in haste to find myself in the open air again... and that I am dying of hunger."

Half an hour later, uncle and nephew sat down before a splendidly served table.

"Now that we are alone, Valois, tell me how this prodigious adventure befell you — to be seized in your own hôtel, despite a garrison of three hundred guards..."

Valois, in a few words, gave an account of what had taken place in his hôtel, and told how Buridan and Lancelot Bigorne, profiting by the darkness that reigned in the corridor where the brawl occurred, had been able to make their way inside.

As for saying why — from that he refrained, being determined to settle that particular point himself, with Simon Malingre.

"Do you know," said the king with admiration, "that those are rough men indeed!"

"Yes, Sire," said Valois in a somber voice, "rough and formidable. Were they alone, there would be no cause for concern — save to have them hanged. But, Sire, the misfortune is that these men are inspired by a man more formidable still, whose mere instruments they are. That man has sworn my ruin. That man, gall-bitten with jealousy, exasperated with hatred against the king's uncle, seeks to deprive you of your best counselor, of your most loyal servant, the most devoted, the most disinterested..."

"Of whom do you mean to speak, Valois?" said the king, in whom anger was already beginning to seethe.

"Of whom would you have me speak, Sire, if not of him who, having ruined my brother, Philippe IV, now seeks to ruin you in your turn? Of whom would you have me speak, if not of him who hates me because I have caught out his peculations, because he knows that I watch him, because he has need of shadow and silence, while I am the torch that gives light, the voice that accuses? Of whom would you have me speak, if not of the one man who has an interest in my disappearance?..."

"Do you believe, then," growled the king, "do you believe that Marigny would dare..."

"Ah! Sire, you see — it is you who pronounce his name, his accursed name! It is Marigny, Sire, who dispatched that band of truands against me with orders to assassinate me! The truands dared not go through to the end. They dared not lay hands upon me. But they would have left me to die of hunger and thirst in that dungeon from which my king came to draw me forth like an angel sent by God."

"By Notre-Dame," stammered the king, in whom fury was breaking loose, "if I were sure of it, I would have Marigny seized, I would have him thrown into a dungeon and leave him there to rot — or rather, I would have him hanged, yes, hanged, first minister of mine though he be, hanged upon those gibbets of Montfaucon which he offered me as a gift for my joyous accession."

At that moment Valois understood that he was staking his power, and perhaps his liberty and his life, upon a single cast of the dice.

He felt that, having entered upon the path of accusation, he must go through to the end; he must crush his rival forever beneath one of those formidable charges from which no man rises again.

His face grew darker; his voice grew more venomous.

"Sire," he said, "if I were to tell you the whole truth — if I were to tell you why, these last few days, Marigny, who has always desired my ruin, resolved at last to kill me, after long hesitation..."

"Speak! I command you," said the king, seeing that Valois had paused.

"If Enguerrand de Marigny had aimed only at the Comte de la Marche, or the Comte de Poitiers — forgive me, Sire! — I should have let Enguerrand de Marigny do his work!"

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
