Chapter One

The Grey Man had told the truth. Neither he, nor Shocking, nor the ragged Irishman had managed to find Ralph again.

So what had become of the little Irish boy?

The child, after jumping down into the garden, hadn't wasted a minute.

He'd run straight to the tree that had occupied his thoughts all day long, the one that climbed the length of the wall, and started scrambling around the trunk until he reached the branches.

There he paused for a moment to get his bearings.

He could see over the top of the wall.

On the other side lay a patch of waste ground fenced in by a wooden paling.

To the left and right stood the roofs of houses.

Climbing from branch to branch, the boy worked his way up to the wall and settled himself astride it.

Then he tried to judge the drop he'd have to make to reach the waste ground below.

The wall stood a good twenty feet above the ground, and on the far side there was neither tree nor anything else to break his fall.

Ralph felt a moment of despair. Would he have to go back the way he'd come and return to his prison?

Suddenly he heard a noise, and his fear doubled.

From where he sat he could see over the roof of Mrs Fanoche's house, and so he had a view of the front of the garden.

Despite the darkness, Ralph made out three men coming in through the gate. He saw two of them knock the third to the ground, and the sight, as you can imagine, did nothing to calm his fright.

It was the Grey Man and his accomplice, pressing a mask of pitch over Lord Palmure's face and getting rid of him.

Ralph was tempted to jump down onto the waste ground, but some instinct for danger held him back once more.

The top of the wall was flat. The boy stood up and began walking along it, and in this way reached one of the two roofs.

A tightrope walker couldn't have handled that perilous crossing any better.

Once he reached the end of the wall, he climbed onto the roof.

But his eyes never left Mrs Fanoche's house, into which the two men had gone.

Prowling about on the roof, he found an opening — one of those skylight windows cut into the slope of an attic.

He was sorely tempted to slip through it and into the house, but the fear of being spotted, caught by whoever lived there, and marched straight back to Mrs Fanoche made him hesitate again.

Suddenly a fresh commotion broke out in Mrs Fanoche's garden. At the same moment a light appeared at the window of the room Ralph had just left, and the boy heard shouting mixed with Mrs Fanoche's shrill voice.

They had just discovered he was gone.

This time the little Irish boy didn't hesitate. He let himself slide down through the skylight.

He found himself in a narrow, bare little room, empty of any furniture, its door standing open.

Ralph crossed the threshold and found a staircase. His small hands gripped the banister as he made his way down.

Where was he going? It hardly mattered, so long as he got away from Mrs Fanoche and the terrible Scotswoman.

The house seemed deserted.

No light showed anywhere, no sound could be heard.

The boy went down in such a rush that he missed a step and crashed against the banister.

That was more than enough noise to bring the household running.

Ralph froze, trembling, and for several minutes didn't dare move.

But no one came.

Hampstead, as we've already said, is full of country houses that stand empty through the winter.

This was one of them.

Reassured, the boy went on down through the darkness.

When he reached the bottom of the stairs, he sensed rather than saw a hallway, and at the end of it a door with a thin, pale line of light showing beneath it.

He went toward the door, but it was locked.

So the boy began turning this way and that; his eyes were growing used to the dark, and he could make out the shapes around him fairly clearly.

Past the door he found a window.

The terror of knowing that Mrs Fanoche and men he didn't recognize were hunting for him doubled his strength and his resolve.

After a great deal of struggling and fumbling, he managed to get the window open.

It looked out onto a small courtyard.

The courtyard was closed off by a railing, but the railing wasn't high, and Ralph, having dropped down into the yard, made up his mind to climb over it.

On the other side of the railing lay a street.

The boy began scaling the bars, which ended in spear-shaped points. He reached the top, though not without cutting himself and drawing blood on his small hands.

He said his mother's name to give himself courage, and jumped down into the street.

He landed on his knees and bruised himself badly.

But what did the pain matter now? He was free!

And he took off running straight ahead.

Deserted or not, a London suburb is lit with gas in the most lavish style.

From six in the evening until morning, it's a festival of hydrogen flame that holds court over some twenty-five square leagues.

Ralph had been brought to Hampstead while asleep, so there was no way for him to know he was now more than three miles from the house on Dudley Street where Shocking had taken him and his mother.

At the end of the street he'd been following, he came to a broad thoroughfare that he thought he recognized as one he'd walked along with her.

In London, all the streets look alike.

So he set off down this wide road, where passers-by and carriages were scarcer than the gas lamps.

It was Hampstead Road.

He walked a long while, without noticing that his hands and knees were bleeding.

After an hour, he thought he saw, off to his left, a street that looked like Dudley Street, and he turned into it.

It was narrower than Hampstead Road, but better lit, livelier, and lined with a long row of shops.

Since the boy didn't know the name of the street where he'd been taken with his mother, he had no way of asking directions.

The bleak solitude of Hampstead Road had gradually given way to the noisy bustle of London.

He was now on King Street, Camden Town.

He walked on and on, sometimes ready to drop from exhaustion, sometimes catching a flicker of hope and thinking he recognized St Giles's or Seven Dials, then ducking into the side streets, left and right, turning back on himself again and again.

This went on for four hours.

By the end of it, the poor child was no further along than he'd been the moment he'd left Mrs Fanoche's garden.

Despair took hold of him then, joining forces with his exhaustion.

He sat down on a doorstep half-lost in shadow and began to cry.

A crowd is indifferent everywhere, but nowhere more so than in London.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
