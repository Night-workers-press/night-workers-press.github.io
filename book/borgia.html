From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — PRIMEVÈRE

◆

Rome! The ancient capital of the civilised world lay slumbering, weighed down by a dreary sadness.

A kind of mysterious and profound terror chilled the superb city to its very marrow. Rome was silent, Rome was praying, Rome was suffocating.

Where the mighty voice of Cicero had once set the tribune of a tumultuous Forum ringing, sinister voices now chanted their psalms. Where the Gracchi had fought for liberty, the sombre and savage despotism of Rodrigue Borgia now pressed down with all its weight.

And Rodrigue Borgia was but one person in the menacing trinity that reigned over the City of Cities. Rodrigue had a son who, more than he, embodied Violence, and a daughter who, better than he, symbolised Cunning!

The son was called César. The daughter was called Lucrezia…

We are in the month of May, in the year 1501, at the dawn of the sixteenth century. On this day, the sun has risen in a blazing sky. The morning is radiant. An immense joy fills the air.

But Rome remains frozen, glacial — for the priests hold dominion over the earth. Yet before the great gate of the Château Saint-Ange, that fortress which bristles its odious turrets hard by the Vatican, a crowd of common folk have gathered out of curiosity.

Barefoot, in rags, their heads covered with filthy Phrygian caps, they gaze with an admiration full of reverence upon a group of young noblemen who, assembled in the square, parade about, converse loudly, laugh uproariously, and disdain to let so much as a glance fall upon the rabble that envies them from afar.

These cavaliers, clad in velvet and silk over fine cuirasses — sometimes glimpsed through a movement of their shimmering cloaks embroidered with gold — and mounted upon handsome horses, are gathered near the gate of the château… Suddenly, that gate swings wide open.

Silence falls. Heads are bared. A man with a swarthy face, dressed in black velvet, appears upon a magnificent black stallion and advances towards the young noblemen, who arrange themselves in a single line to salute him.

He lets his eyes wander over the city which, at his appearance, seems more silent still, as though seized by anguish.

Then his head sinks upon his breast. And he murmurs a few words that no one hears:

— This love is burning me… Primevère!… Primevère!… Why did I ever meet you?…

Then, with a wave of his hand, he signals the cavaliers, and the little troop, laughing and caracoling, sets off towards one of the gates of Rome — whilst among the common folk, bowed low, there passes like a shiver this word, sullenly repeated by hateful and fearful mouths:

— The son of the Pope!… Monseigneur César Borgia!…

On that same May morning, some seven leagues from Rome, upon the road to Florence, a young cavalier was making his solitary way at a walk upon his roan, heading unhurriedly, insouciantly, towards the City of Cities. He appeared to be about four-and-twenty.

His costume was worn, dilapidated. His doublet bore more than one patch, and his doeskin boots were mended in places.

But truly, he cut a fine figure beneath his long hair, which tumbled over his shoulders in natural curls, with his slender moustache twisted up into points, his lithe, boldly proportioned frame, his keen and piercing eyes, and above all that air of ingenuous gaiety which radiated from his countenance.

Although the young man had neither the bearing nor the physiognomy of a contemplative, he seemed to be giving himself over to a kind of reverie, and his gaze swept with indolence across the Roman Campagna, burnt by the sun — a vast, barren, naked plain.

— By heaven! he cried, this bears precious little resemblance to the merry environs of my dear Paris, with its shady woods, its taverns and its pleasure-gardens where one drinks such fine wine, and its comely lasses… Come, Capitan, a spell of trot, old friend… and let us see whether we cannot find some honest hostelry where two good Christians such as you and I might quench our thirst…

Capitan — that was the horse's name. The beast pricked up its ears and broke into a brisk trot.

Not ten minutes had elapsed when the cavalier, rising in his stirrups, perceived in the distance a small cloud of white dust advancing rapidly towards him. A few moments later, he made out two horses at full gallop.

Upon one of them fluttered a black robe: a priest! Upon the other, a white robe: a woman!

Almost at once, they were upon him.

The young Frenchman was preparing to salute the lady in white with all the grace that nature had bestowed upon him, when, to his great astonishment, she reined in her mount — launched at full speed — and came to a halt beside him.

— Monsieur, she cried in a trembling voice, whoever you may be, help me!…

— Madame, he replied with warmth, I am entirely at your service, and if you would do me the honour of telling me how I may be of use…

— Deliver me from that man!…

With her finger, she pointed at the monk, who had stopped and was shrugging his shoulders disdainfully.

— A man of the cloth! exclaimed the Frenchman.

— A demon… I beg you, let me continue on my way alone…

— Ho there, sire monk — you heard the lady?…

The man in black did not so much as cast a glance at the one who thus addressed him and, turning to the young woman:

— You will bitterly repent this… but it will be too late.

— Silence, monk! the young cavalier burst out. Silence, or by heaven, you shall make the acquaintance of this sword!

— You dare threaten a priest? said the monk in a venomous voice.

— And you dare threaten a woman! Stand back! Turn your horse about this instant, or you shall never again have occasion to threaten anyone.

At the same time, the Frenchman drew his sword and advanced upon the monk. The latter cast upon the young man a look of frightful rage, then, wheeling his horse, he fled at a gallop in the direction of Rome. For a moment one could see his black cloak fluttering in the wind like the wings of some bird of ill omen. Then he vanished.

The young cavalier turned then to the lady in white. He stood struck with admiration.

She was a young girl of about eighteen, of marvellous beauty. Admirable hair of an ashen blonde framed harmoniously a face illuminated by two large dark eyes. A kind of haughty grace emanated from her entire person.

At that moment, the flush of indignation empurpled her face and rendered her a thousand times more beautiful still. She too had followed with her eyes the dreadful monk as he took flight like an owl.

— I owe you, she said in a pure and singing voice, I owe you my deepest gratitude, Monsieur… ?

— The Chevalier de Ragastens, replied the cavalier, bowing deeply.

— A Frenchman!

— A Parisian, Madame…

— Well then… Monsieur le Chevalier de Ragastens, accept a thousand thanks for the immense service…

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
