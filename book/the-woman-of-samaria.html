The Woman of Samaria

La Samaritaine

A gospel in three tableaux (1897)

by Edmond Rostand · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Edmond Rostand · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

ForthcomingÀ paraître

The bookLe livre

Rostand's gospel play, staged on Good Friday 1897 with Sarah Bernhardt as Photine, the woman of Sichem who meets a stranger at Jacob's well and comes away a preacher. It is the least characteristic thing he wrote and the strangest: a verse drama about conversion, staged in a commercial theatre, by a playwright who was about to write Cyrano. The crowd scenes are extraordinary, and Photine — vain, funny, quick-tongued — is one of his best parts for an actress.

L'évangile en trois tableaux de Rostand, créé le Vendredi saint 1897 avec Sarah Bernhardt en Photine, la femme de Sichem qui rencontre un inconnu au puits de Jacob et en repart prédicatrice. C'est la plus atypique de ses œuvres et la plus étrange : un drame en vers sur la conversion, monté dans un théâtre commercial, par un auteur qui allait écrire Cyrano. Les scènes de foule sont prodigieuses, et Photine — vaniteuse, drôle, à la repartie vive — reste l'un de ses plus beaux rôles de femme.

THE AUTHOR'S ACKNOWLEDGEMENT

Rostand prefaced the first edition with this note.

I give my thanks to Madame Sarah Bernhardt, who was a flame and a prayer; to the Directress of her Theatre, to whom she sumptuously lent her taste; to Monsieur Brémont, whose tenderness was infinite because of its restraint; to that whole young and fevered Company, unique in the world from now on for expressing the soul of a crowd; to Monsieur Gabriel Pierné, who wrote a mysterious music; to the public of Paris, whose eagerness, whose emotion, whose intelligent quickening at the most furtive of intentions have once again reassured the poets; and to the Critics, who helped me nobly.

FIRST TABLEAU — JACOB'S WELL

At the crossing of the two great roads, one running toward Mesopotamia and the other toward the Great Sea: Jacob's Well, not far from the town of Sichem, in Samaria.

A vast oblong cistern. A low kerb one can sit on. A half-ruined stone vault still arches over the well. A rustic windlass of unbarked wood raises and lowers the rope the jars are hung from.

A great wild fig tree stretches its branches out horizontally. There is also one of those olive trees whose paleness is more silver in Samaria than anywhere else. And a few terebinths, further off, and the slender shapes of cypresses.

The back of the stage is a bank of dusty green, with the roads laid across it like a white fork; a winding path comes down from it to the well, and behind the bank the valley of Sichem is blue.

Mount Ebal and Mount Garizim close the horizon; Garizim lifts the ruins of a temple toward the sky; in the hollow between the two mountains, Sichem scatters the pale cubes of its houses.

So the set will appear presently, when day breaks. But when the curtain opens it is still night. A beautiful transparent darkness. All the stars. Standing on the stones of the well, in the deeper black of the vault, a very tall phantom with the beard of a hundred-year-old man leans, all white, on a staff. A second phantom, as tall and as white, is motionless on a step. A third, like the first two, with the same beard and the same shepherd's staff, comes forward mysteriously.

SCENE I

THE SHADES

FIRST SHADE, gliding toward the well

Driven on by the breeze of the night,

and wandering until the dawn,

I come for men I do not know,

being the phantom that I am.

On a sandal that makes no sound

I come, I glide, and I am gone...

But, O Jehovah whom I worship!

What is this other great shade

standing here beside the well?

SECOND SHADE, to the first

White beard in the brown night,

are you from someone who once lived?

Do you come up out of Sheol, that oasis

where one sleeps in meadows without lilies,

where one walks under a moonless sky?

Are you only a shade?

FIRST SHADE

I am one!

SECOND SHADE

I know your voice, my son.

FIRST SHADE

But another spectre, on the stone,

stands up, clothed in whitenesses!...

Motionless shade, can you hear me?

THIRD SHADE

I know your voice, my father.

SECOND SHADE

It is the child more pious than Job,

standing there on the step!

THIRD SHADE

It is the Father!

FIRST SHADE

The Patriarch!

THIRD SHADE

Abraham!

SECOND SHADE

Isaac!

FIRST SHADE

Jacob!...

JACOB

For what sublime alarms

do our inert feet find again

the sweet firmness of the ground?

ISAAC

It is for great things, surely,

that a black angel with green wings

has left the pale gates of Sheol

standing ajar tonight!

JACOB, to Abraham

What hopes have been born?

Tell us, you, what it was that lifted

our astonished shades tonight!

You must know what is destined:

your hundred and seventy years

have set you nearer to Jehovah!

ABRAHAM, to Isaac

Why are you kissing the dust

of the road so piously?

ISAAC

I feel myself compelled to it

by some obscure foreboding!

ABRAHAM, to Jacob

Why are you kissing the kerb

of the well you dug here?

JACOB

A supernatural force

makes me adore it so!...

— And you yourself: why breathe in

this silence so tenderly?

ABRAHAM

I am kissing, here in this air, beforehand,

the Voice that will set it trembling!

ISAAC

A voice, you say, Patriarch?

ABRAHAM

He comes, he comes, he is on the road,

and hold it for certain;

because tonight, in wild Sheol,

when I passed close to his couch,

laying one finger on his mouth,

Moses murmured it to me!

JACOB, prostrating himself with Isaac

Our hearts are singing psalms under their breath!

ABRAHAM

Long before the blue of the nights

falls back on the gold of the stubble,

there will be, on this very spot,

sighs sweeter than balms,

words greater than kingdoms!...

That is why our three phantoms

come wandering by this well.

JACOB, to Isaac

Is it possible, on this earth,

that out of all the wells of men

the Lord should have chosen, my Father,

for I know not what great mystery,

the one my own hands dug?

ISAAC

My son, let your shade be proud!

You are the workman he wanted

to dig the well of salvation

where the pale future will come to drink;

and it is so beautiful that the mere honour

of being your father or your grandfather

makes a man feel his shroud

suddenly drape itself into a cloak of glory!

JACOB

But here come all those who, since

my hand stopped drawing water for ever,

have come to draw at this well!...

A shade, and then a shade, and then

a long uncertain file

of shades, which has wound slowly down

to come, in holy love,

and kiss this grey kerb!

The whole Tomb has stirred:

I see Joseph and Joshua.

ABRAHAM

Shades who set these roads trembling,

fall on your knees, all of you, all of you,

before the Cistern of love!...

But already the day

has gilded the town and its tower...

Our shapes are about to dissolve!

JACOB

And soon there will be nothing left

of the three shades that stood here

but three diminished whitenesses,

three great beards fluttering,

then three little flakes of silver

that will melt like three breaths of mist!...

ISAAC

A crowd is coming from far off:

it is the Samaritan people,

who in the secrecy of morning

come to talk over their fears.

ABRAHAM

These are the men of Sichem,

who come to break out in complaints

and talk, under the terebinths,

of their never-extinguished hatreds

against Rome and Jerusalem!

JACOB

Let us vanish at their approach!...

And you, things, dreaming witnesses,

earth full of stirring memories,

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
