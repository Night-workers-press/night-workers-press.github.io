Four in the afternoon, late October in the Nivernais, a post-chaise trotting through pastures turning from summer green to the yellowish green that announces the first frosts. Ponson du Terrail is at his most deceptively pastoral. Inside the chaise sits the future of a criminal association that will run Paris from a card table: the Club of the Knaves of Hearts, and the apprenticeship of Rocambole.

Quatre heures de l'après-midi, fin octobre dans le Nivernais, une chaise de poste trottant à travers des pâtures qui passent du vert d'été au vert jaunissant des premières gelées. Ponson du Terrail n'est jamais aussi trompeusement champêtre. Dans la voiture voyage l'avenir d'une association criminelle qui gouvernera Paris depuis une table de jeu : le Club des Valets-de-Cœur, et l'apprentissage de Rocambole.

Chapter One

One evening, around four o'clock, a post-chaise rolled at a brisk trot along a road in the Nivernais.

It was autumn, in the year 184-, toward the end of October. At that time of year, nothing is more splendidly beautiful than the heart of France, especially that part of the Nivernais bordering the Yonne and belonging to the district of Clamecy.

The pastures were turning from the deep green of summer to the softer, almost yellow green that announces the first frosts. The woods were beginning to shed their leaves, and the tall, melancholy poplars lining the canal and the river Yonne bent under the breath of the season's first north winds.

Still, the air remained mild, the sky cloudless; in the morning a thin mist covered the meadows and marshes only to vanish with the sunrise, while toward evening it crept slowly down from the hilltops and stretched along the valleys, transparent and gilded by the last rays of the setting sun.

The post-chaise in question was just then crossing one of the most picturesque and untamed stretches of that beautiful country—a valley through which the river, God's handiwork, and the canal, man's, wound side by side in endless loops.

The valley was hemmed in by two ranges of wooded hills, those vast forests that reach all the way to the Morvan. Here and there, among the mossy rocks and green trees whose lowest roots trailed in the water, a rustic steeple rose into view, a slate-roofed church, a village where thatch outnumbered tile; now and then one of those fine feudal ruins that had escaped destruction by pure chance in 1793 and remained unknown even to the harshest chroniclers of that black era. The great road unrolled its bluish ribbon along the canal, skirting the lock-keepers' cottages and passing below villages, most of them terraced halfway up the slope amid a tangle of oaks and vines, girdled in green meadow.

In the post-chaise, its hood folded back, a man and a woman sat with a beautiful four-year-old child between them—fair-haired, blue-eyed, chattering away nonstop, peppering his father and mother with questions, marveling at the jingling of the bells on the collars of the four sturdy Percherons pulling the aristocratic carriage. The child's father was a man still young, perhaps thirty-seven or thirty-eight, tall, dark-haired, with black hair and blue eyes.

His face, somewhat stern, retained great beauty—a beauty that turned almost boyish whenever the child fixed on him that deep, charming gaze, full of innocent curiosity and respectful admiration, a look that belongs only to early childhood.

The mother might have been twenty-five. She was fair, a little pale, with a smile in which happiness showed itself through a trace of melancholy. She resembled the child the way a full-blown rose resembles the bud it once was.

The child sat between them; each held one of his hands, and each passed their free hand behind his back.

And those two hands were laced together in an affectionate clasp.

This pledge of their love seemed to have prolonged their honeymoon—usually so short—which, for them, seemed as though it would never end.

Now, this man and this woman, whose elegant travel dress, the two footmen seated behind the chaise, and their aristocratic manner of posting all betrayed a lofty social position, were none other than the Comte and Comtesse de Kergaz, returning from Italy and making their way to their fine estate at Magny-sur-Yonne, where they meant to spend the tail end of the season before returning to Paris around mid-December.

Monsieur le Comte Armand de Kergaz had left Paris eight days after his marriage to Mademoiselle de Balder.

The enchantments of that first love had unfolded for them on the shores of the Sicilian sea, beneath the shade trees of a villa the Count had rented in Palermo.

They had lived there six months—an entire winter, the season of black cold and frost in France, but of warm sun and spring breezes there.

Then they had returned to Paris to live in that vast, rather cold mansion on the Rue Culture-Sainte-Catherine.

But there, the change of climate—and perhaps a few bitter memories—had taken a troubling toll on Madame de Kergaz's health.

The frail young woman had fallen ill, seriously enough to worry her doctors, who ordered her back to Sicily.

So Armand de Kergaz had set off again, bringing the young mother—Jeanne was seven or eight months pregnant by then—back to that Sicilian land where the sun is so gentle to those who suffer.

The influence of that blessed climate had not been slow to show itself.

Jeanne recovered quickly, more beautiful, younger than ever. Her child was born in Palermo; the green branches of a sycamore had shaded his cradle, and the murmur of the azure wave shimmering in the sun had been the first song he ever heard.

And since the warm, scented air of that beautiful country was so good for the beloved infant, though the Countess had fully recovered by the end of the first year, they let themselves linger in Palermo for three more years.

Then, one day, homesickness—that strange and yet so common ailment—came knocking at their door.

Amid the pines of Italy, the oleanders and the sycamores, on the terrace of their villa overlooking the sea, blue as a sapphire without end, listening to that eternal, gentle lament of the waves endlessly rolling the golden sand of the shore, the two young spouses, whom happiness had made forgetful for so long, remembered our France. They did not think first of Paris, that great modern Babylon where they had loved and suffered, but of that beautiful, poetic Nivernais countryside where Monsieur de Kergaz had bought a manor on his first return, and where he had rested a fortnight before setting off to seek his wife's health in the warm breath of the South.

They thought of that pretty little château, lost beneath a stand of great oaks, surrounded by an immense park, with a green meadow spreading before it; of those thick woods full of vague murmurs, beneath whose tall stands of trees the bright fanfare of Morvan huntsmen rang out each autumn; and since happiness had followed them everywhere they went together, since it smiled on them wherever they were in the guise of their rosy little cherub, they left.

They took ship for Naples, crossed the length of Italy, visited Rome, Venice, and Florence in quick succession, followed the Corniche road, and re-entered France through the Var, that Italy in miniature.

A fortnight later, they were rolling along that great Nivernais road where we have just found them again, and by four in the afternoon they were no more than five or six leagues from the château of Magny.

"Jeanne, my beloved," Armand murmured, gazing at his young wife with love while his fingers played with little Gontran's blond curls, "won't you miss our villa in Palermo, our dear promised land, once we're in that quiet, silent château we're heading toward?"

"Oh, no," Jeanne answered. "Wherever you are, wherever my hand is in yours—isn't that the promised land?"

"Angel," the Count said softly, "you've made me so happy that God may yet begrudge me my share of paradise. In France or in Italy, living with you, beside you—that's better than the promised land. That's heaven."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
