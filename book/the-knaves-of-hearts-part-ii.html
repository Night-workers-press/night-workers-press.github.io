Chapter Fifty-two

The young Comte Artoff had left Baccarat's the evening before in a kind of enthusiastic daze.

He'd walked in like a Don Juan armed with his millions, as if they were a talisman. He walked out subdued, struck by the majestic sadness of this extraordinary woman, who seemed to him so monstrously slandered by rumor.

Baccarat had suddenly appeared to him as a mysterious creature the crowd would never fathom. Was she some great sinner doing penance? Some dark avenger, arming herself in shadow to hunt down and punish criminals and murderers without mercy?

The count couldn't say which. But he found himself forced toward one or the other of those two possibilities, and understood, however vaguely, that Baccarat had some great mission to fulfill.

He went home with a thousand confused thoughts churning in his head.

Did he already love this woman, whose house he'd entered as a conqueror? Or did he feel for her only a sudden, respectful affection, capable of the deepest devotion?

He could no more settle those last questions than the first two.

He slept badly. Baccarat wove through every dream. In one he was wandering with her across a desert, kneeling at her feet; in another she swept him into a whirlwind, taking the strangest shapes, speaking to him in every possible voice.

When day came, the young Russian was no closer to naming the feeling that drew him to Baccarat than he had been the night before, but he felt a driving need to see her again.

She'd told him as they parted: "I'll expect you for lunch tomorrow, at ten."

The count glanced at the clock on the mantel as he lifted his head from the pillow and saw, to his despair, that it was barely eight. Still, he got up, tried on and discarded three or four morning outfits, and, since time refused to move any faster, called for one of his saddle horses, determined to ride for an hour and make a circuit of the Bois.

The count had forgotten that Monsieur de Manerve was expecting him for lunch as well.

He lived in a handsome little townhouse on the Rue de la Pépinière, almost directly across from number 40, where Chérubin kept an apartment and Madame Malassis occupied a garden pavilion.

The townhouse, which the count had built, had a large garden that wrapped around the sides of the main house. At the far end of that garden, the architect had put up a pavilion.

That pavilion was topped by a very tall belvedere. From up there, the eye traveled easily over the neighboring rooftops and gardens. From that height you could look straight over the house into the garden of number 40 — that is, into the grounds around Madame Malassis's pavilion.

These topographical details matter; they'll be essential to understanding what follows.

The count rode out on horseback through the Faubourg du Roule, then the Champs-Élysées, made a full gallop around the Bois, came back by the outer boulevard, and reined in his lathered horse at the gate of Baccarat's mansion just as ten o'clock struck on the nearby clocks.

Baccarat's groom ran out to open the gate and take the bridle, then showed him into the drawing room we already know — the same one where, two days before, Madame Charmet had waited for Turquoise.

The count threw himself onto a sofa and waited, on edge.

Baccarat wasn't long in appearing.

The count let out a cry of astonishment and admiration at the sight of her, she looked so radiant, so beautiful. She'd dressed simply for the morning: a high-necked blue gown, arms bare to the elbow but for a single heavy silver bracelet engraved with an English motto, her lovely hair coiled the way she used to wear it. She was smiling and composed, nothing like the solemn, grief-stricken woman the count had seen the evening before in the little study.

She held out her hand to the young man.

"Good morning, my friend," she said. "You're as punctual as a man in love."

"That's because I am," he said, with a charming lack of guile.

"Well," she said, kissing his forehead, "your old friend will cure you of that nonsense."

Then she added, with a touch of adorable melancholy:

"You fool. Nobody falls in love with women a hundred years old."

"Oh, you're young and beautiful," he said fervently.

"My heart is old where love is concerned."

And as if she wanted to soften the harshness of those words at once:

"But it's young where friendship is concerned," she said, "and I want to be your friend, because you're noble and good."

She sat him down beside her and kept hold of one of his hands.

"Now then," she said, "let's talk a little — like real lovers, since that's what the world thinks we are. What shall we do with our day?"

"Whatever you like," the count answered, as obedient as a child.

"First, you'll let me give you lunch?"

"Oh, good God," the young Russian cried, "and Manerve's expecting me!"

"For lunch?"

"Yes."

"Well then, write to him. Here, sit down at this desk, take a pen, and write."

The count obeyed and picked up the pen.

Baccarat dictated the very note we already know, the one Monsieur de Manerve would read an hour later to his friends at the Café de Paris, adding the postscript we also remember. When it was done, she folded the note herself, sealed it in an envelope, and had the count press it shut with a signet ring from among his trinkets.

Then she rang for the servant and told him:

"Take this letter to the Baron de Manerve, rue Caumartin, number twelve."

Once the groom had gone, she came back and sat beside Comte Artoff.

"My friend," she said, "you need to prove your affection for me in earnest."

"What must I do?"

"Compromise me as thoroughly as you can."

And as he looked at her, puzzled:

"The weather's fine," she said. "We'll go out after lunch, as you told Manerve — by carriage, around noon, to the Bois. But..."

"But?" the count asked.

"I'd like our first outing together to draw a bit of attention."

"Whatever you wish."

"I've heard you had a magnificent calèche at the last Longchamp meet."

"I still have it."

"And four black horses, harnessed Russian style?"

"They're still in my stables."

"Well then," Baccarat said, "write a note to your head groom. I'd like to try out your calèche."

"Consider it done," the count replied. "The calèche will be here before noon."

Baccarat and Comte Artoff had lunch in a small dining room filled with flowers and rare shrubs.

Then the young woman left the young man alone with a cup of coffee and a box of cigars while she went to dress.

At exactly noon, the calèche arrived, harnessed Russian style. Almost immediately after, Baccarat, dressed and ready, rejoined the count and took his arm.

"Listen," she said, taking his hand to climb into the carriage, "I have a whim."

"Name it, madame."

"On our way back from the Bois, you'll take me to your house, won't you?"

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
