1812. The Grand Army is crawling back from Moscow, half its battalions already under the ice of the Berezina, and two French officers make a bargain in the snow that one of them will spend the rest of his life honouring and the other will spend it betraying. Out of that bargain comes a stolen inheritance, a ruined family, and eventually a boy from the Paris gutter called Rocambole. This is where the whole nineteen-volume saga starts.

1812. La Grande Armée reflue de Moscou, la moitié de ses bataillons déjà sous la glace de la Bérézina, et deux officiers français passent dans la neige un pacte que l'un honorera toute sa vie et que l'autre trahira toute la sienne. De ce pacte sortiront un héritage volé, une famille ruinée, et pour finir un gamin du ruisseau parisien nommé Rocambole. C'est ici que commence la saga entière, dix-neuf volumes durant.

PROLOGUE

Chapter One

It was 1812.

The Grand Army was in retreat, leaving Moscow and the burning Kremlin behind it, along with half its battalions swallowed by the icy waters of the Berezina. Snow was falling. In every direction, all the way to the horizon, the earth was white and the sky was gray.

Across the vast, barren plains crawled the remnants of those proud legions — once led by the new Caesar in his conquest of the world, legions that a coalition of all Europe had failed to defeat — now beaten by the one enemy that could ever force them to retreat: the cold of the north.

Here, a group of horsemen sat frozen stiff in their saddles, fighting with the energy of despair against the grip of a deadly sleep. There, a handful of infantrymen crowded around a dead horse, hacking at it in haste while a flock of ravenous crows fought them for the scraps. Farther on, a man lay down with the stubbornness of madness, falling asleep with the certainty that he would never wake again.

Every so often a distant blast sounded — Russian cannon fire — and the stragglers dragged themselves back to their feet, driven by the raw instinct of self-preservation.

Three men, three horsemen, had gathered at the edge of a small wood around a pile of brushwood they'd struggled to clear of its crust of hardened snow, and had set alight. Horses and riders crowded around the fire — the men squatting with legs crossed, the noble animals standing with heads low and eyes fixed on nothing.

The first of the three men wore the tattered remains of a uniform still bearing a colonel's epaulettes. He looked about thirty-five, tall, with a strong and noble face, and blue eyes that radiated both courage and kindness. His right arm was in a sling, and his head was wrapped in bloodstained bandages. A Russian bullet had shattered his elbow, and a saber blow had split his forehead from temple to temple.

The second man must have been a captain, judging by what remained of his ragged uniform — though by now there were no colonels, no captains, no soldiers left at all. The Grand Army was nothing but a wretched mass of men in rags, fleeing the bitter northern wind far more than the hordes from the Don and the Caucasus who pursued them like a starving pack of wolves and birds of prey.

This second man was young too, with a low brow, an olive complexion, and a shifting, uncertain gaze. His black hair betrayed his southern origins, and his drawling accent and lively gestures marked him as one of those many Italians who served in the French army under the First Empire. Luckier than his commanding officer, the captain was unwounded, and so far he had borne more easily the deadly assaults of the terrible cold that was driving Caesar's bold legions back toward the south.

The third member of this small band was a common soldier, a hussar of the Guard, whose young, rough, manly face would sometimes take on a fierce look when the Russian guns thundered in the distance, only to turn anxious and tender the moment his eyes fell on his exhausted, blood-soaked commander.

It was evening; night was falling, and the mists of dusk were beginning to blur the white earth into the gray sky.

"Are we spending the night here, Felipone?" the colonel asked the Italian captain. "I feel very weak, very tired," he added, "and my arm is giving me terrible pain."

"Colonel," Bastien the hussar broke in quickly, before the Italian could answer, "we have to keep moving. The cold will kill you."

The colonel looked from the soldier to the captain.

"You think so?" he said.

"Yes, yes!" the hussar repeated with the conviction of a man who was certain.

The Italian captain, for his part, seemed to be thinking it over.

"Well, Felipone?" the colonel pressed.

"Bastien's right," the captain replied. "We need to get back on our horses and ride as long as we can. If we stay here, we'll fall asleep eventually, the fire will go out while we sleep, and none of us will ever wake up again. Besides — listen. The Russians are getting closer. I can hear the guns."

"Oh, misery," the colonel murmured in a hollow voice. "Who would ever have told me we'd come to this — fleeing from a handful of Cossacks! Oh, the cold... the cold! What a fierce, terrible enemy! My God, if only I weren't so cold..."

The colonel had crouched down before the fire, trying to bring some feeling back into his numb limbs.

"Thunder and blood!" Bastien the hussar grumbled. "I never would have believed that my colonel — a true lion — could be brought this low by that miserable wind whistling over the frozen snow."

As he spoke under his breath, the soldier's eyes rested on the colonel with a look full of love and respect.

The officer's face had gone livid, betraying his terrible suffering. His whole body shook and trembled, and it seemed as though all the life left in him had gathered in his eyes, which still held their look of quiet, gentle pride.

"Well then," he went on, "let's go, since you want to — but let me warm myself a little longer first. What horrible cold! Ah, I'm suffering as I've never suffered before... and I'm dying for sleep. My God, if only I could sleep for an hour — just one hour!"

The Italian captain and the hussar exchanged a glance.

"If he falls asleep," Felipone murmured, "we won't be able to wake him and get him back in the saddle."

"Well then," the brave Bastien replied, leaning close to the captain's ear, "I'll carry him asleep if I have to. I'm strong, and to save my colonel — ah, I'd turn into Hercules himself."

The captain tilted his head back, seeming to listen for distant sounds.

"The Russians are more than three leagues off," he said finally. "Night's coming on, and they'll surely make camp before they reach us. Since the colonel wants to sleep, let him sleep. We'll keep watch."

The colonel heard these last words and held out his hand to the Italian.

"Thank you, Felipone," he said. "Thank you, my friend. You're a good man, a brave one — you don't let that wretched north wind get the better of you. Oh, the cold!"

The colonel spoke these last words with something like terror in his voice.

"But I'm not wounded," the Italian answered, "so it's only natural I suffer less."

"My friend," the colonel continued, while the hussar fed the fire with every scrap of brushwood and dead branch he could find, "I'm thirty-five years old. A soldier at sixteen, a colonel at thirty — that tells you I've been brave, and patient. And yet all my energy, all my courage, even the indifference with which I accepted the endless hardships of our hard, noble trade — all of it fails against this deadly enemy they call the cold. I'm cold! Do you understand?

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
