From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — Annaïs de Lespars

Alone, motionless amid the dazzling décor of that sumptuous salon, stiff as a board beneath the crimson robe bedecked with fifteen hundred thousand livres' worth of lace and diamonds, you would take him for some sombre and magnificent figure by Philippe de Champaigne whom a sudden pang of grief had quickened into life and caused to descend from its gilded frame…

This man bears a formidable name.

His name is Richelieu!

The Palais Cardinal is scarce completed. On this morning in March of 1626, Richelieu inaugurates it with a solemn mass that he himself shall celebrate in his chapel, to which he has summoned the court, his friends, his enemies — all — that he might display his magnificence and dazzle them with his opulence. And here is what, in that instant, he groans within the depths of his thought:

"She does not come!… Through a lackey, as one would address a lackey, she has signified to me that she cares nothing for this ceremony — this consecration of my power!… She crushes me beneath her disdain. O my queen!… What is to be done? What enterprise remains? A future of splendour, the joys of limitless wealth and power — Richelieu would give them all, and his blood and his very life, for a single glance from Anne of Austria!… It is finished… she will not come!"

In that very second, a voice, close beside him, murmurs:

"Monseigneur, Her Majesty the Queen has just arrived at the chapel!…"

The Cardinal starts… Before him bows a monk — bony skull, angular features, a smile at once cynical and artless, an eye at once naïve and impudent, some indefinable air of the swordsman beneath the cowl — a great devil of a Capuchin, long and lean, who reeks of the spy from a league away. Richelieu, deathly pale, seizes the monk's arm and trembles:

"Corignan! Corignan! What sayest thou?"

"I say that, if you desire it, she is yours! Monseigneur, I come from the Louvre, and I have seen Madame de Givray, your… ambassadress accredited to the Queen. Hearken, Eminence: Catherine the Great had the Tuileries; the King has his Louvre; Marie de' Medici has the Luxembourg. Alone, Anne of Austria has nothing!… And you, Monseigneur, you possess this palace — majestic as the Tuileries, vast as the Louvre, elegant as the Luxembourg…"

"Oh!" stammers the Cardinal in a fever, "what a dream!… Oh! if it were possible that she might deign…"

"To accept?… Ah! Monseigneur, you are a minister of genius, but you do not know women as poor Brother Corignan knows them!… I therefore placed my little word in Madame de Givray's ear. I said… upon my faith! I had the audacity to say that this palace which astonishes the world was not built for the Cardinal, but for an illustrious princess, and…"

"Go on! Go on!" pants Richelieu.

"And the illustrious princess awaits confirmation of my words! Monseigneur, when do you wish me to carry to the Louvre the letter that you are about to write to Queen Anne of Austria?"

The Cardinal stifles a cry of insensate hope. He closes his eyes. Both hands press against his breast.

"This evening… towards midnight… at my hôtel on the Place Royale… I shall await thee!"

At that moment, a man clad in black draws away from the tapestry behind which he has been listening, crosses the darkened cabinet where he had lain in wait, passes through a gallery, and loses himself in the corridors of the Palais Cardinal…

Brother Corignan has humbly bowed, then made his way towards the door of the salon, which he opens — and there he collides with someone entering: stout, short of leg, a sort of pot-bellied runt, clean-shaven, another physiognomy of the spy.

"Rascasse!" growls the Capuchin. "For ever underfoot, then!"

"Corignan!" snarls the runt. "For ever poaching on my preserves, I see!"

And, devoured by jealousy, the two spies threaten one another in unison:

"We shall meet again!…"

Richelieu stands panting. Rascasse, covered from head to foot in dust — a traveller who has not taken time to remove his boots — trots forward, multiplies his bows to attract the attention of his master…

The Cardinal perceives him at last. At once, love, passion, furious desire — all vanish from his mind. And suddenly:

"Madame de Lespars?"

The spy lets fall this single word:

"Dead!…"

"She is dead… very well! Tell me now who helped her to die?…"

Rascasse starts. He stands, perhaps, at the decisive hour when a simple lie may secure a man's life. He struggles. He hesitates. Then suddenly, within himself:

"Bah! Monsieur de Saint-Priac will never dare denounce himself!"

And, quite pale with the heavy burden he casts upon his conscience, he stammers:

"It was I, Monseigneur… I!"

"Rascasse, thou art a good servant. Call upon my treasurer: he awaits thee. This evening, at my hôtel, thou shalt give me the particulars of thy journey to Angers, and how the thing was done. Go now."

"One moment, Monseigneur. I ought to have been here a fortnight since, Madame de Lespars having succumbed on the twenty-third of February. Now, if I have tarried, it is because I searched for someone who vanished the day after the funeral — someone whom I studied for a month… and who slipped through my fingers at the very moment I was about to… but enough: she shall be found again!"

"Of whom — of what — dost thou speak?"

"It concerns the daughter of that noble lady… it concerns Annaïs de Lespars!"

"Annaïs!… That child!…"

"That child was the mother's inspiration!" the spy growls darkly. "Monseigneur, we were mistaken! We should have let the mother live and killed the daughter! There lay the danger, Eminence! She escaped me. Otherwise, she would already have rejoined her mother. Where is she now? She comes to you, perhaps! And if that be so, beware…"

Richelieu has knitted his brows. He ponders, calculates, schemes. And, all at once, he raises his head. He has it!…

"Rascasse, didst thou see, at Angers, this Baron de Saint-Priac?"

"Yes, Monseigneur," replies the spy, suppressing a shudder. "At the same time as I, he set out for Paris, furnished with the letter of audience that will gain him admittance without delay to Your Eminence. A precious acquisition, Monseigneur! Three-and-twenty years of age, devoid of scruples, prepared for any enterprise, a keen wit, a strong arm, and at the end of that arm a sword perhaps more formidable than that of the famous Trencavel himself!"

"Trencavel?" enquires the Cardinal.

"The fencing-master whose academy is the most frequented in Paris. I know him. There is another you ought to acquire, Monseigneur!"

"We shall see. The reports say that this Saint-Priac is smitten with Mademoiselle de Lespars. Is it true?"

"He would sell his soul to the Devil if the Devil offered him Annaïs…"

"Well then!" says Richelieu coldly, his gaze illumined by a baleful clarity, "trouble thyself no more about that child, Rascasse. Thou hast rid me of the mother… Saint-Priac shall rid me of the daughter!…"

"And how, Monseigneur?…"

"By marrying her!" replies Richelieu with a keen-edged smile.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
