From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — The Festival of Love

Roland!… Léonore!…

Venice, on that enchanted evening of the fifth of June in the year 1509, acclaimed those two beloved names.

Those two names — feverish Venice exalted them as symbols of liberty. Tender-hearted Venice blessed them as talismans of love.

On the Piazza San Marco, between the masts bearing the illustrious pennant of the Republic, young girls in brilliant costumes whirled slowly round — barcarols, sailors — all the people, all who throb with life, all who suffer, all who love.

And there was a supreme defiance in that enormous joy which came beating against the ducal palace with its cheering — the ducal palace, silent, menacing, and dark…

Up above, upon a sort of terrace at the summit of the ancient palace, two shadows leaned out over the festival — two men who fixed upon all that joy the dreadful gaze of their hatred.

Venice let rise the ardent breath of her entwined couples who, amidst naïve blessings and wishes of eternal felicity, repeated the names of Léonore and Roland.

For to-morrow the betrothal of the two lovers would be celebrated. Roland! — the son of Doge Candiano, the hope of the oppressed! Roland — he who, it was said, had more than once made the assembly of despots tremble, that terrible Council of Ten, and had wrested from it more than one victim!

Léonore! — the pride of Venice for her beauty — the heiress of the famous house of the Dandoli, still all-powerful despite its ruin… Léonore, who loved her Roland so dearly that one day, when a celebrated painter begged her on his knees to let him paint her, she had answered that her lover alone should possess her in body and in image!

And Venice, terrorised by the Council of Ten, celebrated as the beginning of her deliverance the betrothal of the Doge's son and the daughter of the Dandoli.

For this marriage would be the union of the two families capable of resisting the unbridled despotism of the Ten! This marriage would be — no one doubted it — the coming elevation to the dogal dignity of Roland, the people's hope, and of Léonore, the Madonna of the poor!

At intervals, however, the clamour of cheering collapsed all at once upon the Piazza San Marco, and a silence heavy with dread weighed upon the crowd.

It was because some spy had been seen approaching the trunk of denunciations, casting a paper hastily into it, then vanishing into the shadows.

What name had been delivered to the vengeance of the Ten? Who would be arrested that night?

Then, suddenly more violent, more bitter, the acclamations came crashing against the grim ducal palace, in whose depths Doge Candiano and the Dogaressa Silvia trembled for their son, terrified by the very popularity that marked him for the executioner!

Up above, on the terrace, two men listened intently.

One of them, tall, his face stamped with a savage pride, thrust his clenched fist towards the crowd:

"Howl, race of slaves! To-morrow you shall weep tears of blood! Listen, Bembo! They acclaim their Roland!"

"I hear, Seigneur Altieri! And I confess that those two names — Roland and Léonore — make rather a fine pairing, coupled together!"

"Damnation! Rather than see this marriage accomplished, Bembo, I shall stab them with my own hands!"

"Oh! You hate your dear friend Roland so very much, then?"

"I hate him — him — because I love her! Oh! This love, Bembo! This love that chokes me! O Léonore, Léonore! Why did I ever see you! Why did I ever love you!"

And this man — the most powerful of Venice's patricians, the most formidable of the Ten — this Altieri who, when he crossed Venice, silent and fatal, walked in an atmosphere of dread — this man seized his head in both hands and wept.

Bembo, his face furrowed by a smile of contempt and fear, Bembo watched him, appallingly pensive.

Altieri, his countenance contorted, his bearing stiffened in an effort of ferocious will, strode towards the stairway of the terrace.

"Where are you going, Seigneur Captain?" cried Bembo.

Without answering, Altieri showed him the dagger upon which his hand was clenched.

"Are you jesting, Monseigneur!" murmured Bembo, in that viscous voice of his — a voice which ensured that after finding him hideous to look upon, one found him abject to listen to. "Are you jesting! When one bears the name of Altieri, when one commands twenty thousand men-at-arms, when one may depose the Doge and crown oneself with the ducal tiara, when one may, by lifting a finger, cause a head to fall, when one holds in one's hand that fulminating and sombre weapon called the Council of Ten — permit me to say it, Seigneur — one is but a child if, to rid oneself of a rival, one stoops to strike him! You are a god in Venice, and you would make yourself a bravo! Come, come! It is not by a dagger-thrust that Roland Candiano, the betrothed of Léonore, should die!"

"What do you mean?" the Captain snarled.

Bembo drew him to the far end of the terrace:

"Look!"

In his turn, Altieri leaned over.

This corner of Venice was dark, sinister. In the depths appeared a narrow canal, without gondolas, without songs, without lights. On one side rose the ducal palace — massive, ponderous, formidable; on the other side of the canal stood a terrible façade: the prisons of Venice.

And between those two enormous things, a monstrous hyphen — a kind of sarcophagus flung across the abyss, linking the palace of tyranny to the palace of suffering… It was upon that coffin suspended above the black waters that the gaze of Altieri fell.

"The Bridge of Sighs!"

"The bridge of death!" answered Bembo in a glacial voice. "Whosoever passes there bids farewell to hope, to life, to love!"

Altieri wiped his brow, wet with sweat. And as though his conscience had been struggling in one last convulsion:

"A pretext!" he stammered — "oh! a pretext to have him arrested!…"

"You want a pretext!" said Bembo, drawing himself up with funereal joy. "Very well — follow me, Seigneur Altieri!"

Bembo had moved to another point on the terrace:

"Look!…"

This time he indicated a palace whose façade of Carrara marble and colonnades of jasper were mirrored in the Grand Canal.

"The palace of the courtesan Imperia!" murmured Altieri.

"You seek a pretext," Bembo growled. "It is there that you shall find it!"

"She hates him, then!" Altieri panted.

"She loves him!… Do you hear me, Seigneur? The courtesan Imperia suffers this evening like a soul in torment — like you! And her love, violent as yours, implacable as yours, keeps watch in the shadows! And that love opens for her, as for you, the gate of vengeance… Come, Seigneur — come to the courtesan Imperia!…"

II — The Lovers of Venice

The last sounds of the popular festival had died away. Venice slept. All was shuttered… Only the maw of the Trunk of Denunciations¹ remained open — a menace that never sleeps…

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
