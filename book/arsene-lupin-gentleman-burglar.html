The bookLe livre

Nine stories, and the birth of a legend. A transatlantic liner learns by wireless that Arsène Lupin is on board under a false name; a baron's château is robbed by appointment; Lupin walks out of the Santé; and in the last story an English detective arrives a little too late. Leblanc wrote the first of them in 1905 to fill a few pages of a new magazine, and by the time they were collected in 1907 the gentleman-burglar was the most famous criminal in France. Complete, with Leblanc's dedication to his publisher and the 1907 preface by Jules Claretie.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

The Arrest of Arsène Lupin

What a strange voyage it was! And yet it had begun so well! For my part, I never made one that began under happier auspices. La Provence is a fast, comfortable liner, commanded by the most affable of men. The most select society was gathered aboard. Acquaintances formed, entertainments were organised. We had that exquisite feeling of being cut off from the world, thrown back on ourselves as if on some unknown island, and obliged, in consequence, to draw closer to one another.

And we did draw closer…

Have you ever considered how singular and unpredictable a thing it is, this gathering of people who only the day before were strangers, and who for a few days, between the infinite sky and the immense sea, will live the most intimate of lives together, will together defy the rages of the ocean, the terrifying assault of the waves, the malice of storms and the treacherous calm of sleeping water?

It is, at bottom, life itself, lived in a kind of tragic abridgement, with its storms and its grandeurs, its monotony and its variety—and that, perhaps, is why one savours with such feverish haste, and with a pleasure all the keener for it, this short voyage whose end is in sight at the very moment it begins.

But for some years now something has been happening that adds strangely to the emotions of the crossing. The little floating island still belongs to the world one thought one had escaped. A thread remains, which is loosened only little by little in mid-ocean, and little by little, in mid-ocean, is tied again. The wireless telegraph! A call from another universe, from which news arrives in the most mysterious manner imaginable! The imagination can no longer fall back on picturing iron wires along whose hollows the invisible message slides. The mystery is more unfathomable still, and more poetic too; one has to summon the wings of the wind to explain this new miracle.

And so, in those first hours, we felt ourselves followed, escorted, even preceded by that distant voice which now and then whispered to one of us a few words from back there. Two friends spoke to me. Ten others, twenty others, sent all of us, across space, their farewells, sorrowful or smiling.

Now, on the second day, five hundred miles from the French coast, on a stormy afternoon, the wireless brought us a telegram which ran as follows:

“Arsène Lupin aboard your ship, first class, fair hair, wound right forearm, travelling alone, under the name of R…”

At that precise moment a violent clap of thunder broke in the dark sky. The electric waves were cut off. The rest of the telegram never reached us. Of the name under which Arsène Lupin was hiding, we knew only the initial.

Had it been any other piece of news, I have no doubt the secret would have been scrupulously kept by the wireless operators, by the purser and by the captain. But there are some events that seem to force even the strictest discretion. That very day, without anyone being able to say how the thing had got out, we all knew that the famous Arsène Lupin was hiding among us.

Arsène Lupin, among us! The elusive burglar whose exploits every newspaper had been recounting for months! The enigmatic figure with whom old Ganimard, our best detective, was engaged in that duel to the death whose twists and turns had unfolded so picturesquely! Arsène Lupin, the whimsical gentleman who operates only in châteaux and drawing rooms, and who one night, having broken into Baron Schormann’s house, had left empty-handed, leaving behind his card with this inscription: “Arsène Lupin, gentleman-burglar, will return when the furniture is genuine.” Arsène Lupin, the man of a thousand disguises: by turns chauffeur, tenor, bookmaker, young man of good family, adolescent, old man, commercial traveller from Marseilles, Russian doctor, Spanish bullfighter!

Just consider what that meant: Arsène Lupin coming and going within the relatively narrow confines of a liner—what am I saying?—within that little corner of first class where we ran into one another at every moment, in that dining room, that lounge, that smoking room! Arsène Lupin might be this gentleman… or that one… my neighbour at table… the man who shared my cabin…

“And this is going to go on for another five days!” cried Miss Nelly Underdown the next day. “It’s intolerable! I do hope they arrest him.”

And turning to me:

“Look here, monsieur d’Andrézy—you’re already on the best of terms with the captain. Don’t you know anything?”

How I would have liked to know something, to please Miss Nelly! She was one of those magnificent creatures who, wherever they are, at once take the most conspicuous place. Their beauty dazzles as much as their fortune. They have a court, devotees, enthusiasts.

Brought up in Paris by a French mother, she was on her way to join her father, the immensely rich Underdown of Chicago. A friend of hers, Lady Jerland, was travelling with her.

From the first hour I had put myself forward as a candidate for a flirtation. But in the quick intimacy of shipboard life her charm had unsettled me at once, and I felt a little too moved for mere flirtation whenever her great dark eyes met mine. Still, she received my attentions with a certain favour. She deigned to laugh at my witticisms and to take an interest in my anecdotes. A vague fondness seemed to answer the eagerness I showed her.

Only one rival might have worried me: a rather handsome young man, elegant and reserved, whose taciturn manner she sometimes seemed to prefer to my more outgoing Parisian ways.

He happened to be among the group of admirers surrounding Miss Nelly when she put her question to me. We were on deck, comfortably settled in rocking chairs. The previous day’s storm had cleared the sky. It was a delicious hour.

“I know nothing definite, mademoiselle,” I replied, “but is there any reason we shouldn’t conduct our own investigation, every bit as well as old Ganimard, Arsène Lupin’s personal enemy, would do it?”

“Oh! Oh! That’s a bold claim!”

“How so? Is the problem so complicated?”

“Very complicated.”

“That’s because you’re forgetting the clues we have for solving it.”

“What clues?”

“One: Lupin is calling himself Monsieur R…”

“Rather a vague description.”

“Two: he’s travelling alone.”

“If that detail is enough for you!”

“Three: he’s fair-haired.”

“And so?”

“So all we have to do is consult the passenger list and proceed by elimination.”

I had the list in my pocket. I took it out and ran through it.

“I note first that there are only thirteen people whose initial brings them to our attention.”

“Only thirteen?”

“In first class, yes. Of these thirteen Messieurs R…, as you can check for yourselves, nine are accompanied by wives, children or servants. That leaves four men travelling alone: the Marquis de Raverdan…”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.
