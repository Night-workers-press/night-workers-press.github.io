CHAPTER I

Saint-Germain at Work

At roughly the same hour when these events were unfolding in the house of the quincunxes — that is, around four o'clock in the morning — in the mysterious house on the ruelle aux Réservoirs, the Comte du Barry was contemplating the murder of the Chevalier d'Assas.

Thus, while the woman, in that strange partnership, was deploying all her wiles and working miracles to conquer the king's senses and perhaps his heart, the man was preparing to kill!...

Earlier that evening, when night had just fallen, a man carefully wrapped in his cloak had entered the ruelle aux Réservoirs.

He walked directly, without hesitation, toward M. Jacques's house.

This man was the Comte de Saint-Germain, who, after the magnetism séance in Paris, had climbed into his carriage.

During the entire journey from Paris to Versailles, he slept — not peacefully, but deeply.

The comte had given himself the order to sleep — and he slept!

This might be the moment to lay out the theory of magnetism here: we prefer simply to leave our readers free to believe or not to believe, and to consult on this subject the astonishing works being carried out in our own day: a visit to a psychiatric hospital could convince even the most sceptical.

As for ourselves, we adopt without further ado the accounts that have come down to us regarding this extraordinary man, the Comte de Saint-Germain.

And without further discussion, we pass on to the exposition of the facts.

They are strange — they are compelling...

At the first houses of Versailles, the coachman had woken Saint-Germain, then continued driving.

The comte had stopped his carriage on the palace square, or rather on the esplanade that has since become what is today called the Place.

And he had continued on foot to the ruelle aux Réservoirs.

"If only I arrive in time!" he thought with anguish.

But this anguish showed not at all on the outside: Saint-Germain maintained that appearance of coldness he had imposed upon himself and that he kept up even when alone.

He went and knocked at the door of M. Jacques's house.

As always, a peephole opened first, then the door. A lackey appeared.

"What do you want?" said the servant rather roughly, trying to get a look at the stranger's face.

"I should like to speak to the Chevalier d'Assas," said Saint-Germain simply.

"In that case, you are mistaken, monsieur: the person you name does not reside here... look further on."

The lackey pushed the door shut.

Abruptly, the Comte de Saint-Germain extended his arm toward the door — but without touching it.

The lackey froze in the very motion of closing it.

A sort of horror convulsed the man's face.

He was as though paralysed...

"What is the matter, my friend?" said Saint-Germain.

"I don't know... I think that... I'm dying... I can't breathe... oh!..."

"Come now, steady yourself... and above all, do not cry out... I can, better than anyone, cure you of the fulminant malady that has just seized you..."

"You?... ah!... help me!..." the wretch gasped.

"I am a physician," said Saint-Germain, "a great physician... Shall I describe the symptoms of your ailment? You will then be able to judge my learning..."

"I'm choking... I'm... dying... mercy!... help!..."

"Here it is: you have the exact sensation of a ring of fire around your head..."

"Yes, yes!... it burns..."

"And at your throat, the sensation of a powerful hand strangling you..."

"Yes, yes, I'm choking..."

"You cannot make the slightest movement..."

"Yes, yes... I'm turning to stone..."

"I know your ailment, and I have the remedy on my person..."

"Give it to me! Oh! give it to me!..."

"Impossible... In a moment, you will not even be able to speak; in five minutes, you will be dead..."

The lackey tried to let out a great cry.

But as the terrible visitor had warned him, he could not!... Every sensation Saint-Germain had described, he had experienced one by one as it was named.

He no longer doubted that he was on the point of death.

"Take me into the house," Saint-Germain then went on, "see to it that no one sees me, and I undertake to cure you: in a few moments, you will be as vigorous as before the attack. Come, make haste, for I have no time to waste here, since the man I seek does not reside here. Will you let me in?"

"Yes," replied the lackey, without any surprise that his voice had returned to him.

"Lead me, then: here is my hand..."

And without any surprise either that the power of movement had returned as well, the lackey took Saint-Germain by the hand and, having closed the door behind them, led him into the left-hand pavilion — the one d'Assas inhabited.

"There!" said the comte. "If M. Jacques asks who knocked at the door, you will say it was a passer-by who mistook the door. Will you not, my friend?"

"Yes, master!" said the lackey.

"Go, then. I shall wait for you here."

The lackey felt no surprise that this stranger should speak of M. Jacques. He found it perfectly natural for the foreigner to give him orders. He no longer remembered the fulminant malady that had just seized him. He no longer recalled that this physician, or self-proclaimed one, was supposed to cure him.

He obeyed passively, mechanically.

He went to the pavilion that faced the street. There he found M. Jacques, who indeed questioned him, and he gave the answer he had been told to give.

A few minutes later, M. Jacques was leaving with the Comte du Barry and Juliette, bound for the house of the quincunxes, where we have already seen them at work one after the other.

The lackey had returned to the pavilion on the left side of the courtyard.

"What is your name?" asked Saint-Germain.

"Lubin, master," replied the lackey.

And it seemed perfectly natural to him to call this stranger "master." No other form of address presented itself to him.

"Where is the Chevalier d'Assas?" asked Saint-Germain.

"He has gone out," replied Lubin, who no longer had the slightest memory of having insisted that the chevalier did not live there!

"To go... where Mme d'Étioles is?"

"Most certainly. He can only be there!"

"And do you think he will manage to see her?"

"Without the least doubt. The general's precautions are too well laid..."

"What general?... Are you mad?... There is only M. Jacques."

"That is true," said Lubin. "Forgive me..."

"But how do you come to know secrets of such importance?... Come now, you lied to me... your name is not Lubin..."

"That is true, master... my name is not Lubin."

"Your real name, then?... Speak... You must!..."

"Vicomte d'Apremont..." said Lubin, who was sweating profusely.

"Good. I understand. Besides, I much prefer to call you Lubin. I think on your side..."

Lubin's face, which had been convulsed with anguish, became radiant.

"There, you see," Saint-Germain continued; "I know no one here but Lubin... your real name, I do not wish to know it, I have never known it, you hear?"

"Yes, master!" said Lubin, beaming.

"And what is the Chevalier d'Assas supposed to do?" Saint-Germain went on.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
