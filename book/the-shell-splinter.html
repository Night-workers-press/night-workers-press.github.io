The bookLe livre

A word of warning first, as the edition itself gives it: Lupin is barely in this book. Leblanc wrote it during the war without him and, revising it in 1923, wrote him into a single scene. What it is instead is a spy story and a revenge story. On the last Thursday of July 1914 a young engineer brings his bride home to her father's house on the Lorraine border, looks up at the portrait of her dead mother and recognises the woman who stabbed his own father in front of him when he was a boy. Three weeks later the war arrives. Translated from Leblanc's revised text of 1923.

Un avertissement d'abord, tel que l'édition le donne elle-même : Lupin est à peine dans ce livre. Leblanc l'écrivit pendant la guerre sans lui et, en le révisant en 1923, l'y fit entrer pour une seule scène. C'est autre chose : un roman d'espionnage et un roman de vengeance. Le dernier jeudi de juillet 1914, un jeune ingénieur ramène sa jeune femme dans la maison de son beau-père, à la frontière lorraine, lève les yeux vers le portrait de sa mère morte et reconnaît la femme qui a poignardé son propre père sous ses yeux quand il était enfant. Trois semaines plus tard, la guerre arrive. Traduit d'après le texte révisé de 1923.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Part One — Chapter I — A Crime Has Been Committed

“Suppose I told you that I once stood face to face with him—and on French soil?”

Élisabeth looked at Paul Delroze with the tenderness of a young bride for whom the smallest detail about the man she loves is a wonder.

“You’ve seen Wilhelm the Second in France?” she said.

“Seen him with my own eyes, and in circumstances I couldn’t forget if I tried. And yet it was a very long time ago…”

He spoke with a sudden gravity, as though calling up the memory had woken the most painful thoughts in him. Élisabeth said:

“Tell me about it, Paul. Will you?”

“I will,” he said. “In any case, child as I still was at the time, the thing is so tragically bound up with my own life that I couldn’t keep a single detail of it from you.”

They got down. The train had stopped at Corvigny, the terminus of the local line that runs from the departmental town, reaches the valley of the Liseron and ends, some twenty-four kilometres short of the frontier, at the foot of the little Lorraine town that Vauban ringed about—as he says in his Memoirs—with “the most perfect half-moon outworks that could be imagined.”

The station was in a state of extraordinary bustle. There were a great many soldiers and a large number of officers. A crowd of travellers—middle-class families, farm people, workmen, visitors from the spa towns nearby that Corvigny served—waited on the platform among heaped-up bundles for the next train down to the departmental town.

It was the last Thursday in July, the Thursday before the mobilization. Élisabeth pressed anxiously against her husband.

“Oh, Paul,” she said with a shiver, “if only there isn’t a war!…”

“War! What an idea!”

“But all these people going away, all these families getting clear of the frontier…”

“That proves nothing…”

“No, but you read the paper just now. The news is very bad. Germany is getting ready. She has worked the whole thing out… Oh, Paul, if we were separated!… and then, if I had no news of you… and then, if you were wounded… and then…”

He pressed her hand.

“Don’t be frightened, Élisabeth. None of that is going to happen. For there to be a war, somebody has to declare one. And where is the madman, the odious criminal, who would dare take so abominable a decision?”

“I’m not frightened,” she said, “and I’m even certain I should be very brave if you had to go. Only… only it would be crueller for us than for a great many others. Think, darling: we’ve only been married since this morning.”

At the thought of that wedding, so recent, and so full of the promise of deep and lasting happiness, her pretty fair face, lit by its halo of golden curls, was already smiling its most trusting smile, and she murmured:

“Married since this morning, Paul… So you see, my stock of happiness isn’t very large yet.”

There was a stir in the crowd. Everyone was gathering by the way out. It was a general, with two senior officers, making for the yard where a car stood waiting. A military band could be heard: a battalion of light infantry was going past along the station approach. Then came a team of sixteen horses, led by gunners, dragging an enormous siege gun whose outline, for all the weight of the carriage, looked light, so extremely long was the barrel. And a herd of cattle followed.

With the two travelling bags in his hands, Paul, who had failed to find a porter, was still standing on the pavement when a man in leather gaiters, dark green corduroy breeches and a shooting jacket with horn buttons came up to him and took off his cap.

“Monsieur Paul Delroze, isn’t it? I’m the keeper from the château…”

He had a strong, open face, the skin hardened by sun and cold, his hair already grey, and that rather blunt manner you find in certain old servants whose place leaves them entirely independent. For seventeen years he had lived on and managed the great estate of Ornequin, above Corvigny, for Élisabeth’s father, Count d’Andeville.

“Ah, it’s you, Jérôme,” Paul exclaimed. “Very good. I see you got Count d’Andeville’s letter. Have our servants arrived?”

“All three of them this morning, sir, and they’ve helped my wife and me put the château in some sort of order to receive you and Madame.”

He bowed to Élisabeth again, and she said to him:

“So you recognise me, Jérôme? It’s such a long time since I came here!”

“Mademoiselle Élisabeth was four years old. It was a grief to my wife and me when we heard that Mademoiselle wouldn’t be coming back to the château… nor Monsieur le Comte either, because of his poor dead wife. And so Monsieur le Comte won’t be looking in on us this year?”

“No, Jérôme, I don’t think he will. For all the years that have gone by, my father still grieves a great deal.”

Jérôme had picked up the bags and was putting them into an open carriage hired at Corvigny, which he now brought forward. The heavy luggage he would fetch later with the farm cart. The weather was fine. They put the hood down. Paul and his wife settled themselves.

“It isn’t a very long drive,” said the keeper. “Sixteen kilometres… But it’s uphill.”

“Is the château more or less habitable?” Paul asked.

“Well now! It’s not what a lived-in house would be, but you’ll see for yourselves, sir. We’ve done what we could. My wife is so pleased the masters are coming!… You’ll find her at the foot of the steps. I warned her you’d both be there on the stroke of half past six, seven o’clock…”

“A good man,” Paul said to Élisabeth when they had started, “but one who can’t often get the chance to talk. He’s making up for it…”

The road climbed the heights of Corvigny in a steep gradient and formed, in the middle of the town, between its double row of shops, public buildings and hotels, the main thoroughfare, blocked that day with unusual knots of people. Then it went down again and skirted Vauban’s ancient bastions. After that came slight undulations across a plain overlooked, right and left, by the two forts of Little Jonas and Great Jonas. It was along this winding road, threading between fields of oats and wheat, under the shady dome formed above it by the lines of poplars, that Paul Delroze came back to that episode of his childhood which he had promised to tell Élisabeth.

“As I said, Élisabeth, the episode is bound up with a terrible tragedy, and so closely that in my memory the two are one thing and can’t be anything else. The tragedy was much talked about at the time, and your father, who was a friend of my father’s, as you know, read about it in the papers. If he has said nothing to you about it, that was at my request, because I wanted to be the first to tell you what happened… it is so painful to me.”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.
