Chapter One

Paris has nights that are frightening in their silence and darkness. Fog blurs the rooftops, a fine rain slicks the cobblestones, the wind bends the flame in the streetlamps, and the Seine flows silently between its two stone banks. Not a soul on the quays, not a carriage on the bridges.

The great city has gone quiet; honest people have bolted their doors, and the world of thieves is stirring, gathering itself for its dark expeditions. What does it matter that the boulevard is still alive at one in the morning, blazing with the lights of its string of noisy cafés? Here, by the water, the silence is so complete it might be a graveyard.

There's a grim stretch where one arm of the Seine, squeezed between two high walls, flows past with a dizzying pull for anyone thinking of suicide. More canal than river here — water that churns upstream and will pick up speed again downstream — the Seine seems to come to a stop, black, deep, mysterious, thick with strange secrets of death, between the two wings of the Hôtel-Dieu.

Lean on the parapet of the Pont de la Cité or the Pont de l'Archevêché for a while and watch it slide between those two houses of suffering — this same water that will run clear and blue again out past the hills of Sèvres and Saint-Cloud — and its dark stillness will send a chill through you. If you're looking for oblivion in death, come here; if you're still hesitating to leave this life, come here too. Ten minutes of staring into it, and the madness of suicide will climb straight into your skull.

Now, on one of those nights just described, an enormous raft — a timber float, as they call it — was drifting downstream between those two ill-omened arches, the Pont de la Cité and the Pont de l'Archevêché. Three men sat up at the bow, talking low. A fourth, at the stern, worked a crude rudder made from a long wooden beam.

"What filthy weather," said one of the raftsmen, rubbing his arms and hands hard to warm himself.

"My goatskin's soaked clean through," said the second.

"And to think," muttered the third, "we won't be at Mother Camarde's tavern, the one with the Harlequin sign, before two in the morning! I'm thirsty as hell."

"What's stopping you having a drink?" said the first, laughing. "The big cup's full... and it's fresh water, too."

"No thanks. I don't touch the stuff. I only ever drank water once, and it wasn't by choice, mate."

"When was that, then, Notary?" asked the first raftsman.

"When I was down there..."

He leaned hard on the word.

"Ah, right — Toulon?"

"That's it. One night, a mate and I tried to swim for it. He drowned. They caught me."

"Didn't stop you making a run for it later, though."

"Naturally."

The raftsman who'd complained about his soaked goatskin — and whose fresh, ringing voice marked him out as young — said, with a certain enthusiasm:

"Doesn't matter — I wouldn't be scared of the penal colony, me!"

"Beats the Central prison any day. I did two years there, I know what it's like," said the second.

The one who'd come from Toulon went on:

"You're young, Marmouset — how old are you, anyway?"

"Nineteen."

"You've got plenty of time to see it for yourself and compare notes."

And the ex-convict burst out laughing.

But the first of the three raftsmen, the one who claimed two years in the Central prison, didn't share the joke. His eyes were turned upward, fixed on the Pont de la Cité, which the raft was slowly nearing. The huge arch stood out black as India ink against a sky that was already black. Above it, right in the middle, like a small spire on a church roof, stood a perfectly motionless silhouette. Was it a man? Was it a post? There was no way to tell.

"What are you staring at, Mort-des-braves?" asked the raftsman who'd known life in the penal colony.

The man who answered to that odd name pointed toward the arch of the bridge.

"I do believe," he said, "that's a man up there."

"I'd bet on a streetlamp that's gone out," said Marmouset — for that was the nickname they'd given the boy.

"Idiot!" said the convict. "Don't you know your Paris any better than that?"

"Beg pardon?" said the boy, stung.

"Where are we?"

"On the Seine, obviously!"

"Yes, but whereabouts?"

"Near Notre-Dame and the Hôtel-Dieu."

"Well then, you should know there's no streetlamp in the middle of the Pont de la Cité."

More and more stung, Marmouset shot back:

"Like you said — I've got plenty of time to learn."

Mort-des-braves kept staring at the motionless silhouette.

"I've got a feeling," he said, "that's a man about to kick the bucket and cash in his chips."

In the colorful slang of the Paris streets, both phrases simply meant "to die."

"If that's what he wants," Marmouset said coolly. "Maybe it's a broken heart."

"Unless," sneered the convict, "it's some banker who's helped himself to his shareholders' money."

"Oi! Mister!" Marmouset shouted. "Don't be shy... the water's lovely..."

But even as the boy spoke — probably before his voice had even carried up to the bridge — the silhouette made a movement rather like a steamship's funnel folding down to pass under a bridge. Then something dark spun through the air. Then the still water was struck by something falling, and it opened up, a treacherous gulf, to swallow its victim.

"There you go, Marmouset — the gentleman's been served."

And he burst out laughing.

But the fourth raftsman, the one at the stern who'd taken no part in the conversation, let out a cry, let go of the rudder, and dropped into the water.

"Well now," said Mort-des-braves, "what's got into him?"

"He's gone to fish him out, is what!"

"The idiot!" said the ex-convict.

Marmouset cupped his hands around his mouth and shouted:

"Oi, Starling! If you pull him out alive, you'll only get fifteen francs — drown him and it's ten francs more!"

The raftsman just addressed as the Starling was a strapping young man of twenty-seven or twenty-eight, a fearless swimmer for whom the Marne held neither treachery nor mystery. He cut through the water and headed, with the calm precision of a Newfoundland dog, toward a spot he couldn't see — the night was too dark for that — but where he could hear a dull splashing.

The man who'd thrown himself into the water on purpose had sunk straight to the bottom at first. But nature had reasserted itself. Instinctively, the man — who knew how to swim — had come back up to the surface. And then a struggle had begun: a terrible, fierce, relentless struggle between the soul that wanted to leave this life and the body that refused to die.

Meanwhile the raftsman called the Starling reached him and grabbed the drowning man by the hair. The drowning man had begun to slip under — the soul had won out over the body. And Marmouset kept shouting.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
