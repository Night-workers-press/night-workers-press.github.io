The bookLe livre

1538: the truce of Nice is signed, and then Ghent revolts. To reach Flanders quickly, Charles V must cross France — as the guest of the king he has spent twenty years fighting. Zévaco takes one of the strangest true episodes of the century and hangs a love story on it.

1538 : la trêve de Nice est signée, puis Gand se révolte. Pour gagner les Flandres au plus vite, Charles Quint doit traverser la France — en hôte du roi qu'il combat depuis vingt ans. Zévaco prend l'un des épisodes vrais les plus étranges du siècle et y accroche une histoire d'amour.

Chapter I

A Mysterious Summons

Following the signing at Nice of the treaty of peace — of the truce, rather — between François Ier and Charles-Quint, in 1538, a piece of news reached the Emperor that threw him into a great fury: the city of Gand, in the Flanders subject to his dominion, had just risen in revolt. To crush the insurrection there was but one course: to strike swiftly and hard, so as to keep the rebellion from spreading through all the countries of the North, enslaved beneath the Spanish yoke.

It was amid these circumstances, so trying for the empire, that Charles-Quint dispatched to François Ier his secret ambassador, don Sanche d'Ulloa, to obtain from the King of France leave to let the Spanish troops pass through the kingdom. François Ier consented, hoping to be repaid in kind by the addition to the crown of France of the province of the Milanais.

To this end he employed his counsellor, Amauri de Loraydan, who accompanied the commander Ulloa as far as Angoulême.

When the latter, bearer of the good news, reached the Spanish frontier where Charles-Quint awaited him impatiently, he was seized by a presentiment that some frightful misfortune had fallen upon his family, left behind at Séville. There lived the two daughters he adored: Reyna-Christa and Léonor.

During their father's absence, the young girls went out but little. Yet one of them, Reyna-Christa — the younger — had been unable to resist the promises and the vows of love of don Juan Tenorio, a Spanish gentleman who, though married to doña Silvia, had seduced her. Driven mad with grief on learning, from doña Silvia's own lips, of the felony of her seducer, Reyna-Christa chose death rather than shame and dishonour.

Léonor set out for France in search of her father, pursued in her own turn by don Juan Tenorio, dazzled by her bewitching beauty. Brought face to face with don Sanche d'Ulloa, don Juan had the effrontery to ask him for the hand of his daughter Léonor. Don Sanche d'Ulloa, to avenge this affront and the death of Reyna-Christa, fought a duel against the vile seducer — who killed him.

The Emperor betrothed Léonor — against her will — to Amauri de Loraydan.

Happily, Léonor was to have for her defender a chivalrous French gentleman, Clother de Ponthus, and his valet, known as Bel-Argent. Clother de Ponthus had ever placed his sword at the service of noble causes. The court of King François Ier, with its scandals and its dissolute amours, sickened him. Amauri de Loraydan had sworn against him a savage hatred.

One morning, Clother de Ponthus, just as he was about to leave his lodging, saw his valet Bel-Argent come into his chamber, who said to him:

"Monsieur, there is a sort of man in black out there whom I do not like the look of. He asked for you at the hostelry of the Devinière, and learned that you are lodged here. He claims he has a message for you, and wishes to see you."

"Show him in," said Clother.

"Would it not be better that I throw him out the window, or send him back down the staircase head first, or fell him with a blow of my fist between the two eyes?"

"Show him in."

"Seigneur de Ponthus, have you then forgotten the inn of the Grâce de Dieu? Remember at least — for it is close at hand — that you all but passed from life to death in the cage where the damnable Comte de Loraydan condemned you to hunger, and, what is worse, to thirst. Believe me, this so-called messenger, with his spy's face, fit for the gibbet, deserves no manner of trust."

"Show him in."

"Very well," grumbled Bel-Argent, "I am going. But when you have got yourself killed, where the devil shall I find a master such as you?"

Bel-Argent showed in a man of respectable appearance, clad all in black, who bowed before Clother in a salute of good style, and pronounced:

"Have I the honour of speaking to Clother, sire de Ponthus?"

"To himself."

"In that case, I am Jacques Aubriot, intendant general of the Hôtel d'Arronces, and here is a dispatch which the high and noble dame Léonor d'Ulloa, my gracious mistress, charged me to place in your own hands."

Clother turned very pale, and his heart, for an instant, ceased to beat. He seized the folded paper held out to him, unfolded it, and a dizziness came over him... Léonor's letter was composed of a single word, and that word was: COME...

In the moment that followed, Clother suddenly felt, in a flash, that strange impression that this word had not been written by Léonor d'Ulloa. It was to him like a tearing wound, and he murmured to himself: "It would have been too fair a thing!"

During his journey at Léonor's side, Ponthus had twice had occasion to see the young girl's handwriting. We have told with what precision Clother, by an effort of thought that was almost morbid, contrived to reconstitute Léonor in his imagination. The whole of Léonor, as we have said. Not only her likeness, but her voice, the details of her costume, and all that concerned her. In this minute he had before his eyes the true handwriting of Léonor — large, broad, clumsy, a little schoolgirlish, with downstrokes that plainly bespoke the noble Spaniard's disdain for the art of blackening paper, but which, in their tormented structure, proclaimed as well the instincts of a pure artist.

The word "Come" had been traced in a hand fine and firm and upright, and perfectly elegant, with an indefinable air of great pride in the very strokes.

"This is not her handwriting," Clother assured himself.

He set himself then to study, with an avid curiosity suddenly awakened, this paper he held in his hand.

And truly, the look of it was bizarre, disquieting, apt to breed strange suspicions, in very truth.

We have said that there was but one word written; we meant one single word of message, which was: Come.

But above this word, a little higher on the page, someone had written: Clother de Ponthus. It was therefore indeed to Clother that the word Come was addressed. No doubt was possible.

This is nothing. What gave to this paper that disquieting and bizarre aspect of which we spoke, is that, higher still, above Clother de Ponthus, someone had traced the beginnings of illegible lines. And these beginnings of lines were themselves formed of the beginnings of characters, of unfinished letters, of clumsy signs — tormented, shapeless, perfectly illegible.

Clother de Ponthus, at the last, gave a shrug of the shoulders that signified: "I do not understand!"

He considered Bel-Argent, attentive. He considered the messenger, who seemed to him to grow a little troubled.

"You come from the Hôtel d'Arronces?" he asked.

"By way of the inn of the Devinière, yes, seigneur," the messenger answered firmly.

"You are the intendant of the hôtel?"

"I have that honour."

"And you are sent by your mistress? It is she who, for me, entrusted you with this message?"

"Herself, yes, sire de Ponthus," answered the messenger — but this time after a visible hesitation.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
