From the opening pagesLes premières pages

CHAPTER I

The Subterranean Vaults of Saint Mark's

———

In those days, the chief of the Venetian police was a certain Gennaro — Guido by Christian name — a man of some forty years, swarthy of complexion, vigorous of temperament, and, like all functionaries of that republic perpetually convulsed by antechamber revolutions and struggles for power, endowed with a robust appetite for ambition.

Guido Gennaro coveted Dandolo's position, even as Altieri coveted Foscari's, and as Foscari coveted the transformation of his ducal crown into a royal one.

He was, as we have said, chief of both the visible and the occult police of Venice, and had no superior save the Grand Inquisitor himself. This is sufficient to convey that the personage was formidable.

For the rest, he exercised his trade with a species of conscientiousness and had no passion greater than the scenting out of a fine conspiracy — or, if need be, the invention of one from whole cloth — for the joy and honour of thwarting it. He did not gamble, as many a seigneur did who ruined himself at dice. He did not keep a lavish table, and yet received magnificently twice a year, at Easter and at Christmas. No wife nor mistress was known to him. His chief pleasure was to walk alone through Venice of an evening, disguised now as a burgher, now as a waterman; he would then brush past groups of promenaders, enter taverns — of which every landlord was his creature. Maître Bartolo the One-Eyed, proprietor of the Golden Anchor, was among his friends. The result of these perambulations was generally that two or three wretched devils were seized in their beds at the moment they least expected it and found themselves condemned — some to two years in the Leads, others to the galleys, others to five or six years in the Wells: the sinister bounty of the tribunal was inexhaustible. Thereupon the Seigneur Guido Gennaro would rub his hands. It was his custom to say that in a well-policed city, the principal monument, the only truly useful one, was the prison. He was the visible soul of the prison. He dreamt of a gigantic prison in which he might have confined the entire city, and of a social order admitting but two categories of citizens: the prisoners and the gaolers.

On the morrow of the day on which we saw Bembo manoeuvring between Arétin and Sandrigo, and from Sandrigo to Imperia, towards nightfall, the chief of police, Guido Gennaro, was finishing the application of his disguise before a tall looking-glass.

Having completed the work upon his features, he passed into a cabinet where innumerable costumes were hung, and selected a full outfit of a prosperous barcarol, which he donned with scrupulous attention to detail and the slightest accessory.

"Hum!" he grumbled as he dressed. "Here is the occasion or never. Dandolo was made to be Grand Inquisitor as I was made to be King of Spain. And even then!... There he is, at the end of his tether. He leaves me the entire burden of surveillance and will not even hear my reports. Better still, he vanishes, on the pretext of tending to his daughter's husband, who is wounded, they say... wounded by whom? how?... I would gladly give a month's wages to know... But the Palazzo Altieri has become a tomb into which no man may penetrate... The devil himself could make nothing of it... Be that as it may, Dandolo is no longer Grand Inquisitor save in name... and moreover, from what I have gathered, he will not be long in resigning. Who shall be Grand Inquisitor?... Yes, Gennaro, my friend, who is to seize upon those magnificent and redoubtable functions?"

In posing this question, he regarded himself in the mirror and adjusted a fold of his sailor's cap.

"Why should it not be I?" he said suddenly. "I am no patrician? A fine matter! I am, all in all, tolerably well connected! I cut a decent figure. And besides, have all the Grand Inquisitors been patricians of ancient lineage? And the Doges? And the Bishops? Bembo is a nobody... Yes, yes, Gennaro, here is the occasion or never!"

He seated himself in an armchair, placed himself once more before the mirror, and said:

"If the man I see there in that glass were the Doge, here is what I would say to him: 'Monseigneur le Doge, you are in a sorry plight, and the State runs a grave peril alongside you. What am I? Merely the first constable of the Republic. It is something, assuredly. A constable, Monseigneur, is an ear open upon silence, an eye open upon darkness, a hand that gropes through the void, a shadow gliding through shadow. Silence, darkness, void, and shadow yield up their secrets to him. There are no secrets from me, Monseigneur. Be pleased to hear me. You toppled the house of Candiano. The old Doge — you blinded him; very well. The devil knows what has become of him. Unfortunately for you and for the State, the old wolf left behind a cub that has grown. Beware the cub, Monseigneur. His fangs are now exceedingly sharp. The great error of your reign — I shall tell you what it was: you ought to have let old Candiano live and blinded Roland. The old man would have died of grief, and Roland would have been powerless. But one cannot foresee everything. One would have had to foresee that Roland Candiano would pierce through walls ten feet thick, and that the Bridge of Sighs would be for him a mere promenade, such as the Rialto might be for some young lord hastening to parade before his sweetheart. Let us pass on. Come with me, Monseigneur. Let us enter these taverns: you will hear the memory of Candiano exalted there. Let us traverse the port, the Lido, the quays; everywhere, it is the legend of strength, of courage, and of intrepidity. Monseigneur, if you would stifle the legend of Roland the Strong, lock up the entire populace of Venice. Impossible, you say? Then lay hold of Roland!... Ah, ha! There is the point upon which I await you!... Zounds! Lay hold of Roland Candiano? The devil! Oh ho! There is the masterpiece. Roland is in Venice. He is alone there. He defies archers and constables. He is wherever he pleases. You think you have him? He is there no longer! You surround the island of Olivolo? He vanishes! You invade the house by the harbour? He dissolves into smoke. The devil of a man... Well then, Monseigneur, this terrible Roland, who has made himself King of the Mountain and Duke of the Plain, who has behind him two thousand fanatics — this Roland whom the barcarols sing of in hushed voices, of whom women dream, and in whom men place their hope — this Roland, who is about to pulverise you — here he is: I hold him, I bring him to you, take him!... Monseigneur, for such a service, make me Grand Inquisitor.'"

And Guido Gennaro positively bowed before the mirror.

As he straightened, he looked about him, as though in truth he had been surprised not to hear the Doge's reply.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
