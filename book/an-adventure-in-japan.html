The bookLe livre

In 1868 a young French naval officer left his post, crossed to the north, and joined the rebel Republic of Ezo. Eugène Collache fought at Hakodate, was captured, and came home to write the only first-hand account any of the French officers ever published — printed in Le Tour du Monde in 1874, with engravings after his own sketches. This edition sets English and Japanese on facing pages, following the original pagination, with a reading printed over every kanji.

En 1868, un jeune officier de marine français quitta son poste, gagna le nord et rejoignit la République d'Ezo. Eugène Collache combattit à Hakodate, fut capturé, et rentra écrire le seul récit de première main jamais publié par un officier français — paru dans Le Tour du Monde en 1874, avec des gravures d'après ses propres croquis. Cette édition dispose l'anglais et le japonais en regard, selon la pagination d'origine, avec une lecture imprimée au-dessus de chaque kanji.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Some ten years ago, the French government had sent to Yedo [Edo, now Tokyo], the capital of Japan, officers and non-commissioned officers to teach the Japanese troops the rules and practice of our military art. But grave political dissensions were shaking the country, and their mission was soon interrupted. Most of the great Japanese lords, or daimyō, had risen against the head of the temporal government, the Taïcoun [Shogun], regarding the institution of the shogunate — already three centuries old — as a usurpation of true sovereignty, that of the Mikado, who had been imperceptibly reduced to the empty appearance of spiritual power.

The civil war took on formidable proportions. The Taïcoun, after suffering several defeats, lost heart and, giving up the struggle, ceased to contest power. A few princes, however — notably those of Aidzou [Aizu], Sandaï [Sendai] and Nambou [Nanbu] — and the admiral in chief, persisted in defending his cause.

In the midst of these events, one of the French officers, M. B... [Jules Brunet], and four non-commissioned officers attached like him to the mission, resolved to remain in the service of the Japanese admiral, and left Yédo aboard the fleet that was heading north to support the efforts of the princes loyal to the shogunate.

Two other French officers — one M. E. Collache, the author of the account we are publishing, the other M. H. de N... [Henri de Nicol] — then in Japan and eager to offer M. B... their assistance and to join him, embarked at Yokohama on 29 November 1868 aboard the Sophie-Hélène, a merchant vessel chartered by M. V..., a Swiss trader. This small ship, which carried goods under a European flag, first put in at the port of Samimoura (Shark Village), in the province of Nambou.

The two young officers had hoped to find there the army of the Tokugawa (this was the name given to the troops of the former Taïcoun's party, while the troops of the insurgent daimyō were designated by the name of Cangouns [kangun, the imperial army]). 十年ほど前、フランス政府は日本の首都江戸に士官および下士官を派遣し、日本の軍隊にわが軍事技術の規則と実際とを教授させた。しかし国内では重大な政治的対立が起こっており、その使命はまもなく中断された。日本の大領主すなわち大名の多くは、世俗政府の首長たる大君に対して蜂起していた。すでに三百年を経た大君の職を、真の主権者たる帝の権威を簒奪するものとみなしたのである。帝はいつしか、精神的権威という空虚な外見にまで押しこめられていた。

内乱は恐るべき規模に達した。大君は幾度かの敗北を喫して意気を失い、闘いを放棄して政権を争うことをやめた。しかしなお数人の諸侯、とりわけ会津・仙台・南部の各侯と海軍の総裁とは、その大義を守り続けた。

こうした出来事のさなか、フランス人士官の一人B氏と、同じく使節団付きの下士官四名は、日本の提督のもとに留まることを決意し、大君方に忠実な諸侯を支援すべく北へ向かう艦隊に乗って江戸を離れた。

当時日本にいた他の二人のフランス人士官──一人は本誌が掲載する手記の筆者E・コラッシュ氏、いま一人はH・ド・N氏──は、B氏に力を貸し、これに合流することを望み、一八六八年十一月二十九日、横浜からソフィー・エレーヌ号に乗りこんだ。スイスの商人V氏が傭船した商船である。ヨーロッパの旗を掲げて商品を運ぶこの小さな船は、まず南部領の鮫(鮫の村)の港に寄港した。

The Sophie-Hélène, continuing her voyage after selling a few bales of tobacco, made for the town of Aomori, situated at the north of the island of Nippon, in the province of Tsoungarou [Tsugaru].

Aomori is a town of some forty thousand souls. On their arrival M. Collache and his friend were received there, in an otchaya (tea house), by one of the ministers of the prince of Tsoungarou, a friend of M. V...

"The geishas (singing women)," says M. Collache, "accompanied themselves on their samchen [samisen] (a three-stringed guitar with a very long neck). A porcelain cup, thin as an eggshell and full of steaming saki (a fermented drink made from rice), was passed from hand to hand. In little saucers were served eggs prepared in various ways, a kind of turnip preserved in brine called coco [kōko], salted or raw fish, boiled bamboo roots, and awouabis [awabi, abalone], much-prized shellfish. This was only the first course. Then a small lacquer table a foot high was set before each guest, bearing a large porcelain bowl full of rice, two lacquer bowls each filled with a different kind of soup — the principal ingredients being vegetables, eggs, mushrooms, rice pastes and very small fish — and then a saucer containing a piece of grilled fish. Each guest armed himself with little sticks to dip now into one bowl, now into another. Women kneeling a few paces away hastened to refill the rice bowls as soon as they were emptied.

"When appetites had been satisfied, a little tea was drunk and everyone began to smoke, while the singing and dancing started up again."

The minister insisted that the two Frenchmen be his guests. They were shown to their rooms, where futons had been prepared — fairly thin mattresses which, together with a covering shaped like a thick kimono (a long garment with sleeves), make up the whole of Japanese bedding.

"We stretched out on them fully dressed," says M. Collache, "taking care to push away the uncomfortable pillow that had been thoughtfully placed at the head of the bed. It is a small block of wood, very tall and very narrow, supporting a little cushion of rice straw covered with a dozen sheets of paper fastened together in the middle, which are folded over one another as they cease to be clean."

M. Collache had no leisure to study the manners of the inhabitants of Aomori for long. A few days after his arrival, the officers of an American ship warned M. V... that an order had been sent from Yokohama to arrest the two Frenchmen on their way to Yesso. He had no course open but flight. During the night a boat carried him aboard the American ship.

The next day the ship lay at anchor in the bay of Hacodaté [Hakodate], which shares with the town of Matsmaï [Matsumae], on the south-east coast, the honour of being the capital of the island of Yesso. This was the very goal M. Collache and his companion had set out to reach.

At Hacodaté they found their compatriots busy with a plan for the defence of the island, together with the governor, the deputy governor, the admiral and the Japanese general. A peace treaty had at first been obtained from the daimyō, and there were hopes that by establishing themselves in Yesso they might found there a sort of Franco-Japanese colony, capable in time — with France's help — of rivalling Nippon in industry, civilisation and progress. But these first agreements having come to nothing, there could be no doubt that they were exposed to a very imminent attack by the Cangoun fleet. They had, moreover, only a small number of ships, and the enemy had just bought from the Americans a powerful armoured gunboat with a ram and a turret. Even so, courage did not yet fail. The defence plan having been finally settled, the Frenchmen divided the most important tasks among themselves. M. B... went off to restore the fortifications of the other great town of Yesso, Matsmaï; the non-commissioned officers had to train recruits and cavalrymen, put the artillery in order, and so on. M. H. de N... took charge of organising the fleet. M. Collache's special mission was to organise the defence works along the chain of volcanic mountains that protect Hacodaté.

Through the harsh season of 1868-1869, M. Collache, separated from his compatriots, gave himself entirely to this difficult task. He was near completing it when he received word to return immediately to Hacodaté.

幕末外国人見聞録 · Bakumatsu Gaikokujin Kenbunroku

First-hand accounts by foreigners who witnessed the collapse of the Tokugawa shogunate and the Boshin War of 1868–69 — the few months in which Japan's political order was rebuilt from the ground up, described by people standing inside it. The volumes are set as parallel texts, so that each readership can check the other.

Les témoignages directs d'étrangers ayant assisté à l'effondrement du shogunat Tokugawa et à la guerre de Boshin de 1868-1869 — ces quelques mois où l'ordre politique japonais fut refondé de fond en comble, décrits par ceux qui s'y trouvaient. Les volumes sont composés en textes parallèles, pour que chaque lectorat puisse vérifier l'autre.
