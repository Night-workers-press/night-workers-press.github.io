CHAPTER I — THE DEATH OF FAUSTA

At dawn on the twenty-first of February, 1590, the funeral knell tolled over the Rome of the popes — the Rome of Sixtus V. At the same moment, the muffled roar rolling through the streets still dark with night told that crowds were moving toward some mysterious rendezvous. That rendezvous was the Piazza del Popolo. There stood a scaffold. There, before long, the axe gleaming in the executioner's hands would rise above a head. That head the executioner would seize by the hair and hold aloft before the people of Rome. And it would be the head of a young and beautiful woman — a woman whose illustrious name, conjuring the strangest adventure of those distant centuries, was being murmured with a kind of wondering awe by the crowd assembling around the scaffold.

⁂

Princess Fausta had been imprisoned in the Castel Sant'Angelo for ten months — ten months since she had been taken prisoner in this same Rome to which she had lured the Chevalier de Pardaillan… the only man she had ever loved… the man to whom she had given herself… the man she had in the end sought to destroy, and whom she doubtless believed dead. This was what the formidable adventuress — who had dreamed of reviving the tradition of Pope Joan — had been waiting for: the day on which the death sentence passed against her would be carried out. A terrible thing had intervened: the execution had been stayed because, at the very moment of delivering Fausta to the headsman, word came that she was with child. But now that the child had entered the world, nothing could save her.

And soon the hour would strike for Fausta to pay for her audacity and her great war against Sixtus V.

⁂

That same morning, in one of those halls of sumptuous elegance that the Vatican possessed, two men stood face to face, very close, hurling words of mortal hatred into each other's faces. Both were in the prime of life and handsome; both, though men of the Church, wore with haughty grace the elegant dress of the cavaliers of the age. And it was indeed the same hatred that rumbled in both their hearts — for it was the same love that had made them enemies.

One of them was named Alessandro Peretti — the family name of His Holiness Sixtus V. This man was in fact the Pope's nephew. He had recently been created Cardinal of Montalte. He was openly designated as the successor to Sixtus V, whose intimate confidant and counsellor he was. The other was named Hercule Sfondrato; he belonged to one of the most opulent families of the Romagna, and he exercised the office of Grand Judge with a severity that made him one of the most terrible instruments of Sixtus V's will.

And this is what the two men were saying to each other:

— Listen, Montalte, listen! Hear the knell… nothing can save her now, nothing and no one!

— I shall go throw myself at the Pope's feet, the nephew of Sixtus V was raging, and I shall obtain her pardon.

— The Pope! But the Pope, had he the strength for it, would kill her with his own hands sooner than save her. You know it, Montalte, you know it — I alone can save Fausta. Yesterday the sentence was read to her. Now the scaffold stands ready. Within the hour Fausta will have ceased to live — unless you swear to me upon Christ, upon the crown of thorns and upon His wounds, that you renounce her…

— I swear… Montalte stammered, drunk with rage and horror.

— Well then, Sfondrato growled, what do you swear?

They were now so close that they touched. Their wild eyes hurled a last mutual menace, and their hands tormented the hilts of their daggers.

— Swear — swear it then! Sfondrato repeated.

— I swear, Montalte snarled, to tear out my own heart sooner than renounce my love for Fausta — though she hate me with a hatred as imperishable as my love. I swear that while I live no hand shall be laid upon Fausta — neither the headsman's, nor the Grand Judge's, nor even the Pope's. I swear to defend her alone against all of Rome if need be. And in the meantime, Grand Judge — die first, since it is you who pronounced her sentence.

In the same instant, with the swiftness of a lightning-stroke, Cardinal Montalte, nephew of Pope Sixtus V, raised his dagger and drove it into Hercule Sfondrato's shoulder.

Then Montalte flung himself toward the door.

Under the blow Hercule Sfondrato had fallen to one knee. But almost at once he rose again, tore open his doublet in haste, and saw that Montalte's blade had failed to pierce the coat of mail beneath. Hercule smiled a terrible smile:

"Those steel shirts they forge in Milan are of fine temper indeed. I take the blow as received, Montalte! And I swear to you that my dagger shall know how to find the road to your heart."

Montalte had plunged into the covered passage connecting the Vatican to the Castel Sant'Angelo. He reached the dungeon where the vanquished Fausta was awaiting the hour of her death, and he approached, trembling, the door guarded by two halberdiers. The two soldiers made as if to cross their halberds. But doubtless the authority of Sixtus V's nephew carried great weight within the Vatican, for the two guards drew back. Montalte opened the wicket that allowed one to observe the cell's interior.

And this is what Cardinal Montalte saw then through that wicket… A fleeting, swift, and terrible vision.

On a narrow bed lay a young woman… The young mother… she herself… Fausta… a creature of dazzling beauty. In her two hands she holds the child, lifting him in a gesture of strength and tenderness, gazing upon him with her wide and deep eyes.

At the foot of the bed stands a handmaid.

And Fausta, in a voice of strange calm, says:

— Myrthis, you will take him. You will carry him far from Rome. Have no fear — no one will hinder your departure from the Castel Sant'Angelo. I have obtained this much: that when I die, the vengeance of Sixtus V shall die with me.

— I shall have no fear, Myrthis answered with a kind of exalted fervour. Since I must go on living after you are gone, I shall live for him.

Fausta gave the faintest nod, as if receiving and recording this promise. For a moment she was silent; then, her eyes fixed on the child, she added:

— Son of Fausta!… Son of Pardaillan!… What will you be?… Your mother, in dying, gives you the kiss of pride and of strength, by which she hopes her soul will pass into your being!…

It is done. Myrthis has taken into her arms the child she must carry far from Italy — the son of Fausta, the son of Pardaillan. She steps back, she turns away, as if to shield the innocent little being — scarce entered into life — from the sight of his mother entering into death.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
