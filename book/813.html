Leblanc's darkest Lupin novel (1910)

by Maurice Leblanc · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Maurice Leblanc · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

The bookLe livre

The richest man in the world is strangled in his suite at the Palace Hotel in Paris. In his pocketbook the police find a number, 813, and a word nobody can explain. By the next morning his secretary is dead too, and the only man known to have been in the room is Arsène Lupin. The longest and darkest of the Lupin novels, and the one Leblanc was proudest of: Lupin wears three faces at once, hunts a murderer nobody can see and a bundle of letters that could pull Europe apart, and ends on a cliff at Capri with the worst defeat of his life.

L'homme le plus riche du monde est étranglé dans sa suite du Palace-Hôtel, à Paris. Dans son portefeuille, la police trouve un nombre, 813, et un mot que personne ne sait expliquer. Le lendemain matin, son secrétaire est mort à son tour, et le seul homme qu'on sache entré dans la pièce est Arsène Lupin. Le plus long et le plus sombre des romans de Lupin, celui dont Leblanc était le plus fier : Lupin y porte trois visages à la fois, traque un assassin que personne ne voit et une liasse de lettres capable de faire éclater l'Europe, et finit sur une falaise de Capri, sur la pire défaite de sa vie.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Part One — Chapter I — The Massacre

Monsieur Kesselbach stopped dead on the threshold of the sitting room, took his secretary by the arm and murmured uneasily:

“Chapman, someone has been in here again.”

“Come, come, sir,” protested the secretary, “you opened the hall door yourself just now, and while we were at lunch in the restaurant the key never left your pocket.”

“Chapman, someone has been in here again,” repeated Monsieur Kesselbach.

He pointed to a travelling bag standing on the mantelpiece.

“There, the proof is plain. That bag was shut. It is not shut now.”

Chapman objected:

“Are you quite sure you shut it, sir? Besides, the bag holds nothing but worthless odds and ends, toilet things…”

“It holds nothing else because I took my wallet out of it before going down, as a precaution… otherwise… No, I tell you, Chapman, someone came in here while we were at lunch.”

There was a telephone on the wall. He lifted the receiver.

“Hello… This is for Monsieur Kesselbach… suite 415… That is right… Would you get me Police Headquarters, mademoiselle… the Sûreté… I have the number… one moment… Ah, here it is… 822.48… I shall hold the line.”

A minute later he began again:

“822.48? I should like a word with Monsieur Lenormand, the head of the Sûreté. It is Monsieur Kesselbach speaking… Hello? Why, yes, the head of the Sûreté knows what it is about. It is with his authority that I am telephoning… Ah, he is not there… To whom have I the honour of speaking? Monsieur Gourel, police inspector… But surely, Monsieur Gourel, you were present yesterday at my interview with Monsieur Lenormand… Well, monsieur, the same thing has happened again today. Someone has been into the suite I occupy. And if you were to come at once, you might perhaps discover something from the traces… In an hour or two? Perfectly… You have only to ask for suite 415. Thank you again!”

Passing through Paris, Rudolf Kesselbach, the diamond king, as people called him—or, by his other nickname, the Master of the Cape—the multimillionaire Rudolf Kesselbach, whose fortune was reckoned at more than a hundred million, had for a week been occupying suite 415, on the fourth floor of the Palace Hotel: three rooms, of which the two larger ones on the right, the sitting room and the principal bedroom, looked out over the Avenue, while the third, on the left, which served Chapman the secretary, took its light from the Rue de Judée.

Beyond that bedroom, five rooms had been reserved for Madame Kesselbach, who was to leave Monte Carlo, where she was at present, and join her husband at his first word.

For some minutes Rudolf Kesselbach walked up and down with a preoccupied air. He was a tall man, high-coloured, still young, whose dreaming eyes—their soft blue visible through gold-rimmed spectacles—gave him an expression of gentleness and diffidence that sat oddly with the force of his square forehead and his bony jaw.

He went to the window: it was shut. In any case, how could anyone have come in that way? The private balcony that ran round the suite stopped short on the right; and on the left it was cut off by a stone partition from the balconies on the Rue de Judée.

He went through into his bedroom: it had no communication with the rooms on either side. He went through into his secretary’s room: the door opening on the five rooms reserved for Madame Kesselbach was closed, and the bolt shot.

“I can make nothing of it, Chapman. Several times now I have noticed things here… strange things, you must admit. Yesterday it was my cane that had been moved… The day before, somebody had certainly handled my papers… and yet how would it be possible?…”

“It is not possible, sir,” cried Chapman, whose placid, honest face betrayed not a flicker of anxiety. “You imagine it, that is all… you have no proof… nothing but impressions… And besides, this suite can only be entered through the entrance hall. Now, you had a special key made the day you arrived, and nobody has the duplicate but your man Edwards. You trust him?”

“Of course! He has been in my service for ten years… But Edwards lunches at the same time as we do, and that is a mistake. In future he is not to go down until after we come back.”

Chapman gave a slight shrug. There was no doubt of it, the Master of the Cape was becoming a trifle odd with these unexplained fears of his. What risk does a man run in a hotel, above all when he keeps nothing of value about him or near him, no considerable sum of money?

They heard the hall door open. It was Edwards.

Monsieur Kesselbach called him in.

“You are in livery, Edwards? Ah, good. I expect no callers today, Edwards… or rather, yes, one caller: Monsieur Gourel. Until then, stay in the hall and watch the door. Monsieur Chapman and I have serious work to do.”

The serious work lasted a few minutes, during which Monsieur Kesselbach went through his post, ran his eye over three or four letters and indicated the replies that were to be sent. But all at once Chapman, waiting with his pen raised, saw that Monsieur Kesselbach’s mind was on something other than his correspondence.

He was holding between his fingers, and studying closely, a black pin bent into the shape of a fish-hook.

“Chapman,” he said, “look what I found on the table. It is obvious that it means something, this bent pin. Here is proof, here is material evidence. And you can no longer pretend that nobody has been into this room. After all, the pin did not come there by itself.”

“Certainly not,” replied the secretary. “It came there thanks to me.”

“How so?”

“Yes, it is the pin that fastened my tie to my collar. I took it out last night while you were reading, and twisted it without thinking.”

Monsieur Kesselbach rose, considerably put out, took a few steps and stopped.

“You are laughing at me, no doubt, Chapman… and you are right… I do not deny it, I have been rather… eccentric since my last voyage to the Cape. The fact is… well… you do not know what has come into my life… a tremendous scheme… an enormous thing that I still see only through the mists of the future, but that is taking shape all the same… and that will be colossal… Ah, Chapman, you cannot imagine… Money—I do not care about money, I have it… I have too much of it… But this is more than money, this is power, strength, authority. If the reality answers to what I feel coming, I shall no longer be merely the Master of the Cape, but the master of other kingdoms as well… Rudolf Kesselbach, the son of the Augsburg coppersmith, will walk as an equal with a good many men who until now have looked down on him… He will even take precedence over them, Chapman, he will take precedence over them, you may be sure of it… and if ever…”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
