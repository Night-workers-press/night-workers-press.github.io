The bookLe livre

Normandy, the spring of 1894. In a barn in the pays de Caux a dozen noblemen hold a secret trial. The accused is a woman of about thirty who calls herself Joséphine Balsamo, Countess of Cagliostro, and the evidence against her is that she does not seem to have aged. Lying in the loft above, one eye to a crack in the boards, is a young man of twenty, Raoul d'Andrésy, who has not yet decided to be Arsène Lupin. Written in 1924, this is the first episode of Lupin's own life: a treasure hunt the length of Normandy, and the first woman he loved. If you are starting the series, start here.

Normandie, printemps 1894. Dans une grange du pays de Caux, une douzaine de gentilshommes tiennent un procès secret. L'accusée est une femme d'une trentaine d'années qui se fait appeler Joséphine Balsamo, comtesse de Cagliostro, et la preuve qu'on lui oppose, c'est qu'elle ne semble pas avoir vieilli. Couché dans le grenier au-dessus, un œil collé à une fente du plancher, un jeune homme de vingt ans, Raoul d'Andrésy, n'a pas encore décidé d'être Arsène Lupin. Écrit en 1924, c'est le premier épisode de la vie de Lupin : une chasse au trésor d'un bout à l'autre de la Normandie, et la première femme qu'il ait aimée. Pour commencer la série, c'est ici.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — Arsène Lupin at Twenty

Raoul d’Andrésy put out the lamp of his bicycle and threw the machine down behind a bank topped with scrub. At that moment three o’clock struck from the church tower at Bénouville.

In the thick darkness he followed the country lane that served the estate of the Haie d’Étigues, and so came to the walls of the enclosure. He waited a little. Horses stamping, wheels ringing on the stones of a yard, a jingle of harness bells, both leaves of the gate thrown open at once… and a wagonette went by. Raoul barely had time to catch the voices of men and make out the barrel of a gun. The carriage was already on the high road and running towards Étretat.

“Well,” he said to himself, “guillemot shooting is a fascinating sport, and the rock where they are slaughtered is a good way off… I shall find out at last what this sudden shooting party means, and all this coming and going.”

He went along the walls of the estate to the left, followed them round, and after the second corner stopped at the fortieth pace. He had two keys in his hand. The first opened a low door, and beyond it he climbed a stair cut in the hollow of an old half-ruined rampart that flanked one wing of the château. The second let him in by a private entrance on the level of the first floor.

He lit his pocket lamp, and, without taking much care, since he knew that the servants lived on the other side and that Clarisse d’Étigues, the baron’s only daughter, slept on the floor above, he followed a passage that brought him into a large study. It was there that, a few weeks before, Raoul had asked the baron for his daughter’s hand, and there that he had been received with a burst of outraged fury of which he kept an unpleasant memory.

A mirror gave him back his pale boy’s face, paler than usual. But he was trained to strong feeling, he stayed master of himself, and he set to work coolly.

It did not take long. During his interview with the baron he had noticed that the man kept glancing at a large mahogany desk whose roll top was not pulled down. Raoul knew every place where a hiding hole can be contrived, and every mechanism that is worked in such a case. A minute later he had found, in a slot, a letter written on very thin paper and rolled up like a cigarette. No signature, no address.

He studied this missive, whose text struck him at first as too commonplace to be hidden with such care, and by working over it minutely, fastening on certain words that told more than the rest and cutting out the phrases evidently put in to fill the gaps, he was able to reconstruct the following:

“I have picked up our enemy’s trail again at Rouen, and I have had it put into the local papers that a farmer near Étretat had dug up in his meadow an old seven-branched brass candlestick. She at once telegraphed to the carriage hirer at Étretat to send a brougham to Fécamp station on the twelfth at three in the afternoon. On the morning of that day the hirer will receive, from me, a second telegram countermanding the order. So it is your brougham that she will find at Fécamp station, and it will bring her under safe escort into the middle of us at the hour of our assembly.

“We shall then be able to constitute ourselves a court and pass a merciless verdict on her. In the days when the greatness of the end justified the means, punishment would have followed at once. Kill the beast and you kill the venom. Choose whichever solution you please, but remember the terms of our last conversation, and bear well in mind that the success of our undertakings, and our very existence, depend on this infernal creature. Be careful. Arrange a shooting party to turn aside suspicion. I shall arrive by way of Le Havre at four exactly, with two of our friends. Do not destroy this letter. You will give it back to me.”

“Too much precaution is a fault,” thought Raoul. “If the baron’s correspondent had not been suspicious, the baron would have burned these lines, and I should never have known that there is an abduction planned, an illegal trial planned, and even, God forgive me, a murder planned. The devil. My future father-in-law, pious as he is, seems to me to be mixed up in some very unchristian arrangements. Will he go as far as killing? All this is uncommonly serious, and it might very well give me a hold over him.”

Raoul rubbed his hands. The affair pleased him and did not greatly surprise him, since one or two details had caught his attention several days before. So he decided to go back to his inn, sleep there, and return in time to learn what the baron and his guests were plotting, and who this “infernal creature” was whose removal they desired.

He put everything back in its place, but instead of leaving he sat down in front of a little round table on which stood a photograph of Clarisse, and, setting it square in front of him, gazed at it with a deep tenderness. Clarisse d’Étigues, hardly younger than himself. Eighteen. A soft mouth… eyes full of dreaming… a fresh fair face, pink and delicate, with hair as pale as the hair of the little girls who run along the roads of the pays de Caux, and so gentle a look, and so much charm.

Raoul’s gaze hardened. A bad thought he could not master was taking possession of him. Clarisse was alone up there in her separate rooms, and twice already, using the keys she herself had given him, twice already, at teatime, he had gone up to her there. What was to stop him today? No sound could carry as far as the servants. The baron was not due back before the afternoon. Why go away?

Raoul was no Lovelace. A good many feelings of honesty and delicacy stood in him against the loosing of instincts and appetites whose excessive violence he knew. But how was he to resist such a temptation? Pride, desire, love, the imperious need to conquer, all drove him to act. Without lingering over idle scruples he went quickly up the stairs.

At the closed door he hesitated. If he had crossed it before, it had been in broad daylight, as a respectful friend. What meaning did the same act take on at this hour of the night?

The debate with his conscience did not last long. He knocked softly, whispering:

“Clarisse… Clarisse… it is I.”

After a minute, hearing nothing, he was about to knock again and harder, when the boudoir door opened a little way and the girl appeared, a lamp in her hand.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
