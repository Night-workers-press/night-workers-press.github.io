The bookLe livre

An old soldier sits by a window and looks across at the towers of Montmorency with a hatred he has nursed for years. His son will inherit the quarrel, a worn sword, and a piece of advice about not bowing. France is sliding toward Saint Bartholomew; the Wars of Religion are a business opportunity for great families and a death sentence for everyone else. The first volume of the ten-volume cycle, in English for the first time.

Un vieux soldat, assis près d'une fenêtre, regarde au loin les tours de Montmorency avec une haine qu'il entretient depuis des années. Son fils héritera de la querelle, d'une épée usée et d'un conseil sur l'art de ne pas courber l'échine. La France glisse vers la Saint-Barthélemy ; les guerres de Religion sont une affaire pour les grandes maisons et une condamnation pour tous les autres. Premier des dix volumes du cycle, en anglais pour la première fois.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

The Two Brothers

The house was low — all ground floor — with a humble face. Beside an open window, in a heraldic armchair, sat a man: a tall old man with a white head. One of those rough-hewn countenances that the captains wore who had survived the martial epics of King Francis I.

He was fixing a gloomy gaze on the grey mass of the Montmorency feudal manor, which raised against the blue sky, far off in the distance, the proud menace of its towers.

Then his eyes turned away. A sigh, terrible as a silent curse, swelled his chest; and he asked:

— My daughter?… Where is my daughter?…

A serving-woman who was tidying the hall answered:

— Mademoiselle has gone to the wood to gather lily of the valley.

— Yes, of course — it is spring. The hedgerows are fragrant. Every tree is a bouquet. Everything laughs, everything sings — flowers everywhere.

But the fairest flower of all, my Jeanne, my noble and chaste child — it is you…

His gaze then returned to the formidable silhouette of the manor crouching on the hill.

— Everything I hate is there! he growled. There is the power that has broken me, annihilated me! Yes, I — the lord of Piennes, once master of an entire province — reduced to living in near misery, in this humble corner of land left to me by the Constable’s rapacity!… What am I saying, fool that I am! Is he not, at this very moment, trying to drive me from this last refuge!…

Two silent tears carved a bitter furrow through the wrinkles of that desperate face.

Suddenly, he turned dreadfully pale: a rider, dressed in black, was entering and bowing before him!…

— Hell!… The Montmorency bailiff!…

— My lord of Piennes, said the man in black, I have just received from my master the Constable a document which I am ordered to put before you at once. This document, which you see here, is a copy of a ruling of the Paris Parlement dated yesterday, Saturday the 25th of April in this year 1553. The ruling states that you occupy the domain of Margency without right; that King Louis XII exceeded his authority in granting you ownership of this land, which must revert to the house of Montmorency; and that you are hereby enjoined to surrender castle, hamlet, meadows and woodland.

The lord of Piennes did not move, did not stir. Only a greater pallor spread across his face, and his trembling voice rose:

— O my worthy lord Louis XII! And you, illustrious Francis I! Will you not rise from your tombs to see how they treat the man who, on forty fields of battle, risked his life and shed his blood? Come back, sires!

And you shall witness this fine spectacle — the old soldier stripped of everything, wandering the roads of Île-de-France to beg a crust of bread!

Before such despair, the bailiff trembled.

Furtively, he set the accursed parchment on the table, then stepped back, reached the door, and fled.

Then, in that poor house, a mournful and harrowing cry rang out:

— And my daughter! My daughter! My Jeanne! My daughter is without shelter! My Jeanne is without bread! Montmorency! A curse on you and all your race.

The catastrophe was terrible. Margency, which had belonged to the lord of Piennes since the time of Louis XII, was all that remained of that man’s former splendour — a man who had once governed Picardy. In the wreck of his fortune he had taken refuge in this poor domain, hemmed in on all sides by the Constable’s estates.

Now it was finished. The Parlement’s ruling meant, for Jeanne de Piennes and her father, degrading destitution.

Jeanne was sixteen years old. Slender, frail, proud, of exquisite elegance, she seemed a creature made for the delight of the eye — an emanation of that radiant spring, like, in her slightly wild grace, a hawthorn blossom trembling with dew beneath the rising sun.

That Sunday, the 26th of April 1553, she had gone out as every day, at the same hour.

She had made her way into the chestnut forest on whose edge Margency leant. Beneath the trees, Jeanne, breathless, one hand at her heart, began to walk quickly, murmuring:

— Will I dare tell him? This evening — yes, this very evening, I will speak!… I will tell him that terrible secret… and yet so sweet!

Suddenly, two strong and tender arms encircled her. A trembling mouth sought her mouth:

— You, at last! You, my love…

— My François! My dear lord!…

— But what is it, my darling? You are trembling…

He leaned down and clasped her more tightly.

He was a fine, tall young man with an honest gaze, a gentle face, a brow high and serene.

And this young man’s name was François de Montmorency!… Yes — the eldest son of that Constable Anne who had just torn from the lord of Piennes the last shred of his fortune!

Arm in arm, they walked slowly among the open flowers, whose souls breathed out in mysterious perfumes.

At times, a tremor ran through the young woman. She would stop, strain her ears, and murmur:

— Someone is following us… watching us… did you hear?

— Some startled bullfinch, my dearest love…

— François! François! Oh, I am afraid…

— Afraid? My dear one! These three months you have been mine — since that blessed hour when our impatient love outran the law of men to obey the law of nature — more than ever, Jeanne, you are under my protection.

What have you to fear? Soon you shall bear my name. The hatred that divides our two fathers — I shall break it!…

— I know it, my lord, I know it! And even if that happiness were not mine to have, I would still be glad to be wholly yours. Oh, love me, love me, my François! For a misfortune hangs over my head!

— I adore you, Jeanne. I swear by heaven, nothing in this world shall prevent you from being my wife!

A laugh — muffled — rang out close by…

— So then, François continued, if some secret grief is weighing on you, confide it to your lover… your husband.

— Yes, yes!… This evening. Listen — at midnight, I will wait for you… at my good nurse’s cottage… there is something you must know!…

— At midnight then, my beloved…

— And now — go, leave… farewell… until tonight…

One last embrace bound them together. One last kiss made them shiver.

Then François de Montmorency leapt away and vanished into the undergrowth.

For a moment Jeanne de Piennes remained where she stood, moved, breathless.

At last, with a sigh, she turned. At that very instant she went very pale: someone was before her — a man of about twenty, with a violent face, dark eyes, an arrogant bearing. Jeanne let out a cry of terror:

— You, Henri! You!

— Myself, Jeanne! It seems I frighten you! By God’s death, have I not the right to speak to you… as he does… as my own brother?

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
