The bookLe livre

Everything Rostand wrote before the theatre took him: the 1887 prize essay comparing Honoré d'Urfé's sentimental novel with Zola's naturalism, and Les Musardises, the first book of poems. The essay is the surprise — a nineteen-year-old refusing to pick a side in the great literary quarrel of his day, and arguing with real critical nerve that both novels are true. The poems show the voice arriving: light, self-mocking, already in love with the useless gesture.

Tout ce que Rostand a écrit avant que le théâtre ne le prenne : l'essai primé de 1887 confrontant le roman sentimental d'Honoré d'Urfé au naturalisme de Zola, et Les Musardises, son premier recueil. L'essai est la surprise — un garçon de dix-neuf ans refusant de choisir un camp dans la grande querelle littéraire de son temps, et soutenant avec un vrai nerf critique que les deux romans disent vrai. Les poèmes montrent la voix qui s'installe : légère, moqueuse d'elle-même, déjà éprise du geste inutile.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

TWO NOVELISTS OF PROVENCE

Honoré d'Urfé and Émile Zola: the sentimental novel and the naturalist novel

An essay awarded the Maréchal de Villars prize by the Academy of Marseille in 1887

"Let us neither overrate the old novel nor sacrifice it. And why insist absolutely on awarding the prize, on looking for a winner and a loser? There is none — or rather I see two winners: each of them, seen in his own hour, has his crown."

Sainte-Beuve, Nouveaux lundis

It would seem that nowhere should the Novel stand in higher favour than in the country of all-powerful imagination — in this Provence in love with Love, where Love once held its famous courts, and which loves everything that speaks of it; where in the old days, in the manor houses of the nobility, they waited impatiently every new year, with the season of violets, for the coming of the troubadour, that travelling novelist.

There, beside the singing sea, under the blue sky, in the scented air, everything is Novel. And whatever is not, becomes so. For the imagination of the Provençaux is like their sun — that sun whose warm light transfigures and makes things blaze. Colour bursts out wherever it lays its caress; out of an old street climbing through a squalid quarter, out of a group of people in rags, it makes something picturesque and arresting. Ask any painter: with that sun you can make a picture out of nothing! And with that imagination, which has only to shine as the sun does for everything to turn golden and poetic — it does not take much to make a novel either.

Has anyone noticed how, in Provence, the least incident of ordinary life, an insignificant, trivial anecdote, transforms itself and turns dramatic? And this thanks to that facility for telling a tale — perhaps also for telling tall ones — which almost everyone there possesses; to that verve, that enthusiasm in the telling which makes it lively, coloured, carrying; which enriches it with details not always authentic but marvellously chosen, exactly right for making you see, so natural that they would lend plausibility to truth itself, which can be short of it. One would have to be a great enemy of one's own pleasure to hold a touch of southern exaggeration against them — quite unconscious exaggeration, at that — and not to admire the astonishing art of staging a scene, planting the characters, getting the dialogue going. One really cannot be surprised that there have been a great many novelists in Provence. But then, with us, everybody is more or less a novelist!

If you asked for a list of Provençal novelists, few people, having named the best known — the two Mérys, Léon Gozlan, Pontmartin, Louis Reybaud, Madame Reybaud, Amédée Achard, Alphonse Daudet — would forget to end with the name of Émile Zola. How many would think of beginning with that of Honoré d'Urfé?

And yet we must consent to it: our novelists — not only ours, but all those of France — are descended from him. After all, those pretty shepherds wandering in their sorrow under the shade of Forez must be looked on as the elder brothers of today's heroes of fiction, however hard the family resemblance is to make out.

They used to be painted in discreet colours, in muted tones, in love rather than passionate: tender herdsmen of a Virgilian and very gentle melancholy, with nothing troubled or unhealthy about them, always keeping the innocent graces, the fresh pink faces of the Cupids frisking on the first page around some old engraving. Today they are shown in a raw light, pale and withered more often than not, with outbursts of cynical passion or sickly sadnesses — less beribboned and more true, so we are told. But a link still holds. And Céladon begins the line of sympathetic sighing lovers; he is the eternal juvenile lead, who ought by now to be starting to look old, but who keeps transforming himself according to the times and manners, following the fashions. Once he raised his eyes to heaven and, with large gestures, spoke of the rocks of cruelty, invoked the sly god Cupid — and his speech went on interminably, full of mythological allusions. Today that way of paying court would raise a laugh, and he has adopted the new one, taking simpler poses, though no less studied, on sofas and plush ottomans, leaning on the back of an armchair, slipping in a word from behind a fan. But it is still Céladon!

No doubt one must take care not to go too far, and not to try to discover close relations between the old sentimental novel and the new. But to study them face to face, even to suggest a parallel with some discretion — would that not be piquant?

Suppose I had in front of me two canvases by very different masters, a long way apart from each other — a Boucher, say, and a De Nittis. A painter might tell me: "There is no connection at all — Boucher, the delicate, insipid artist, and De Nittis, the impressionist master; do not compare them!" I should compare them all the same. Or rather no: compare is not the exact word. I should simply want to see what this encounter brings into my mind, to enjoy the juxtaposition, to note what ideas it can wake, what points of view it opens; to analyse everything curious that I am made to feel by having there, on my right, the impression of the eighteenth century, and there, on my left, that of the nineteenth.

That is why, in the list of the novelists of Provence, it seems well worth considering the first and the last: Honoré d'Urfé and Émile Zola. Is there not something attractive and new in speaking of the one apropos of the other — of L'Astrée and L'Assommoir, and of the Astrée sequence apropos of the Rougon-Macquart?

D'Urfé and Zola! In the contrast between these two names the genius of Provence reveals itself, full of harshness and violence, and of delicacy too. She is the country of ardent and sensual loves, and equally the country of pure and platonic tendernesses, which keeps the memory of a Petrarch and a Laure de Noves. There is the wild Provence: a girl with tawny hair growing thick on a powerful neck, browned by the sun, superb with health and overflowing sap, loving a strong and truthful speech, but often a hard and cynical one. And there is also a woman of a softened, almost enervated grace, refined in her tastes, Italian in her love of sweetnesses and concetti, with a musical and coaxing way of talking, who has preferred to the simple wholesome smell of her lavender the quintessential, musky perfumes. And the famous phrase comes back to us: Provence appears here indeed as the perfumed beggar-woman — perfumed with d'Urfé, a beggar with Zola.

I

I shall no doubt be forgiven a somewhat over-subtle comparison, if you consider that one does not read Honoré d'Urfé's Astrée altogether with impunity, or without picking up something of its own preciousness and elaboration. Suppose that a very old looking-glass had by some miracle kept the image of everything it once reflected; that the mysterious, sleeping depths of the old mirror were peopled with charming phantoms, with confused shades of shepherds and shepherdesses passing arm in arm — touching, faded couples, of a ridiculousness that moves you; and that in the tarnished frame we saw the society of long ago bowing and smiling, with its outdated graces. Today's society would appear facing it, reproduced in a modern glass, tall and clear. Obviously, if it were a question of comparing the two mirrors, we should be led to compare what they reflect: the two societies. So we shall speak of Honoré d'Urfé's society and of Émile Zola's. And afterwards we can attend to the mirrors, which are certainly no longer made today as they were made then — flattering and mendacious, lending charm and poetry, reflecting things at their best — but cruelly faithful, reproducing everything with a hard, cold, implacable brilliance; and there are some one might almost reproach with making things uglier than they are.

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
