The bookLe livre

Henri III kneels in the street to wipe the blood from a dying favourite's face, and the crowd that wanted him dead an hour ago weeps for him. Zévaco understood mobs. Fausta's design comes apart, but not cleanly, and not for long — this is the volume in which the saga discovers that defeating her is not the same as being rid of her.

Henri III s'agenouille dans la rue pour essuyer le sang au visage d'un favori mourant, et la foule qui le voulait mort une heure plus tôt pleure sur lui. Zévaco comprenait les foules. Le dessein de Fausta se défait, mais mal, et pas pour longtemps : c'est le volume où la saga découvre que la vaincre n'est pas s'en débarrasser.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

II. HENRI III

The King, without paying any attention to Guise, halted before Joyeuse and, kneeling, cried into the silence:

—Lord Jesus, You called to me—to me, a poor king whom his subjects have struck, abandoned, driven out! Here I am, my gentle Lord Jesus! And since You have gone so far as to call me to Your aid, let me wipe away the precious blood that flows from Your wounds!…

At these words, Henri III rose, drew his handkerchief, and set about mopping Joyeuse’s brow.

The crowd is fickle in its feelings. At the sight of the King kneeling before the figure representing Jesus, incorporating himself, as it were, into the Parisian procession, furious applause broke out. The King raised his arms to command silence.

—Seize those two wretches! he cried, pointing at the two terrified flagellants. Throw them in prison and then hang them, short and sharp!

—But, Sire, stammered Joyeuse, Your Majesty is mistaken… it was not they…

—Thus shall be treated the enemies of God and the Church! cried Henri III.

An immense acclamation greeted these words, and this time it was a great shout of “Long live the King!” that rose to the heavens. Henri III’s eyes flashed. Then he turned to the Duke of Guise:

—Cousin, said he, let us go and praise and bless the Lord for the great joy He grants us on this day. And then we shall hear, at the townhouse of the good aldermen of this city, the complaints that our Parisians have charged you to convey to us. And, turning his back on Guise, he was the first to make for the great central portal, thrown open wide.

—[…]

He followed with his gentlemen and entered the enormous church, where the Mass of thanksgiving was immediately begun. Outside, the mingled crowd of Parisian penitents and Chartres burghers took what it could of this Mass—which is to say what reached them of hymns and benedictions through the open doors.

When the Mass was over, Henri III, surrounded by guards, came out of the church and made his way toward the aldermen’s townhouse, where the city of Chartres was offering him a hospitality that was, if not royal, at least perfectly sufficient for a king without a kingdom. He had not addressed a single word to Henri de Guise.

On the parvis, the Duke had stopped, uncertain what to do, devouring his rage, wondering whether he should not at once take the road back to Paris.

At that moment, one of Henri III’s gentlemen approached him and, having bowed, said:

—My lord Duke, the King my master has asked me to tell you that he will receive you tomorrow morning at nine o’clock, in audience at the townhouse, together with the men of the law and the burghers who serve as your escort…

—Tell His Majesty, he replied, that I thank him for the audience he is pleased to grant me, and that I shall present myself at the appointed hour.

Thereupon, Guise and his people made their way toward the Soleil-d’Or inn. As for the Cardinal of Guise and Mayenne, they had gone there directly and had not been seen since the procession entered Chartres. At the moment Guise and his gentlemen were entering the inn, Maurevert seized Maineville’s arm and, pointing out a face in the crowd, said to him, turning pale:

—Look…

—What is it? said Maineville, unconcerned.

—No, it is not he! Maurevert then said, passing his hand over his brow… but for a moment I thought it was Pardaillan…

The Duke heard these words and started.

—Where is he? he asked in a low, hoarse voice.

—He is dead! answered someone beside him.

Guise, Maineville, Bussi-Leclerc and Maurevert, in a single movement, turned around and saw the Duchess of Montpensier smiling. She beckoned Guise to follow her.

—By God! growled Bussi-Leclerc, if he is dead, it has not been for long!

The Duke, troubled, had walked on to the apartment assigned to him, drawn along by his sister.

—Brother, she said when they were alone, you must cease making enquiries about this Pardaillan.

—You say he is dead? How do you know?

—I know it through her who knows all, who has never yet been wrong, who has never yet deceived us…

—Fausta? said the Duke with a start.

—She has just confirmed the thing to me.

Guise remained pensive. Had Bussi-Leclerc been mistaken?… Fausta, for her part, was never wrong! No doubt she had known that Pardaillan was in the procession. No doubt she had set some trap into which, that very night, the Chevalier had fallen, after his encounter with Leclerc.

Guise carefully concealed his feelings. But the deep sigh that escaped him proved to his sister what relief he drew from this news.

—Let us leave that, he resumed. Whether this adventurer is alive or dead, it is all the same to me. Where is the man?

—In Chartres, the Duchess replied calmly. He came with the procession. Are you ready, brother?

—Ready?… What do you mean by that? said the Duke with a shudder. I will on no account be mixed up in what is about to happen. I am lost if ever it is learned…

—Then rest easy! The King’s death will be nothing but one of those accidents God permits from time to time. No one will know. Jacques Clément himself does not know. Only be ready, brother!…

—When will the… accident take place?

Marie de Montpensier looked at her brother and answered:

—Tomorrow!…

—So soon!… murmured the Duke with a shudder.

—Tomorrow, after the audience, Valois will proceed to the cathedral in procession, barefoot, taper in hand, and covered in a penitential sack. It is a vow he made, should he be reconciled with Paris. Now, tomorrow, the reconciliation will be complete. The monk will walk beside the King, for in these processions he is accessible to all. The blow will be struck before the cathedral. You, meanwhile, will gather outside the walls whatever gentlemen and Leaguers you have… the rest is your affair!

The Duke of Guise, having summoned Mayenne and the Cardinal, conferred with them at length. Then, toward evening, he sat down to table and insisted that Maurevert, Leclerc and Maineville take their places beside him. And, despite the terrible deed that was being prepared in the shadows, it was still of Pardaillan that they spoke. Bussi-Leclerc very aptly recalled that the Chevalier had said to him:

—I may not even reach Chartres!…

There was no longer any doubt: Pardaillan was dead.

Around that same hour, the man who was the object of these sinister thoughts was dining calmly with the Duke of Angoulême in a small inn, at a table set against a window. Across from the inn stood a townhouse, and from time to time Pardaillan, lifting the curtain, cast a glance at the façade where everything was dark.

—Who owns that house? Pardaillan asked the serving girl, lifting the curtain once more.

—That house?… Ah, well… it belongs to nobody, as you might say. That is, in the old days, it was the townhouse of the lords of Bonneval. But in all my life—and that is twenty-nine years now—I have never seen anyone go in there, never seen the door or the windows open.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
