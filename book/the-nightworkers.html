Les Travailleurs de la Nuit

The true story behind Arsène Lupin — memoirs of the anarchist gentleman burglar

The bookLe livre

Between 1900 and 1903, Alexandre Marius Jacob and the crew he called les Travailleurs de la Nuit committed some hundred and fifty burglaries across France. He robbed the rich, refused violence, left notes, and gave a share away. Tried at Amiens in 1905, he explained himself to the court in a speech the anarchist press reprinted as Pourquoi j'ai cambriolé. Maurice Leblanc read the reports and, two years later, invented Arsène Lupin. This is Jacob's own account — the first book this house translated, and the origin of its name.

Part Two

The Road to Pont-Rémy

II

The rain had stopped, but a fog of the densest kind wrapped the road and the countryside in gloom. Without the trees that stood at intervals along the road, it would have been very difficult for us to find our bearings. From time to time, Bour flashed a few beams with the electric lamp, trying to discover a milestone.

Where were we? Where were we going?

For the moment, these questions were so many mysteries. Fortunately, everything comes in time—even the discovery of a milestone. It was only after reading its inscriptions that we learned we were on the departmental road from Poix to Abbeville. But alas! Poix was far away, very far away, and the prospect of covering the distance on foot was not one to fill us with enthusiasm. Our aim was to reach any village with a station, so that we could take the train for Boulogne-sur-Mer. In the hope of finding one before Poix, we kept going.

An hour after leaving Abbeville, having crossed the village of Épagne, I felt such exhaustion that I proposed a halt of a few minutes. I needed rest. Not yet fully recovered from a sharp bout of fever that had kept me in bed for eight days in Paris, this forced march through the night, in fine but penetrating rain, had laid me low. My companions made no objection to this wish. As the grass was wet, I spread my raincoat on the ground and the three of us sat down on the embankment, at the foot of a tree beside the road.

“I’ve been wondering what that she-ape meant,” Bour said.

For the moment I did not catch the allusion.

“Which she-ape?” I asked.

“The apparition at the window, of course!”

“Ah!… I’d wager it’s her ‘There are still three of them!’ that’s tickling your curiosity?”

“Exactly.”

“I’ll explain. Do you remember the job on December the 24th, when we came to burgle the church of Saint-Jacques?”

“Do I remember! I remember the bottle of white wine too,” he added, laughing.

Then, coming to the point:

“But I don’t see what—”

“Let me go on and you’ll understand,” I interrupted.

I relit my cigarette, which had gone out, and continued:

“Since your memory is so obliging as to recall the bottle of white wine, you must also remember that on the way back from the job, past the bridge over the Somme, the café owner on the corner, in a shrill voice, greeted us as we passed with a resounding: ‘Good evening, troops!’”

“I should think I remember. You answered: ‘Evening, citizen.’”

“At the time we understood nothing of this salutation. It was only on arriving at the station, where we saw a troupe of comedians preparing to catch the train, that we grasped the allusion. They had taken us for traveling players. On the other hand, you must also recall that I was obliged to have our tickets upgraded to second class because the 3:30 train did not carry third class. Now, the next day, after the discovery of the sacrilege, the fellow from the ‘Good evening, troops!’ as well as the ticket inspector at the station both remembered our passing. And so, as the day after that you could read, as I did, in the newspaper: ‘The malefactors numbered three. They took the 3:30 train for Paris.’ And that, finally, is why the turtle at the window, who had no doubt read the papers, said to us a few hours ago: ‘There are still three of them!’—meaning, there are still three of them, just as at the church of Saint-Jacques. Do you follow?”

“Perfectly.”

I had barely finished my explanation when Pélissard drew our attention to a discovery he had made while scrutinizing the darkness.

“Look over there,” he said, extending his arm in the direction of the east. “Don’t you see a light?”

Then, without waiting for a reply, he went on:

“I’ve been watching it for a good while now. And the more I look, the more I believe I’m not mistaken. It’s the headlamp of a train, I’d say.”

Indeed, he was not mistaken. A few minutes later, the train, rounding a curve, showed us its flank. Then it was no longer a single light but dozens that we saw chasing one another. Each carriage door seemed a lamp. You would have said it was a steamer with its lit cabins, the light filtering through the portholes. As the train advanced it changed position, following the sinuosities of the rails. From the distance that separated us from it as it passed opposite our position, we judged that the railway line could not be far off.

“It would be the very devil,” said Pélissard, “if we didn’t find a railway station within four or five kilometers.”

And with that hope, having each lit a cigarette, we set off again, still trusting to luck.

About an hour later, we arrived at Pont-Rémy.

Ordinarily, when I set out on a trip, I took the precaution of bringing a Chaix timetable with me. As ill luck would have it, on this particular trip I had forgotten it. So, in order to remain no longer in uncertainty, our first concern was to make for the station and consult the posted schedule. The station was locked; but a notice was posted against the glass panes of the door, and with the help of our lamp we were easily able to satisfy our curiosity.

Until 6:10 in the morning, no train stopped at this station. I checked my watch; it was barely two o’clock. Four hours, then, that we had to wait.

Certainly, had such an adventure befallen us a few months later, all well and good! The situation would have been bearable—pleasant, even. For my part, I am one of those for whom a night under the open sky, in high summer, holds no terrors. But unfortunately for us, the season was not so far advanced; and in April, in rainy weather, the Picardy sky offering not the slightest charm, we resolved to go and seek shelter at the two hotels adjoining the station.

I at one, Bour and Pélissard at the other, we rang, called, knocked, shouted, and banged—all in vain; no one gave a sign of life.

This silence boded ill. I especially was fairly well convinced that neither of the innkeepers would consent to receive us. But, whether from a sense of duty or to avenge myself upon their impudent silence, I went along with Pélissard’s suggestion that we serenade them. But there too we wasted our efforts. We hammered our fists raw, drumming on their storefronts for a good five minutes, to the tune of “Viens Poupoule”; yet the honorable innkeepers did not deign so much as to answer us. In truth, this did not surprise me. In all my traveling, this was not the first time it had been given me to appreciate the hospitable customs of country innkeepers. Let it rain, let it snow, let it blow, let it freeze hard enough to crack stone—these are miseries about which this particular breed of biped cares very little. Is he not warm and dry himself? So he rarely gives shelter, at night, to a stranger. It is all he can do to condescend to examine the intruder through his window.

ContextContexte

Alexandre Marius Jacob (1879–1954)

Between 1900 and 1903, Jacob and the crew he called les Travailleurs de la Nuit carried out some hundred and fifty burglaries across France. He targeted the rich, refused violence, left notes behind, and gave a share away. Tried at Amiens in 1905, he was sentenced to hard labour for life and transported to French Guiana. Maurice Leblanc read the trial reports and, two years later, published the first Arsène Lupin story. Jacob's memoirs were the first book this house translated, and the origin of its name.

Entre 1900 et 1903, Jacob et la bande qu'il appelait les Travailleurs de la Nuit commirent quelque cent cinquante cambriolages à travers la France. Il visait les riches, refusait la violence, laissait des mots, redistribuait une part. Jugé à Amiens en 1905, il fut condamné aux travaux forcés à perpétuité et transporté en Guyane. Maurice Leblanc lut les comptes rendus du procès et, deux ans plus tard, publia la première nouvelle d'Arsène Lupin. Les mémoires de Jacob furent le premier livre traduit par cette maison, et l'origine de son nom.
