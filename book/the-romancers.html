Les Romanesques

A comedy in three acts (1894)

by Edmond Rostand · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Edmond Rostand · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

ForthcomingÀ paraître

The bookLe livre

Two fathers, neighbours and old friends, want their children to marry. Knowing that nothing kills a romance faster than parental approval, they build a wall between the gardens, stage a spectacular feud, and hire a professional abductor so that the boy can rescue the girl and win her properly. It works. Then the children find out. Rostand was twenty-six, and already writing about the gap between the pose and the feeling — the subject he would spend the rest of his life on.

Deux pères, voisins et vieux amis, veulent marier leurs enfants. Sachant que rien ne tue un amour plus vite que l'assentiment des parents, ils dressent un mur entre les jardins, mettent en scène une brouille spectaculaire et louent un ravisseur professionnel pour que le garçon sauve la fille et la mérite. Cela marche. Puis les enfants découvrent tout. Rostand avait vingt-six ans, et écrivait déjà sur l'écart entre la pose et le sentiment — son sujet pour le reste de sa vie.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

ACT ONE

The stage is cut in two by an old mossy wall garlanded all over with rambling creepers. Right, a corner of Bergamin's park; left, a corner of Pasquinot's park. On each side, against the wall, a bench.

When the curtain rises, Percinet is sitting astride the top of the wall with a book on his knee, reading aloud to Sylvette, who listens attentively, standing on the bench on the other side of the wall and leaning on it.

SCENE I

SYLVETTE, PERCINET.

SYLVETTE

Oh, Monsieur Percinet — but how beautiful it is!

PERCINET

Isn't it?... Listen to Romeo's answer:

"It's the lark, my love — I tell you it's the lark!

Look, the edge of the thin mist is turning to lace,

and over there, on the rose-coloured summit of the far mountain,

morning is rising on the tips of her toes!

We must go —"

SYLVETTE, quickly, listening

Hush!

PERCINET listens a moment, then:

Nobody! So, mademoiselle,

don't put on that startled look of a little bird

about to fly off the branch at the least noise...

Listen to the Immortal Lovers talking:

She: "Love, dear love, no, that is not the dawn —

it's a meteor, to light your going!"

He: "Since she will have it so — very well! It is not

the lark singing and the dawn breaking:

that gleam is yours, Cynthia, up in the cloud!

Let Death come — Death will be welcome!"

SYLVETTE

Oh no, I won't have him talking about that,

or else I shall cry —

PERCINET

Then let's leave it there!

And, closing our book until tomorrow,

let us let sweet Romeo live, since it pleases you.

What an adorable place this is — made on purpose, it seems,

for coming and rocking oneself in the great Will's lovely verse!

SYLVETTE

Yes, the verse is very beautiful, and the divine murmur

of the branches does go well with it, that's true,

and the setting suits it, all this green shade;

yes, Monsieur Percinet, the verse is very beautiful!

But what makes its beauty go straight to me

is that you read it in that singing voice of yours.

PERCINET

The wicked flatterer!

SYLVETTE, sighing

Ah, the poor lovers!

How cruel their fate is — how unkind people were to them!

Ah, I'm thinking —

PERCINET

Of what?

SYLVETTE, quickly

Of nothing!...

PERCINET

Of something

that has suddenly turned you quite pink!

SYLVETTE, the same

Of nothing!...

PERCINET, shaking a finger at her

Oh, the liar — with eyes far too transparent!

I can see what you're thinking of!...

Our parents!

SYLVETTE

Perhaps —

PERCINET

Of your father, and mine, and this hatred

that divides them!

SYLVETTE

Yes, that's what grieves me,

what makes me cry in secret, often.

When I came back from the convent last month,

my father pointed to your father's park

and said: "My dear child, there you see the lair

of my old mortal enemy, Bergamin.

Keep clear of that scoundrel and his son;

promise me — otherwise, you see, I disown you —

that you will always be an enemy to those people,

because their family has loathed yours from the beginning of time!"

I promised... You see, monsieur, how well I keep my word.

PERCINET

And didn't I promise my father, just the same,

to hate you for ever, Sylvette? — And I love you!

SYLVETTE

Holy Virgin!

PERCINET

And I love you, child!

SYLVETTE

It's a sin!

PERCINET

A big one... What do you expect? The more you're prevented

from loving someone, the more you want to.

Sylvette, kiss me!

SYLVETTE

Never in my life!

PERCINET

But you love me!

SYLVETTE

What is he saying?

PERCINET

Dear child,

I'm saying what your heart still holds out against,

but doubting it any longer would be a pretence!

I'm saying... what you yourself said a moment ago —

yes, you yourself, Sylvette, when you compared

the Lovers of Verona to the two children here.

SYLVETTE

I made no comparison!...

PERCINET

You did!... My father and your father

to Juliet's and Romeo's, my dear!

That's why Juliet and Romeo are us,

and that's why we love each other like mad things!

And I defy them both, for all their sharp hatred —

Pasquinot-Capulet, Bergamin-Montague!

SYLVETTE, moving a little closer to the wall

So we love each other? But, Monsieur Percinet,

how did it happen so fast?...

PERCINET

Love is born;

one never knows how, or why, or when it means to be born.

I used to see you go past from my window —

SYLVETTE

And I you —

PERCINET

And our eyes talked on the sly.

SYLVETTE

One day, there, near the wall, I was picking up walnuts,

by chance —

PERCINET

By chance, there, I was reading Shakespeare;

and — to bring two hearts together, see how everything conspires —

SYLVETTE

The wind blew my ribbon, psst!... over to your side!

PERCINET

To give it back, I climbed straight up on the bench —

SYLVETTE, climbing

I climbed up on the bench —

PERCINET

And from that day, little one,

every day I wait for you, and every day my heart

beats faster when at last — blessed signal! —

your soft nesting laugh comes up behind the wall,

and never finishes before your head comes out

of the trembling tangle of wild vine!

SYLVETTE

Since we love each other, we must be betrothed.

PERCINET

That is exactly what I had just been thinking.

SYLVETTE, solemnly

Last of the Bergamins, to you is joined

the last of the Pasquinots!

PERCINET

Noble madness!

SYLVETTE

They will speak of us in ages to come!

PERCINET

Oh, children too tender, of two fathers too hard!

SYLVETTE

But who knows, my friend — perhaps the hour is striking

when God means their hatred to be put out by us?

PERCINET

I doubt it.

SYLVETTE

I have faith in events,

and I can already see five or six endings

that are perfectly possible.

PERCINET

Really — and what are they?

SYLVETTE

Well, suppose

— I've read such a thing in more than one old romance —

that the Reigning Prince happens to pass by one day...

I run and beg him, I tell him about our love,

about the old hatred between our fathers —

a king did marry off Don Rodrigo and Ximena —

the Prince sends for my father and Bergamin

and reconciles them —

PERCINET

And gives me your hand!

SYLVETTE

Or else it works out the way it does in Donkey-Skin.

You waste away, some fool of a doctor gives you up —

PERCINET

My father asks me frantically: "What do you want?"

SYLVETTE

You say: "I want Sylvette!"

PERCINET

And his stubborn pride

is forced to give way!

SYLVETTE

Or else, another adventure:

an old duke has seen some portrait of me,

falls in love, sends a magnificent squire in his name

to offer to make me a duchess —

PERCINET

And you answer: "No!"

SYLVETTE

He is furious: one fine evening, in some dark walk

of the park where I have gone to dream about you,

I am carried off!... I scream!...

PERCINET

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
