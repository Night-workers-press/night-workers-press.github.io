From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — TRUANDS AND RIBALDS

In the period of which we are attempting to give some notion through the character of our personages and the adventures they may have lived, public affairs were wrapped in far less mystery than they are today.

In our own day a police operation—a round-up, for example—remains a secret until it has been carried out.

The truands of the Court of Miracles were one and all aware of the expedition being prepared against them, a sort of vast dragnet conceived by Monclar at the prompting of Ignatius of Loyola.

The single thing the truands did not know was the day on which the attack would fall.

In the meantime, the Court of Miracles had made ready to withstand a veritable siege.

The King of Argot—the beggar Tricot—had very cunningly put about the rumour that the expedition would not take place; but, thanks to the counsel of Manfred and Lanthenay, they had acted as though the king's men were on the very point of arriving.

That is to say, they had heaped up provisions, and they had strongly barricaded the alleys that gave onto the Court of Miracles.

Tricot, at first opposed to any resistance, had feigned to take seriously his role of general of an army.

That evening, as was his custom, he had posted advance sentinels in the streets surrounding the vast quadrilateral. Only, these sentinels were creatures of his own, and to each of them he had given this watchword:

"Say nothing, and let them pass!"

These few explanations given, let us return to Ragastens.

The chevalier and Spadacape had rapidly drawn away from the Rue Saint-Denis, making by the shortest path toward the Court of Miracles. Ragastens was uneasy.

He had to reach Manfred, whatever the cost.

Now, he had run up against difficulties so strange every time he had tried to enter onto the territory of Argot that he almost despaired of ever penetrating it.

"Monseigneur," said Spadacape, smiling into his terrible moustache, "do you know what I was thinking, when we were shut up in the cellar of the Tuileries enclosure?"

"No, but I should be glad to have you tell me."

"I was thinking that if, by chance, one of the soldiers had been thirsty, and had wished to drink the wine from our cask…"

"I can guess the rest: we should have been obliged to come to blows. But why this recollection?"

"For nothing; because I was thinking that men who are thirsty are capable of anything—even of forgetting an order."

"I see what you mean. A poor expedient! I have tried it… No, I have another idea that may perhaps succeed. Come along…"

Soon they found themselves in the maze of foul and gloomy alleys that wove an inextricable web about the truands' domain.

Ragastens's idea was to recount to the first truand who tried to bar his passage what he had just learned from the king's own lips—namely, that at midnight the empire of Argot was to be assailed by the whole of Monclar's forces.

To his great astonishment, he advanced without hindrance.

All at once he found himself in an alley across the middle of which rose a barricade.

"Aha!" he thought, "so it seems our people were on their guard! It is here we shall be stopped."

A man stood up beside him.

"My friend," said Ragastens, "I must pass; the lives of us all depend upon it."

"You are of the court?" said the man. "Pass."

It was one of Tricot's sentinels.

"What court does he mean?" Ragastens asked himself.

And he stepped rapidly over the obstacles heaped up in that place.

Without pausing to wonder further what this extraordinary ease could signify—above all at a moment when, more than ever, the truands' suspicions ought to have been awake—Ragastens went on his way.

And it was a violent emotion for him to perceive, at the end of the alley, a vast open square lit by fires.

A few seconds later he was within the Court of Miracles. He stopped at first to take his bearings, if he could, and to cast a glance over the strange spectacle unfolding about him.

Five or six braziers, kindled at intervals, burned with heavy flames wrapped in smoke; now and then a gust of wind drove the smoke aside, and then the flames lit with red reflections the houses that bordered the vast quadrilateral—houses cracked and leprous, whose black windows seemed so many shifty eyes fixed upon the square.

About each fire swarmed a true multitude, and round great tables men of sinister visage, women of weary countenance, sang in raucous voices and drained their pewter goblets, which the ribalds refilled with wine as fast as they were emptied.

Others, seated on the sodden ground, furbished rapiers or whetted daggers.

Some were charging arquebuses.

Ragastens and Spadacape passed through the midst of these groups without anyone appearing to pay them the least attention.

For, since they were there at all, they must have given good reasons to the sentinels.

Ragastens studied with avid attention these bizarre groups, which formed, in the glow of the braziers, a fantastical whole.

He sought to make out, among these dark figures, among these fierce countenances, the open, laughing, energetic face of the young man he had drawn from the charnel-pit of Montfaucon.

But would he succeed in recognising him, even if he saw him?

At the centre of the square, a larger and more interesting group drew his attention. There, no one drank, no one sang. And this group, made up of two or three hundred men, seemed to be listening attentively to someone who was speaking.

These men were all solidly armed.

Most of them wore cuirasses.

They constituted, in short, the true army of the Court of Miracles.

Ragastens drew near, threaded his way through the close-packed ranks, and reached the foremost places.

At the centre of this group, in a fairly wide space left clear, rose a sort of scaffold composed of planks laid across empty barrels.

Upon this scaffold there was a chair, and upon the chair a seated man was speaking in a voice loud enough to be heard by the whole group. Ragastens recognised him at once. This man was Tricot.

By the attentive silence that reigned about him, Ragastens divined what authority this brigand enjoyed.

Near the scaffold a few men waited, perhaps to speak in their turn before this kind of assembly of notables; for, in the Court of Miracles as everywhere else, one found a social hierarchy—softened, it is true, by the independence each member of the brotherhood might claim.

"In a word," Tricot was saying, finishing a harangue begun some minutes earlier, "they will not attack us; they will not dare! We have privileges hallowed by the usage of several centuries; we have something better still—we have force! Therefore I say we have nothing to fear. But if it be impossible that the grand provost should have so far lost all prudence as to risk a violent attack upon the kingdom of Argot, understand also that you must not give yourselves over to dangerous provocations. I propose, then, that the barricades—which are a sort of useless insult addressed to the grand provost—be pulled down this instant, and that each of you, laying down his arms, go quietly to sleep. I have spoken."

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
