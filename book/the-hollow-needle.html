The bookLe livre

A shot in the night at a Normandy country house, a dead man, and a wounded burglar who vanishes from a walled park the police have surrounded. The detective nobody expects is Isidore Beautrelet, seventeen, a schoolboy who reasons his way to the truth from a newspaper and a scrap of paper covered in dots and figures. Then the book opens out: the scrap of paper hides the oldest secret of the kings of France, kept in plain sight on the coast at Étretat. The cipher is printed exactly as Leblanc printed it, so the reader can follow every step.

Un coup de feu dans la nuit, dans un château normand, un mort, et un cambrioleur blessé qui disparaît d'un parc clos de murs que la police a cerné. Le détective que personne n'attend s'appelle Isidore Beautrelet : dix-sept ans, lycéen, il remonte jusqu'à la vérité à partir d'un journal et d'un bout de papier couvert de points et de chiffres. Puis le livre s'élargit : ce papier cache le plus vieux secret des rois de France, gardé au vu de tous sur la côte d'Étretat. Le cryptogramme est reproduit tel que Leblanc l'a imprimé, pour que le lecteur suive chaque étape.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — The Shot

Raymonde listened. Twice more the sound came, distinct enough to stand out from the blur of small noises that made up the great silence of the night, yet so faint that she could not have said whether it was near or far, whether it came from inside the walls of the vast château or outside, somewhere among the dark retreats of the park.

She rose quietly. Her window stood ajar; she pushed the casements wide. Moonlight lay over a still landscape of lawns and copses, in which the scattered ruins of the old abbey stood out in tragic silhouette—truncated columns, broken arches, the stubs of porticoes and the shreds of flying buttresses. A little air stirred at the surface of things, slipping through the bare, motionless branches of the trees but troubling the small new leaves of the shrubbery.

And then, suddenly, the same sound again… It came from her left and from below her own floor—from the drawing rooms, therefore, which took up the west wing of the château.

Brave and strong as she was, the girl felt the grip of fear. She slipped on her dressing gown and picked up the matches.

“Raymonde… Raymonde…”

A voice as faint as a breath was calling her from the next room, whose door had been left open. She was groping her way towards it when Suzanne, her cousin, came out of that room and collapsed into her arms.

“Raymonde… is it you?… Did you hear?…”

“Yes… so you aren’t asleep either?”

“I think it was the dog that woke me… a long while ago… But he has stopped barking. What time can it be?”

“About four o’clock.”

“Listen… Someone is walking about in the drawing room.”

“There’s no danger, Suzanne, your father is there.”

“But there is danger for him. He sleeps next to the small drawing room.”

“Monsieur Daval is there too…”

“At the other end of the château… How do you expect him to hear anything?”

They hesitated, not knowing what to do. Call out? Shout for help? They did not dare; the mere sound of their own voices seemed a fearful thing. Then Suzanne, who had moved to the window, stifled a cry.

“Look… a man, near the pond.”

A man was indeed walking rapidly away. Under his arm he carried something fairly large, whose nature they could not make out, and which knocked against his leg and hampered his stride. They saw him pass close to the old chapel and make for a small door set in the wall. The door must have been open, for the man vanished all at once, and they never heard the usual grinding of the hinges.

“He came from the drawing room,” whispered Suzanne.

“No—the staircase and the hall would have brought him out much further to the left… Unless…”

The same thought shook them both. They leaned out. Below them a ladder stood against the front of the house, reaching to the first floor. A light glimmered on the stone balcony. And a second man, also carrying something, climbed over that balcony, let himself slide down the ladder and made off by the same way.

Suzanne, terrified and without strength, dropped to her knees, stammering:

“Let’s call out!… Let’s call for help!…”

“Who would come? Your father… And if there are other men and they throw themselves on him?”

“We could warn the servants… your bell rings on their floor.”

“Yes… yes… perhaps, that’s an idea… If only they get here in time!”

Raymonde felt for the electric bell beside her bed and pressed it. A bell shrilled upstairs, and they had the feeling that down below the sound must have been heard quite distinctly.

They waited. The silence grew terrifying, and even the breeze had stopped stirring the leaves of the shrubs.

“I’m frightened… I’m frightened…” Suzanne kept saying.

And suddenly, out of the deep night below them, came the noise of a struggle, a crash of furniture knocked over, shouts, and then—horrible, sinister—a hoarse moan, the death rattle of a creature with its throat cut…

Raymonde sprang towards the door. Suzanne clung desperately to her arm.

“No… don’t leave me… I’m frightened.”

Raymonde pushed her off and rushed out into the corridor, with Suzanne close behind, reeling from wall to wall and crying out. She reached the staircase, stumbled down it step by step, threw herself at the big drawing-room door and stopped dead, rooted on the threshold, while Suzanne sank down beside her. Three paces in front of them stood a man holding a lantern. He swung it towards the two girls, blinding them with light, studied their faces at length, and then, unhurried, with the calmest movements in the world, he picked up his cap, gathered a scrap of paper and two wisps of straw, rubbed out some marks on the carpet, went to the balcony, turned back to the girls, bowed deeply to them, and was gone.

Suzanne was the first to run to the little boudoir that separated the big drawing room from her father’s bedroom. But on the very threshold a dreadful sight terrified her. In the slanting moonlight two motionless bodies could be seen on the floor, lying side by side.

“Father!… Father!… Is it you?… What’s wrong?” she cried wildly, bending over one of them.

After a moment the Comte de Gesvres stirred. In a broken voice he said:

“Don’t be frightened… I’m not hurt… And Daval? Is he alive? The knife?… The knife?…”

Just then two servants arrived with candles. Raymonde threw herself down beside the other body and recognised Jean Daval, the count’s secretary and confidential man. His face already had the pallor of death.

Then she rose, went back into the drawing room, took from a trophy of arms hanging on the wall a gun she knew to be loaded, and stepped out on to the balcony. Certainly no more than fifty or sixty seconds had passed since the man had set foot on the first rung of the ladder. He could not be far off, all the more so as he had taken the precaution of moving the ladder away so that no one could use it. And indeed she soon caught sight of him, skirting the remains of the old cloister. She raised the gun to her shoulder, took calm aim and fired. The man fell.

“That’s done it! That’s done it!” cried one of the servants. “We’ve got that one. I’ll go down.”

“No, Victor, he’s getting up again… Go down the stairs and make for the little door. That’s the only way he can escape.”

Victor hurried off, but before he was even out in the park the man had fallen again. Raymonde called the other servant.

“Albert, do you see him over there? Near the big archway?…”

“Yes, he’s crawling in the grass… he’s done for…”

“Keep watch on him from here.”

“There’s no way he can get off. To the right of the ruins it’s all open lawn…”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
