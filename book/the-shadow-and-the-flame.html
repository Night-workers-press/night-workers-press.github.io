The bookLe livre

Rostand's Act IV ends at Arras with Christian dead; his Act V opens fifteen years later in a convent garden. Between them: a single stage direction. This play lives inside that silence. Cyrano missing from the morning roll, drinking in a Paris tavern, talking to no one by the river — the years in which he lived without Christian, without Roxane, and, as he believed, without hope. An original sequel in English alexandrines.

L'acte IV de Rostand s'achève à Arras, Christian mort ; l'acte V s'ouvre quinze ans plus tard dans un jardin de couvent. Entre les deux : une seule didascalie. Cette pièce habite ce silence. Cyrano absent de l'appel du matin, buvant dans une taverne parisienne, parlant à personne au bord de l'eau — les années qu'il vécut sans Christian, sans Roxane, et, le croyait-il, sans espoir. Une suite originale en alexandrins anglais.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

ACT ONE — THE FLIGHT INTO OBLIVION

A note to the reader and the audience — The first three acts of this play take place in the fifteen-year silence that Rostand leaves between his Act IV (the siege of Arras, Christian's death, 1640) and his Act V (the park of the Dames de la Croix, 1655). It is within that interval that Cyrano lived without Christian, without Roxane, and — so he believed — without hope. Rostand said nothing of it. We imagined the rest.

Scene 1 — The Cadets and Le Bret

A Paris tavern, loud and dark. Greasy candles, pipe smoke, rattling dice. Three CADETS of Gascony drink at a table. LE BRET enters, scanning the room, and spots them.

CADET 1

Ten days! Ten days he's missed the morning roll!

CADET 2

Carbon won't speak of it.

CADET 1

Shrugs off the whole Affair.

CADET 3

They saw him stumbling late at night, between The willows by the river—

CADET 2

Raving, seen Talking to no one—

CADET 1

Like a common fool.

CADET 3

He's not the same.

CADET 2

At Arras he was cruel As any devil! Crossed through cannon flame To carry letters!

CADET 1

And now? He reeks of shame. He drinks in this foul barn.

CADET 3

He seems a valet.

CADET 2

Less than a valet. Valets rise and stay Upon their feet all night and do what's set. Him — lying in the gutter, soaking wet, A bottle clutched —

CADET 1

The man who sang us into war!

CADET 3

Who wrote his verses dodging musket roar!

CADET 2

What's left?

CADET 1

A drunkard —

CADET 3

— staggering —

CADET 1

Who won't So much as show his face where we confront The day.

LE BRET (approaching, cold)

You speak of Cyrano? Before My face? That's bold.

CADET 2

We say what we have seen. No more.

LE BRET

You drink. He drinks. Where lies the difference, then?

CADET 1

We keep our feet. We show up. We stand fast.

LE BRET

On what? Your third jug's courage? That won't last.

CADET 3

It's different. We drink — and then march on again.

CADET 2

With him it's an abyss.

CADET 3

He won't return.

LE BRET

He carries more than you.

CADET 1

What's his concern?

LE BRET (curtly)

The kind that can't be spoken.

CADET 2

Then let him drink At least without the stain upon our name! We're Cadets — Gascons — the world regards our fame —

CADET 1

One of our own face-down beside the brink Drags mud across us all.

LE BRET

A coward? — Tell: The night we sallied out — who carried back Guiscal, wounded, under fire? Was that your act?

CADET 2

No, but —

LE BRET

And who crossed through the lines of hell So we might eat?

(Silence.)

CADET 1 (lower)

We don't say he was craven. We say he's nothing now of what he's been.

CADET 3

It is a grief.

LE BRET (voice thick)

Yes. Grief. But not the one You think.

CADET 2

Then what? Christian? The handsome son — The one who fell?

LE BRET (catching himself, sharp)

I said Nothing.

CADET 2

You're hiding something, Le Bret.

LE BRET

I sheathe A man. That is my right.

(A pause. Le Bret turns away, adjusts his hat.)

CADET 3

Still — someone ought to make him rise.

LE BRET (without turning)

One day. How much upon his tab?

CADET 1

Nine sous, I'd say.

LE BRET

(setting coins on the counter) Here. — Where is he?

THE SERVER

Against the beam, in back. Asleep.

LE BRET

I'll go.

(Le Bret disappears into the smoke. The cadets look at each other in silence. One lifts his glass, hesitates, sets it down.)

Scene 2 — The Dialogue of the Deaf

LE BRET approaches a table at the back of the tavern. CYRANO is slumped against the beam, an empty bottle before him, another — full and corked — which he turns between his fingers. He does not look up.

LE BRET (sitting across from him)

Cyrano.

CYRANO (without looking up, examining the bottle)

Le Bret. What grace. What honour shown. You've come to see if I still hold this throne? I'm here. Take note and leave. We're done.

LE BRET

No. I'll sit.

CYRANO (shrugging)

Then sit. But hold your tongue. My skull Is splitting.

LE BRET

More than just your skull is full Of pain.

CYRANO

A poet and a fraud! Le Bret Prescribes! Come, doctor — tell me how I weigh. What is my temperature? My pulse? My fate? I'm burning to be told.

LE BRET

Six months. Six straight Unbroken months.

CYRANO (lifting his eyes at last, ironic)

You're counting? How amusing. And what's your remedy? A bath? A hymn? A sermon on the virtues of refusing To live? You'd cure me? Please — begin.

LE BRET

Let go.

CYRANO (stiffening — but it's the bottle he addresses, struggling with the cork)

Let go? Just look at how she grips — And with what force! As if she'd never yield! You pull, you twist, and still she keeps it sealed — The tighter that you try, the more she slips Behind her bark, resisting every turn.

LE BRET (believing Cyrano speaks of Roxane — moved)

I know she won't give way. I know you go Back every week. And nothing changes.

CYRANO (tugging at the cork, grunting)

No! She will not yield! She wants to keep within Whatever good she holds — not one bright shard Must surface — locked — sealed tight — as if her sin Were openness — as if each thing she'd guard Becomes a relic, holy, shut beneath The stopper.

LE BRET

Then speak to her. Just speak. That's all I'm asking.

CYRANO (shaking the bottle, turning it over)

If I force too hard, she'll fall Apart — and everything is lost. The brief Kiss of the air upon what she conceals — One instant — then it's gone. Do you know grief? I'd rather go by inches. That's the deal That's left: this waiting, this resistance. One Does not break open what is shut without Inflicting wounds.

LE BRET (shaken — believing he hears a confession)

How long?

CYRANO (with a smile, applying corkscrew technique)

Until she's done. Until she opens — or until I wilt In trying — but not before. You don't — give — up — Like — this —

The cork gives way with a pop. Wine spurts. Cyrano raises the bottle, triumphant.

CYRANO

HA! You see? She gives herself! One turn Was all it took! Come — let us taste her —

He pours a generous glass, drinks a long draught, holds the bottle out to Le Bret. Silence. Le Bret does not move. He looks at Cyrano. Then the bottle. Then Cyrano. His face changes.

LE BRET (slowly, in a bloodless voice)

The bottle.

CYRANO (drinking, jovial)

Superb! A little green, but honest wine.

LE BRET

You meant the bottle.

CYRANO (raising his glass)

What else could I have meant? You thought I was — what? What was your intent, Le Bret? What were you hearing?

LE BRET (standing)

Nothing. Fine.

A terrible pause.

LE BRET

It's my coin, every night, that fills your void. And I can bear no more.

The Rostand Project

The house's theatrical flagship: Rostand translated, extended and transposed. The Panache Trilogy frames the first English translation of Cyrano de Bergerac that keeps the alexandrine — the twelve-syllable line that carries the play's swagger — between an original prequel and an original sequel. The Project then turns to Chantecler, Rostand's fable of the rooster who believes his song makes the sun rise.

Le navire amiral théâtral de la maison : Rostand traduit, prolongé et transposé. La Trilogie du Panache encadre la première traduction anglaise de Cyrano de Bergerac qui conserve l'alexandrin — ce vers de douze syllabes qui porte tout le panache de la pièce — entre un prologue et une suite originaux. Le Projet aborde ensuite Chantecler, la fable du coq qui croit que son chant fait lever le soleil.
