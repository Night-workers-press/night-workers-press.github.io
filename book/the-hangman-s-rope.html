PART ONE

Chapter One

The collapse of the tunnel was still going on. The vault of the gallery kept shedding huge chunks of rock. The ground went on roaring and shaking, as if the whole passage had been caught up in one of those earthquakes that level cities in the New World.

Vanda had fallen to her knees and was praying. Pauline, clinging to Polyte's neck, kept saying, "At least we'll die together!" Milon bellowed with rage, brandishing his enormous fists and roaring, "Ah, the Fenian scum! The good-for-nothings! The filthy cowards!"

Marmouset, for his part, was watching the master. The master stood calm, upright, head held high, seeming to wait out this cataclysm with the composure of a man who knows he's beyond death's reach.

At last the tremors eased. The roar stopped all at once, and the falling rock went still.

"Forward," Rocambole said.

Vanda straightened, her eyes blazing. "We're saved!" she cried.

"Not yet," he answered. "But let's keep moving."

The tunnel was choked with enormous blocks of stone. Rocambole, pickaxe in hand, went first, forcing a path through the rubble, and his companions followed, reassured by his lead. They pressed on that way for roughly a hundred paces.

Then, all at once, Rocambole stopped. Something bulky in the middle of the gallery had caught his eye. It was a barrel—and the barrel was full of gunpowder. There was no mistaking it: a sulfur-tipped fuse stuck out half a foot from the bunghole.

What was this barrel doing there? Who could have brought it? Did the Fenians know about this passage too?

Marmouset had come up beside him, and like the master, he stared at the keg in astonishment, clearly turning over the same questions.

Vanda and the others hung back a little way behind.

Finally Rocambole spoke. "There's no way the Fenians brought this here."

"Then who, master?" Marmouset asked.

Rocambole circled the barrel, turning it over with his eyes. At last the furrow in his brow smoothed out, and a smile touched his lips.

"My friends," he said, "none of us had been born yet on the day this barrel was carried down here."

"Truly?" Marmouset murmured.

"This powder is two hundred years old," Rocambole went on.

"Is that possible?"

"Look at the barrel—examine it yourself. The wood's rotted through; it crumbles at a touch."

"That's true," Marmouset said.

"Don't touch the fuse," the master added. "It's so dry it would fall to dust."

"Well," said Polyte, who had never studied the subject in any depth, "powder that old, I'd think, has gone stale and harmless."

"You think so?"

Rocambole looked at the Paris street kid with a smile.

"Sure," Polyte said. "Powder that old ought to have lost its kick."

"You're wrong."

"No!"

"It's ten times more violent than fresh powder."

"Blimey! Then we'd best be careful."

"Careful of what?"

"Not setting it off."

"And why's that?"

"Well—after everything that's just happened to us!"

"Leave the powder be and let's keep moving," Rocambole said, and he pressed on.

The tunnel kept sloping downward, the ground falling away underfoot—proof enough that they were drawing steadily closer to the Thames. But suddenly Rocambole stopped again.

"Ah," he said. "This is what I was afraid of."

The tunnel was blocked by a massive slab of rock that had broken from the vault and now sealed the passage like a door.

"We're trapped!" Vanda murmured, seized once more by terror.

Rocambole said nothing. He watched his last hope crumble. The way forward was barred. Turning back would be just as impossible—and doing so would only risk delivering them straight into the hands of the police, who, once the first shock had passed, might within minutes flood into these tunnels that had just been exposed and that no one living had known existed.

"Come on," Rocambole said after a moment's silence. "We win through, or we die here."

"I'm strong enough," Milon said, "but I wouldn't want to be the one shoving that boulder out of the way."

"What if we could tunnel around it?" Marmouset suggested.

"With what? We don't have the tools for it."

"True enough."

"And besides, that's solid rock..."

"Ah," Vanda said again, "I can feel it—we're going to die here."

"Maybe," Rocambole said.

Pauline had thrown herself around Polyte's neck again, and Polyte told her, "Don't cry. It's not hopeless yet. Look how calm that man is."

And indeed, Rocambole seemed at that moment just as composed as if he'd still been sitting in the governor's parlor at Newgate.

"Marmouset," he said at last, "and you, Milon—listen closely."

"Go on, master."

"Don't you hear a low rumble?"

"Yes."

"That's the Thames. It's not far from us now."

"Good," Milon said.

"Now look at the vault of this gallery. It's cut straight into the living rock."

"Yes," Marmouset said, "and it's that same living rock that's stopping us from going any farther."

"Wait," Rocambole said. "Both of you have handled firearms plenty of times."

"Of course," Marmouset said.

"Then follow my reasoning. Let's assume two things. First, that this gallery runs right alongside the Thames."

"That much is certain," Milon said.

"Second, that it's shaped like the barrel of a gun."

"All right," Marmouset said.

"And that the rock blocking our way is the shot."

"And then?" Milon asked.

"We have the powder," Rocambole continued.

"You mean to blow the rock apart?"

"No—to drive it forward."

"Ah!"

"And send it flying down to the end of the gallery, where it will meet the Thames."

"That sounds difficult," Marmouset said.

"Why?"

"Because the powder, with nothing solid behind it to brace against, will have no purchase—and all we'll manage is to bring the gallery down again, this time burying us for good."

"Marmouset's right," Vanda said.

"He's wrong," Rocambole said flatly.

They all exchanged anxious looks. But he, still calm, still cold, turned to Marmouset.

"What you're missing is the resistance, isn't it?"

"Yes—the resistance that powder finds in a gun barrel, which lets it expend its force forward."

"Well, nothing could be simpler to arrange."

"How?"

"Milon, you and I are going to push the barrel ahead of us and lay it against the rock, fuse pointing back, of course."

"And then?" Marmouset asked.

"Then, one by one, we pack in every smaller block choking the gallery."

"And build a kind of wall behind the barrel—is that it, master?" Milon said.

"Exactly. And we'll make that wall six times thicker than the rock we mean to drive out."

"And how many hours do you reckon a job like that will cost us?"

"Six hours, at least."

"But," Vanda said, "before six hours—maybe before even one—we'll be lost!"

"Why do you say that?"

"Because the police and the soldiers will be pouring into these tunnels."

Rocambole shrugged.

"First of all," he said, "the total collapse of the round chamber we left behind us protects us. Second, it's likely they'll believe us dead."

"Six hours is a long stretch of time," Milon said.

Rocambole allowed himself a smile.

"You think that's long?"

"Well..."

"Suppose the wall we need to build is finished."

"Fine."

"And all that's left is to light the barrel."

"And then?"

"We'd still have to wait another seven or eight hours."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
