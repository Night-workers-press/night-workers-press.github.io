The bookLe livre

Damville's false confession, a duel believed fatal, a countess who vanished, a marriage to a woman he never loved — the older Pardaillan relives the whole ruin of his life while his son walks into the same trap from the other end. The love story at the centre of the saga, and the volume in which Zévaco proves he can do tenderness as well as steel.

L'aveu mensonger de Damville, un duel qu'on croyait mortel, une comtesse disparue, un mariage avec une femme jamais aimée : le vieux Pardaillan revit toute la ruine de sa vie tandis que son fils s'engage dans le même piège par l'autre bout. L'histoire d'amour au cœur de la saga, et le volume où Zévaco prouve qu'il sait la tendresse autant que l'acier.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

He was reliving, as in a dream, the scene in which Damville had pretended to confess to him that he had been Jeanne's lover... their duel, in which he had believed he had left him for dead on the ground... and the disappearance of the Comtesse de Piennes, Duchesse de Montmorency.

He was reliving his divorce, his marriage to another woman whom, moreover, he had never loved — the image of the first remaining entirely in his heart.

The years had flowed by; and then, suddenly, a young lord — a young hero, the Chevalier de Pardaillan — had brought him a letter from the woman he had believed gone from his life for ever.

Jeanne de Piennes was alive!

In her letter, she appealed to her former lord and master, she cried out against Damville's treachery, she asked for grace and help for Loïse — her daughter, his daughter, the daughter of the Duc de Montmorency.

A dawn of gratitude and joy had risen in the old duke's soul: he had gone — in vain — to invoke the king's justice against his brother; in vain he had challenged him, knowing that he held Jeanne and her daughter in his power; in vain he had searched Paris to find them — and he was about to fall back into his night of mourning when, once again, the Chevalier de Pardaillan had come to him.

That young man — hero of another age, whose secret he perhaps dimly guessed — had led him by the hand to the mysterious dwelling where everything he had ever loved in the world lay hidden, had brought him face to face with Jeanne de Piennes, the first Duchesse de Montmorency.

The hour so long hoped for, after seventeen years of tears and mourning, had struck at last.

At last he had found again everything he had cherished — everything that had been the joy of his heart, the marrow of his bones, the very essence of his being; in a word, the woman he had loved.

Alas! As a sap too powerful splits the bud, happiness had split the mind of the woman who had been his.

How did he find her?

Mad?...

Jeanne de Piennes, in the last days of her martyrdom, when she felt herself mortally struck, lived only by a single thought:

«I must not die before I have secured my daughter's happiness... And what happiness can there be for the poor child as long as she is not under her father's protection!... Yes — find François, even if he still believes me guilty... put his child in his arms... and then die!...»

When she questioned the Chevalier de Pardaillan — when he told her that it was for another than himself to say how her letter had been received by the marshal — Jeanne was from that moment inwardly certain that François had read the letter, and that he knew the truth. And she waited.

When old Pardaillan announced to her that the marshal was there, she showed no surprise.

No commotion stirred her. Only, she murmured:

«This is the hour in which I am going to die!...»

The thought of death never left her now. She neither desired it nor feared it.

In truth, she felt herself dying.

What had broken in her? Why had the return of the beloved provoked in her soul nothing but a sort of devouring flame, instantly extinguished? She did not know.

But something was certainly breaking in her. And she could say to herself: Here is death! Here is the hour of rest!...

She pressed Loïse convulsively in her arms and murmured in her ear a few words that produced some thunderstruck effect on the young girl — for she tried in vain to answer, made a futile effort to follow her mother, and remained as if riveted to the spot, failing, held up by old Pardaillan.

So immense was Jeanne's exhaustion, so morbidly fixed her thoughts, that she did not even notice Loïse's faint.

She set out, thinking:

«O my François, O my Loïse. I am going to see you reunited at last! I am going to be able to die in your arms!...»

She opened the door Pardaillan had pointed out to her — and she saw François de Montmorency.

She tried — she even believed she was rushing toward him.

She believed she was crying out — a great cry blazing with her happiness.

And all that movement of her thoughts was reduced all at once to the word she believed she spoke:

«Farewell... I am dying...»

Then there was nothing left in her.

Only it was not her body that died...

Her mind alone was annihilated in madness: this woman who had endured so many griefs, who had held her own against such frightful catastrophes; this admirable mother, sustained through her ordeal by nothing but the fixed idea of saving her child; this unhappy creature, finally, gave way — ceased to resist — from the instant she believed her daughter saved, in safety. The madness that had no doubt been lying in wait for her for years fell upon her.

Seventeen years and more of misfortune had not been able to lay her low.

One second of joy killed her.

But by a consoling mercy of the fate that had hounded her — if indeed there are consolations in these atrocious dramas of the human mind — by a kind of pity on the part of destiny, Jeanne's madness was carrying her back to the first years of her radiant youth, of her pure love, to those dear landscapes of Margency where she had loved so deeply...

Poor Jeanne! Poor little fairy of the flowers!

History — unjust — has granted you only a few arid words. For the dreamer who loves to venture with hesitant step into the dark annals of the past, who searches trembling among the heap of ruins for the humble flower that lived, that loved, that suffered — you remain a pure symbol of human suffering; and we, who have just traced your anguish, salute with a moved remembrance your sweet and noble face.

✦ ✦ ✦

When the Marshal de Montmorency came back to himself, he raised himself on one knee, and casting through the room the astonished look of a man who believes he is waking from a dream, he saw Jeanne seated in an armchair — smiling, her expression calm — but, alas, her eyes without life.

A young girl kneeling before her, her head hidden in the madwoman's lap, was sobbing without sound.

François rose and drew close, staggering, to that group so graceful and so melancholy.

He bent toward the young girl and touched her gently on the shoulder.

Loïse raised her head.

The marshal took her by both hands, brought her to her feet — without her mother attempting to hold her back — and contemplated her with avidity.

He recognised her at once.

Loïse was the living image of her mother.

Or rather — she was the beginning of Jeanne, as he had seen and loved her at Margency.

«My daughter!» he stammered.

Loïse, trembling all over with sobs, let herself fall into the marshal's arms; and for the first time in her life, with an inexpressible rapture mingled with an infinite sweetness, she pronounced the word her lips had never before spoken...

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
