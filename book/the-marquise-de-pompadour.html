From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I

We Shall Go to the Woods No More...

⁂

Luminous and clear, that October afternoon of 1744 seemed a festival of the sky, with its flights of birds along the hedgerows, its light white clouds sailing through the bluish immensity, its pretty shimmer of golden rays in the pure air where perfumes and the shivers of autumn swayed together.

On the path of moss and leaves that led from the Ermitage to Versailles — from humble cottages to the majestic colossus of stone — a horseman was coming along at a walk, reins floating at the whim of his spirited, supple chestnut.

His hat jauntily tilted to one side over his catogan, his fine rapier at his mount's flank, slender, elegant, very young — barely twenty — his face stamped with careless audacity, his lip mischievous and his eye ardent, he smiled at the sun which, beyond the empurpled boughs, was sinking toward horizons of silky azure; he smiled at the beautiful forest clad in its autumnal magnificence; he smiled at the comely girl who passed, at the peasant who hummed a tune; he smiled at himself, at life, at his dreams...

Before him, a thousand paces ahead, a man on foot was trudging along, his blackthorn staff in hand.

The man was dusty, ragged. He had been walking since morning, coming from who knows where — from very far, no doubt — going perhaps toward formidable destinies...

Near the pond, the walker stopped suddenly...

There before his eyes, in the radiance of the clearing, in the magnificent setting of that corner of the forest, was a vision of charm and grace:

A young girl... an exquisite marvel... slender, lithe, harmonious, complexion of pearl and rose, opulent cloudy tresses...

supremely pretty in her pannier gown of rose satin brocaded with pink flowerets, the great bouquet of roses pinned at her bodice... a living pastel...

She was laughing out loud, leaning toward a dozen little girls who, aprons askew, tousled little faces, surrounded her, boisterous, spirited... and she was saying:

— Oh! You insatiable imps! Already the dancing devil has hold of you! What, mesdemoiselles, you want yet another round?...

— Yes, yes... Jeanne, dear Jeanne... another round!...

— So be it! Here is one I composed for you yesterday on my walk.

And while the little ones joined hands, she, in a melodious and penetrating voice, sang this:

Nous n'irons plus au bois, les lauriers sont coupés

La belle que voilà, la lairons-nous danser?

Then, to the so pretty little refrain whose childish vogue a hundred and fifty years had not exhausted, the round, amid crystalline laughter, unfurled itself along the shimmering pond...

Over there, on the leafy, mossy path, the young horseman was coming along unconcernedly...

La lairons-nous danser?

Entrez dans la danse

Voyez comme on danse...

The round, all at once, took fright. The laughter froze on the mischievous lips.

The dusty walker was emerging from his thicket; he was approaching with slow steps and halting, an enigmatic, silent silhouette, near the girl the little ones called Jeanne... dear Jeanne...

Smiling, fearless before this unforeseen apparition, she asked gently:

— What do you want?...

The man awoke from his admiring ecstasy. He stammered:

— Forgive me... excuse me... where is one here?

— You are on the grounds of the Ermitage; here is the clearing, and there the pond; here ends the royal park of Versailles, and there the woods begin...

— The château... is it far?

— That way... do you see? she said, her arm extended in the gesture of a woodland nymph.

In the depths of the underwood, the hunting horn sounded; a pack gave tongue.

— How beautiful she is! murmured the walker...

Excuse me again... can you tell me?... The King... is he at the château?

She stood speechless, turning pale. And pensive, in a breath of reverie, she repeated:

— The King!...

— Yes... Louis XV... do you know if he is at the château?

— No... I do not know... Poor man, how wretched you look... and so weary!

— Weary, yes... and wretched... truly wretched...

— Oh! Wait!... I must bring you luck!

Light as a doe, she dashed away. Twenty paces off, beneath a beech, two women were resting; one fair and frail; the other vigorous, buxom, ruddy-complexioned, who set to crying out:

— Jeanne! Jeanne!... Why run about like that, child? You are dripping... you are ruining your complexion... and you are ruining your hair.

Without answering, Jeanne seized a purse lying on the grass beside the scarves; she drew out a louis and, still running, returned to the walker.

At that moment, the sound of the horn drew nearer, sounding the view and the gone-away.

At that moment too, the young horseman with the fine rapier was entering the clearing, while a huntsman, horn slung across his chest, knife at his belt, was galloping around the pond on his foam-flecked horse...

— Here... take this... said Jeanne, coaxing and gentle.

— I do not beg for alms, the walker answered darkly.

— Oh! she said, her voice moved, do you want to cause me pain?...

The man, fierce, hesitated, trembled...

Then, slowly, his hand opened...

Jeanne slipped in the gold coin!

Then she clapped her hands gaily.

But as the stranger remained motionless and sombre, she went on gravely:

— I believe I might be of use to you... if you would confide your name to me?

The man gave a start, a strange look...

then he murmured:

— My name is François Damiens...

The huntsman, at that instant, arrived upon the group, reined in his horse with a jerk, and, his tone curt, his voice hard, let fall this command:

— Ho there! Clod! You must clear off!...

You too, children!... You too, madame!

Jeanne turned round, measured the huntsman with a moue of exquisite impertinence, and burst into a bright laugh:

— Monsieur, you hold your hunting horn badly; that is a fault, and it would prove to me that you are no gentleman, if proof were needed!

— Madame! growled the huntsman, white with anger.

— Go, monsieur, go and ask M. de Dampierre for a lesson in venery, and any Frenchman you meet for a lesson in courtesy... when that is done, you may return.

She pirouetted on the high heels of her rose satin slippers.

Livid, the huntsman spurred his horse forward. He was about to reach her... to knock her down...

The children screamed. The vagabond clenched his blackthorn staff in his hand. He let out a growl, raised his cudgel... but before it could fall, the huntsman's horse was suddenly backing away...

The young horseman, who had just entered the clearing, had with a furious leap placed himself between the girl and the huntsman, and had seized the bridle, shaking it violently; at the same time, his voice rang out, vibrant:

— By God's death, monsieur, are you mad?...

Chest to chest, the two horses stamped and whinnied... Glare against glare, the two men threatened each other.

— I say! the young stranger continued, so one insults women here, does one!

The huntsman let fly an oath; but, at once calming himself:

— Take care, monsieur, he said with glacial politeness, take care! I am here on duty, which is to clear the path for the hunt...

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
