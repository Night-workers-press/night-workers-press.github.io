The bookLe livre

A clear autumn morning in 1536, the Place de Grève full of Sunday crowds — and between two gibbets, a pyre. For whom? The novel that answers the question runs from that square to the court of Henri II and Catherine de' Medici, and it is Zévaco at his most gothic: prophecy, sorcery, vengeance carried across decades.

Une claire matinée d'automne 1536, la place de Grève pleine d'une foule de dimanche — et, entre deux gibets, un bûcher. Pour qui ? Le roman qui répond mène de cette place à la cour d'Henri II et de Catherine de Médicis, et c'est Zévaco au plus gothique : prophétie, sorcellerie, vengeance portée sur des décennies.

First Chapter

The Sorceress

I

The Lovers

A clear, mild autumn morning in the year 1536. Beneath a sky of delicate satiny blue, the old Paris of François I breathed the very joy of living. On the Place de Grève, all was the sparkling gaiety of a fine Sunday — Paris stretching in the sunlight, laughing... and yet, there, upon that square, in that bright air, between two gibbets, there stood a hideous thing: a pyre.

For whom, that pyre? For whom, those gibbets? The heedless crowd was perhaps about to learn, for here upon his charger came the royal herald, unfolding a parchment and proclaiming in a ringing voice:

"In the King's name!... We, Jérôme Gerlaine, sworn royal herald, mandated by Monseigneur de Croixmart, Grand Provost Judge, do hereby make known to all here present:

"By royal command, the said Baron Gerfaut, Seigneur de Croixmart, shall seek out, seize, and summarily execute all sorcerers, sorceresses, diviners, demoniacs, and agents of Satan who infest the capital of the realm.

"Every loyal inhabitant of this city is bound, on pain of condemnation to the King's galleys, to denounce the said minions of hell; and, in execution of the royal will, Monseigneur de Croixmart has caused the necessary pyres to be erected."

The herald went on to repeat his proclamation further afield. And from mouth to mouth, amid muffled imprecations, ran the name of Croixmart.

At the far end of the Place de Grève, a young man and a young woman passed.

She, slender in her long-pleated gown — one of those fair-tressed maidens such as the Primitives dreamed for their stained-glass Madonnas.

He, one of those beings of unforgettable aspect who seem to bear the visible seal of invisible fates. A strange physiognomy, of tormented beauty. A brow where genius blazed. Dark eyes, now of ineffable gentleness, now of an unearthly brilliance.

Upon a stone bench before the river, they sat with hands entwined. A sort of duenna, who followed them step by step, approached and said with a curtsey:

— Marie, mass is over; it is time to go home.

— Dame Bertrande, one moment, sighed the young woman.

— Must you leave us already! murmured the young man. Marie, beloved Marie, must I then depart Paris — forever, perhaps — without even knowing who you are? You bade me respect the mystery in which you shroud yourself, and I have obeyed... And yet I must rejoin my father — my god upon this earth. You know it — my father was forced to take refuge in Montpellier. Accused of sorcery, hunted by Croixmart...

— Croixmart! stammered the young woman, gone quite pale.

The young man made a violent gesture... then continued:

— My mother urges me to go, and wonders at my hesitation. She does not know that I have met you!...

— My beloved Renaud! breathed Marie. Tomorrow, you shall know all that you must know of me. For today I mean to consult a woman who will surely guide me, console me in my anguish...

— A woman? mused Renaud. Her mother, no doubt.

— Come, demoiselle, the duenna insisted, it is time...

But Marie raised her great eyes to the young man:

— My Renaud, I love you for your obedience. You were willing to let me remain the unknown. But tomorrow, here, you shall learn why I trembled to tell you who I am. And besides — my name is Marie, and you love me. Your name is Renaud, and I adore you. What more do we need? And when I think of the irresistible force that placed this love in my heart, I feel a kind of vertigo seize me. It was so strange. I was in the street. All at once I felt one of those shivers that one never forgets. I turned round. And I understood that you were exercising some magical power over me...

— Magical? the young man started.

— Then you said to me: "Fear nothing. I wish to hold you by your own will alone. I forbid myself even to follow you. In a moment, I shall no longer know where you are. I do not know who you are. But if you love me, come tomorrow beneath the poplars of the Grève." And you left. And when I returned home, I threw myself upon my knees to pray. But then I understood that it was to you — unknown to me an hour before — that I was speaking, believing I spoke to God.

— Dearest beloved! Renaud shivered.

— And the next day, I went out for mass, resolved to forget you. But it was toward the Seine that I came, and I found myself beneath the poplars, before you. And ever since, each morning, at the hour of mass, this has been my church.

Renaud, pensive, lowered his noble head upon his broad chest.

— I wish to hold you only by your own will. I shall wait...

— Tomorrow, you shall know to whom you must apply for our union!

And a breath of joy rose to the young man's lips. They stood. Their hands clasped. Their mouths murmured:

— Tomorrow — oh! what will tomorrow bring!...

On the Place de Grève, maledictions... Amid archers, a lord of formidable aspect passed.

— Make way for Monseigneur de Croixmart! the captain of the archers cried roughly.

Marie had gone white as lilies. Renaud's fists were clenched. And already the silhouette of Baron Gerfaut, Seigneur de Croixmart, Grand Provost Judge, was fading from view.

II

The Denunciation

Renaud took his leave. Marie crossed the square, turning her back on the dwelling the duenna pointed out. And she asked:

— Where does this woman live — the one who knows the future and the past?

— Lord above! Do you mean to enter a sorceress's house?

— To whom can I turn? sighed Marie. I have no mother. And I do not know whether tomorrow I shall dare tell Renaud... Ah! Those cries of malediction! And what a blazing look of hatred he wore! Did you not tell me this woman gives precious counsel?

— Her advice has served many a townswoman well, and she is so charitable to the poor that they call her the Good Providence.

— What did you say her name was, Bertrande?

— She is known as the Dame, and none knows who she is. As to her dwelling, it is here, just across from the hôtel de—

— Silence! Marie interrupted in alarm. Wait for me.

Already she had knocked at the door, and it opened.

The young woman entered. She passed into a hall adorned with fine carved furniture. The mistress of the house came forward. She might have been fifty. Beneath her silver hair, her face had remained young. Her features bore the stamp of an inexpressible dignity. In her bearing was revealed the serenity of intrepid souls.

She bade Marie sit, and in a gentle voice:

— Tell me what grief oppresses you. If I can help or comfort you, I shall do so with all my heart.

— Yes, murmured Marie, already your voice soothes me and lulls my fears. Here, then, is the cause of my alarm...

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
