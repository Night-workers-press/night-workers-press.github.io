From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter Thirty-eight

Victor de Passe-Croix was twenty, the age of naïve passions.

At twenty, the woman you love becomes an angel, and once she's ascended to that status, you tell her everything.

By the time they reached Nantes, the Countess d'Estournelle knew the entire Sologne story down to the last detail, from Victor's encounter with Albert Morel to his sister's descent into madness.

Victor had taken Olympe, that is to say Émeraude, for a woman of high society.

The two women checked into the Hôtel de la Marine, on the quay.

Victor, for propriety's sake, took a room in a lodging house nearby. The countess put him at ease, though, by telling him he could come dine at the Hôtel de la Marine every day.

That evening, once they'd shut themselves in their room, since they'd taken a shared suite, the two women talked in low voices.

"My dear," said Émeraude, "it seems to me the time has come for you to share a few confidences with me."

"I'm quite ready to," the countess replied. "You should know, then, that the young man we're going to see on Belle-Île, this Andrewitsch who's a prisoner there, is someone I take a great deal of interest in."

There was irony in the countess's tone.

"Yes," said Émeraude, "I understand that much. What I'd like to know is where this sympathy of yours comes from."

"Well," the countess replied, "imagine that this young man, who calls himself Andrewitsch and is the son of an actual Cossack, has the nerve to claim a different origin altogether."

"Oh?"

"He claims to be the grandson of Baroness René, whose estate my husband and I are set to inherit."

A smile crossed Émeraude's lips, mysterious and mocking, which drew this remark from the countess:

"I can see you've understood me perfectly, my dear. You know very well that this boy, on Belle-Île, is far too close to Paris for comfort."

"No doubt. But do you actually have a way of getting rid of him?"

The Countess d'Estournelle fixed her friend with a steady look.

"I'll find one," she said.

And rising from her seat by the fire, she went to stand before a mirror and studied herself, smiling.

"Word of honor," she said, "I'm still beautiful, and I can certainly turn the head of a boy of twenty!"

"Well," said Émeraude, "Monsieur Victor de Passe-Croix is proof enough of that, it seems to me!"

The countess put a finger to her lips.

"Hush," she said. "I do believe that's the very method I was looking for."

"Really?"

"Of course. At this very moment, Victor is madly in love with me. One wave of my hand and he'd throw himself down a well."

"Which didn't stop him leaving the two of us to go chase after his naval officer, who..."

Madame d'Estournelle cut her friend off.

"Listen carefully," she said. "Suppose we're on Belle-Île..."

"But we will be, tomorrow evening. The steamers make the crossing quickly."

"True. So, suppose we're on Belle-Île."

"All right."

"We settle into a little rented house by the sea."

"Wonderful."

"And we receive this young Andrewitsch there."

"Then what?"

"Andrewitsch falls in love with me..."

"That could happen."

"Victor is also in love with me."

"That's already happened."

"The two young men fight a duel..."

Émeraude stared hard at the Countess d'Estournelle.

"There," she said, "I can see you're still my old Topaze underneath it all. Fine, I follow you. But..."

"Oh," said the countess. "Do you have some small objection to raise?"

"A rather large one."

"Let's hear it."

Émeraude rose just as the countess had, and, like her, stood before the mirror.

"How do you find me?" she asked.

"Always pretty enough to eat."

"Really?"

"Word of honor."

"Well then, suppose..."

"What?"

"That this young Andrewitsch, instead of falling for you, falls for me..."

"That would upset my plans," the countess replied. "But..."

That "but" was magnificent. It meant: I am ten times more beautiful, ten times more seductive than you! Just as Émeraude was no doubt about to answer back, there came a knock at the door of the little sitting room where the two women were waiting for supper.

"There's my Amadis," the countess murmured.

It was indeed Victor de Passe-Croix, fresh from combing the town.

The young man was pale, but his eyes shone with a feverish joy.

"I've found him!" he said, coming in and kissing the hand the countess held out to him.

"Oh?" she said, all curiosity. "And did you get his secret out of him?"

"Not yet."

Madame d'Estournelle fixed him with a clear, searching look. Victor went on.

"He's promised to explain himself tomorrow."

"Ah."

"Oh, don't worry, madame, I'll make sure he does."

"Meaning," said the countess, "that you challenged him, and you're fighting him tomorrow morning?"

Victor blushed and fell silent.

"You'll kill him, or he'll kill you... and either way you'll learn nothing."

The young man gave a start.

"Whereas I," the countess went on coolly, "if I were to get involved..."

"Yes?"

"I'd know, this very evening, the names of the men who treated you so shamefully."

Victor stifled a cry and looked at Madame d'Estournelle with something like awe.

"But for that," she said, "I first need some information about your officer. Where is he staying?"

"Aboard the steamer the Saumon, which he commands."

"Ah, he commands a dispatch boat?"

"Yes, madame. It runs the mail service between Nantes and Belle-Île."

The countess gave a start, though her face stayed perfectly composed. Victor added:

"He even has a rather unusual crew, from what I hear. His sailors have been mixed in with Russian prisoners."

The countess and Émeraude exchanged a quick, furtive glance.

"What's your officer's name?" Madame d'Estournelle asked.

"Monsieur de Fromentin."

"Fromentin!" Émeraude exclaimed. "A lieutenant in the navy?"

"Yes, madame."

"I know him."

"Oh?"

"And," Émeraude added with a smile, "whatever he wouldn't tell you, he'll tell me."

"But madame," Victor pointed out, "keep in mind I've already challenged him."

"Bah, I'll straighten that out, don't worry."

Then, turning to the countess, Émeraude went on.

"If you'll take my advice, my dear, we'll go see Monsieur de Fromentin this very instant. We need to stop this reckless boy," she added, smiling at Victor, "from getting his head blown off tomorrow morning."

The countess fixed Victor with the look of a woman certain she is loved.

"I'm making you my prisoner," she said, "and I order you to remain confined here until we return."

"I'll obey you, madame," the young man replied, "but bear in mind that if Monsieur de Fromentin won't give you the names of these men, I'll have to fight him tomorrow regardless."

"Don't worry," Émeraude replied. "We'll learn everything."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
