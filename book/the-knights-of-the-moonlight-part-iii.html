Two leagues south of the Loire at Orléans the country turns sandy and arid, dotted with stunted pines. This is the Sologne — unhealthy, feverish, monotonous, and carrying a deep melancholy poetry all its own. Red turrets of a brick manor glimpsed through the woods; a household nobody visits. The saga's most atmospheric stretch.

C'est la Sologne — malsaine, fiévreuse, monotone, et porteuse d'une profonde poésie mélancolique. Les tourelles rouges d'un petit manoir de brique aperçues à travers bois ; une maison où personne ne vient. Le passage le plus atmosphérique de la saga.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter One

A traveler crossing the Loire at Orléans has barely gone two leagues south before running into a sandy, arid country dotted with stunted pines. This is the Sologne.

The Sologne is an unhealthy region, feverish and monotonous, but its overall look carries a deep melancholy and an undeniable poetry.

Now and then, from the roadside, you glimpse the red turrets of a small brick manor lost in the middle of the woods.

Sometimes, in the morning, as the sun comes up, you hear a hunting horn ring out, and you see an eager pack of great Poitou hounds go streaming past.

In the evening, through the little stands of fir, the reddish glow of a charcoal-burner's fire flickers, and somewhere nearby a lost hound bays for its master.

To the north lies Orléans, a somewhat monotonous town perhaps, but at bottom the best country in the world.

To the east lies Vierzon, the capital of blacksmiths, the anvil that never sleeps, day or night.

To the west lies Chambord, that beautiful residence, the palace ringed by great woods; a little farther on, Blois, a refined and courteous town that still remembers its illustrious guests.

Then, to the south, comes the Berry, celebrated by George Sand — the Berry, land of legends and dense forests.

Between La Motte-Beuvron and Nouan, the countryside is entirely covered in woodland. In the middle of these woods, about five kilometers from the railway line, stands a handsome house dating from the last century, built, like every structure in the region, of red brick.

Is it a château?

You might think so, seeing the two hexagonal turrets flanking its south face, and counting the hundreds of old trees that form a park a square league around it.

And yet, locally, no one calls it "the château" — people simply refer to the place as la Martinière.

La Martinière belonged, at the time of the 1789 revolution, to a tax farmer named Martin. Hence the name.

Monsieur Martin died at the start of the Empire, and his Sologne estate was bought by a certain Bernard.

This Bernard was a fat oaf who'd made his fortune in the cloth and wool trade. Full of foolishness and vanity, he had "Château de la Martinière" painted in gold letters on the gate of his park. But locally, people went right on calling it simply la Martinière.

Master Bernard, who had married his only son to a tall, thin, dry, disagreeable woman, wanted to play the great lord. He banned hunting on his land, showed no mercy to poachers, and tried to cultivate his neighbors.

The poachers went to prison, but the neighbors shut their doors in his face.

His little lordship was short-lived, in any case; the Restoration arrived. Master Bernard got caught up in two bankruptcies and was ruined, to the applause of the neighborhood, which had long been irritated by the ridiculous extravagance of this old traveling salesman.

A gentleman returning from exile, the Baron de Passe-Croix, father-in-law of General the Marquis de Morfontaine, then bought la Martinière, lived there until his death, and left it to his son — that same Baron de Passe-Croix who would become one of the murderers, first of the Comte de Main-Hardye, and then of the unfortunate Diane de Morfontaine.

Now, in November of 184-, the baron was staying at la Martinière, following the English fashion of spending part of the winter in the country.

Monsieur de Passe-Croix was then about forty-two years old.

The baroness, his wife, was nearing her thirty-sixth year.

Their marriage had produced two children: a son who was to graduate from Saint-Cyr the following year, and a daughter of sixteen, as beautiful as her mother had once been, named Flavie.

So it was that one evening in October 184-, at nightfall, the household at la Martinière heard, a quarter of a league from the house, a hunting horn sound out vigorously.

Three people were gathered in the drawing room at that moment: Monsieur and Madame de Passe-Croix and their daughter.

Madame de Passe-Croix, seated at her embroidery frame, kept pausing in her work to steal glances at her daughter.

The baron, sunk into an armchair by the fire, was reading his newspaper.

As for Flavie, seated across from her father, she sat with her eyes lowered, seemingly lost in deep thought.

The sound of the horn made all three of them start.

"Oh," said Monsieur de Passe-Croix. "Could Victor already be back?"

"That seems unlikely," the baroness replied.

"Victor left this morning for Les Rigoles, where he means to hunt for a week," Flavie observed.

"And yet," Monsieur de Passe-Croix went on, "I'm not mistaken — that's certainly the sound of his horn. No one else around here blows it quite so vigorously."

Madame de Passe-Croix rose and went to open the window. Then she leaned out.

"You were wrong, monsieur," said the baroness. "I don't hear anything now. It's probably the Messieurs de Cardassol."

"Actually, that's possible," said the baron. "Those petty squires poach like common peasants. While they ban hunting on their own land, they don't hold back on anyone else's, and they're forever crossing our grounds."

The people Monsieur de Passe-Croix was referring to, who are destined to play a part in our story, deserve a few lines to sketch them out.

The Messieurs Brûlé de Cardassol were small woodland landowners propping up a modest nobility on modest income, forever scraping by, working their meager estate themselves, dealing in bad faith in every transaction, swearing they owed nothing to a creditor who trusted their word, while at the same time claiming what wasn't owed to them whenever they could catch a court off its guard.

In the Sologne, where the nobility is generally well regarded, liked, and respected, people commonly said: "As dishonest as a Cardassol."

These charming petty squires, five of them in all, allowed themselves the luxury of a single gamekeeper, who combined those noble duties with those of coachman, farmhand, and gardener. They kept one hunting horse, three half-breed hounds, and a pointer. Since their own woods were small, they poached on other people's land. In summer, they fed their laborers and day workers on roe deer shot at the watering hole.

In winter, they went off to cut a figure in the nearby town, parading through the sub-prefecture's drawing rooms with wives who were fairly plain and had been married up who knew where.

Monsieur de Passe-Croix and the Cardassols kept up a relationship of the yearly-visit sort. They exchanged a call on New Year's Day and informed each other of marriages and births.

Victor de Passe-Croix, the young Saint-Cyr cadet, and the youngest Cardassol, named Octave, had known each other at school, but they had never become close, for the excellent reason that Victor was open and straightforward, while Octave de Cardassol was sly, selfish, a liar, and showed every sign of growing into a miser.

At school, Victor and Octave had come to blows; later, at the preparatory academy where they crossed paths again, they fought a bout with buttoned foils. Cardassol was wounded. We shall see later that he never forgave it.

Such were Monsieur de Passe-Croix's closest neighbors.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris.
