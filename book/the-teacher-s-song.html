The bookLe livre

Rostand's rooster believed his song made the sun rise. Here he is a teacher, and the staff room is the farmyard: Monday morning, marked papers, union posters, a dead pot plant nobody has removed. Then the machine arrives that can write the essays, mark them, and explain them better. When the sun comes up without him, what was the song for? A verse transposition of Chantecler into the present tense of the classroom.

Le coq de Rostand croyait que son chant faisait lever le soleil. Ici c'est un professeur, et la salle des profs est la basse-cour : lundi matin, copies à rendre, affiches syndicales, une plante morte que personne n'enlève. Puis arrive la machine qui sait écrire les dissertations, les corriger et les expliquer mieux. Quand le soleil se lève sans lui, à quoi servait le chant ? Une transposition en vers de Chantecler au présent de la salle de classe.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Scene I

THE STAFF ROOM. A large table in the centre, cluttered with marked papers, mugs, paperclips, and orphaned pens. Metal pigeonholes along the walls, some locked, some gaping. A coffee machine in one corner (Bernadette, the photocopier, is in the next room — you can hear her). A microwave at the back. Union posters pinned to the wall: "NO TO THE REFORM,"

"YES TO DIALOGUE." A giant, illegible timetable at the rear. Pallid strip lights. A green plant, long dead, that no one has removed.

Enter successively: LA PINTADE (Mme Durocher); MME VERDIER; M. GARCIA; MME KLEIN. Then other teachers.

LA PINTADE (bursting in, slamming her bag down on the table.)

Monday! The day of death, of papers, woe!

The day the alarm clock deals its cruelest blow

Announcing one more blow — and then a third,

A fourth — until the only living word

Is Monday. Always Monday. Monday's dread:

These walls again, these neon tubes of lead,

This corridor, these papers marked but never

Truly understood, these students, dull as ever,

Trudging in with torpor in their eyes —

Monday, my friends. The day our good

Lord dies

Of boredom — or sleeps in. The day God stirred,

Looked down at Monday morning, and demurred.

MME VERDIER (entering, plain mug in hand.)

Good morning. Thirty papers. Linear functions.

LA PINTADE

Maths! At least you have an answer waiting there!

M. GARCIA (entering in tracksuit.)

Morning, all!

MME KLEIN (entering, book under her arm, sitting without a word in a corner.)

LA PINTADE

Klein! Jane Austen?

MME KLEIN

The only author who has never let me down.

PATRICE enters, personal mug in hand, sits at the end of the table, opens a newspaper.

LA PINTADE

Patrice! At last! A word! That's more than nothing.

PATRICE

Good morning.

LA PINTADE

One word! "Good morning"! That's the lot?

PATRICE

It is enough.

The coffee — still as vile?

LA PINTADE

Like yesterday.

PATRICE

Like it will be tomorrow. That's the one

True constant here — the only thing that's done

The same way, every day: the coffee's bad,

The strip light flickers, and the grumbling's had

By all. There's something reassuring in

Consistent

mediocrity. A din

Of sameness.

M. GARCIA

Comforting!

PATRICE

No. Reassuring. Not the same. "Comforting"

Implies a need for comfort — something sore.

"Reassuring" implies you need, and nothing more,

That nothing happens. Here, Garcia —

Nothing happens. Nothing ever will. So: there you are.

LA PINTADE rummages in her pigeonhole and pulls out a white envelope.

LA PINTADE

Here. A letter in our pigeonholes.

Has everyone got one?

MME VERDIER

I have.

M. GARCIA

Me too.

PATRICE (peeling open his envelope without interest.)

Same here. Let's see.

He reads.

"Dear Sir or Madam. In my capacity

As parent representative, and on behalf

Of a collective of concerned families,

I take the liberty..."

LA PINTADE

It's Beaumont!

PATRICE

The father of Léna. The delegate. He signs

With a title, like a man of consequence

Who holds a function. The delegate.

But pray — Who delegated him?

He delegated — Beaumont.

LA PINTADE

Read on!

PATRICE

Oh.

"...of alerting you to certain pedagogical

Practices ill-suited to the current framework,

And notably the persistence of so-called

'Open' methods that place students in a state

Of cognitive distress. Several families report

Sleep disturbances in their children following

The sessions of

M. S. We ask

the teaching staff

To kindly align..."

MME VERDIER

Align.

PATRICE

The word is in the letter. Align. How nice.

It's military. Harks back to a time when schools

Taught children how to stand in rows. Align, then:

"...align their practices with those approved

By the Board in the context of the ÉCLORE

Deployment. Yours sincerely,

M. Beaumont,

Parent

Representative."

M. GARCIA

It's Solères he's after.

PATRICE

Of course. The whole school knows who

"M. S." is round here. Our only

S. who asks unanswerable questions.

The capital says all: big

S for stigma.

MME KLEIN (without looking up.)

Disgraceful.

LA PINTADE

It's clever. Letter sent to every teacher,

But with one name squarely in the crosshairs.

Not

A formal complaint, you see. An "alert."

Much smarter. You can't contest an alert —

you just digest it.

PATRICE

The perfect poison. It goes down with ease —

No taste, no smell. You swallow by degrees.

And in a few weeks' time, the whole staff room

Will wonder why they find Martin a gloom,

A bit of a bore, a bit of a lost cause —

Without quite knowing why. Without the pause

To ask who planted it. That's how it works.

M. GARCIA

Do we show it to him?

PATRICE (a beat.)

No. Not yet. Not this morning.

You don't kill a man before his first coffee.

A silence. The door opens timidly.

LUCAS enters, new briefcase, searching eyes, clumsy tie.

LUCAS

Excuse me — is this the staff —

LA PINTADE

Shipwreck survivors' lounge? Yes.

Welcome to the raft. Grab a mug. Start swimming.

LUCAS

I'm the new trainee — in French.

Modern French. Lucas. Lucas

Martel.

LA PINTADE

A trainee! Look at him!

He's pink, he's tender, he's fresh as a new penny!

The way we all were, once, before the blows began.

LUCAS

It's my first posting. My heart is racing.

LA PINTADE

Ah, the beautiful élan!

Just wait until you see the payslip, dear.

The dream negotiates at fourteen hundred clear —

Net, monthly. After a master's and a national exam.

Fourteen hundred. Less than a starting plumber. Bam.

PATRICE (from his corner, without looking up.)

The only real salary you'll ever see

Is one look from a student — just trust me —

When something clicks. Not something from the lesson.

Something else. Something larger. A concession

By the world, that for a second, thought occurred.

Remember that. The rest is just a word.

LUCAS

But surely money's not the point...

PATRICE

"The point" — the only alibi the State

Has found to justify underpaying you.

The only word in the language that can make

Injustice sound like a vocation. Bravo.

LUCAS

I've been assigned to

M. Solères for my mentorship.

I understand he's rather...

LA PINTADE

Particular?

LUCAS

I was told: exceptional.

PATRICE

People say both. Depending

on the corridor, the day, the rain.

LUCAS

Where do you sit? Is this chair free?

LA PINTADE

But not that chair. That's Solères's.

LUCAS

Solères?

PATRICE

The prophet. He who comes before the world

And locks himself inside his room — last door,

End of the corridor — the way one enters prayer:

With the same lopsided faith, the same sick dread

That God's gone somewhere else.

A pause.

Once — tell him, Christine.

LA PINTADE

I peered through his door one morning. Left ajar.

A Tuesday, quarter past six. There he was, standing,

Facing the wall, repeating the same question —

The same one, three or four times, like a prayer.

With little changes — one word swapped, a shift

In tone.

Then silence. Then he smiled. And I pulled the door shut.

I had surprised

Someone at worship.

PATRICE

There it is. The morning ritual.

The priest at his devotions. Except the temple

Is a room that reeks of disinfectant.

And the faith — his faith — is that a question asked

The Rostand Project

The house's theatrical flagship: Rostand translated, extended and transposed. The Panache Trilogy frames the first English translation of Cyrano de Bergerac that keeps the alexandrine — the twelve-syllable line that carries the play's swagger — between an original prequel and an original sequel. The Project then turns to Chantecler, Rostand's fable of the rooster who believes his song makes the sun rise.

Le navire amiral théâtral de la maison : Rostand traduit, prolongé et transposé. La Trilogie du Panache encadre la première traduction anglaise de Cyrano de Bergerac qui conserve l'alexandrin — ce vers de douze syllabes qui porte tout le panache de la pièce — entre un prologue et une suite originaux. Le Projet aborde ensuite Chantecler, la fable du coq qui croit que son chant fait lever le soleil.
