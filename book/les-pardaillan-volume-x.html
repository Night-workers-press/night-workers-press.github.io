The bookLe livre

Fifty archers hold the Halles end of the rue de la Cossonnerie under the provost's own command; an equal company blocks the rue Saint-Denis. The street has been sealed. Ten volumes, some three thousand pages and five narrative cycles across half a century of French history end here — and with this volume the first complete English translation of Les Pardaillan is finished.

Cinquante archers tiennent la rue de la Cossonnerie du côté des Halles, sous le commandement du prévôt en personne ; une compagnie aussi nombreuse barre la rue Saint-Denis. La rue est bouclée. Dix volumes, quelque trois mille pages et cinq cycles narratifs sur un demi-siècle d'histoire de France s'achèvent ici — et avec ce volume s'achève la première traduction anglaise intégrale des Pardaillan.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

The Rue de la Cossonnerie ran from the Rue Saint-Denis to the Rue du Marché-aux-Poirées, deep in the heart of Les Halles. On the Halles side, a troop of archers held the ground — Landry Coquenard had not exaggerated when he said there were fifty of them at least, under the personal command of the provost. On the Rue Saint-Denis side, an equally numerous and formidable company stretched right and left along the street, blocking all passage. Traffic had been cut off at that point and throughout the whole of the Rue de la Cossonnerie. And naturally, on both sides — the Rue du Marché-aux-Poirées and the Rue Saint-Denis alike — a dense crowd of idlers, mad with curiosity, was crushed up behind the archers, trading quips and enormous jokes, and, without knowing in the least who or what was at issue, instinctively siding with force, already letting loose a low rumble of threats.

That was not all.

Between the two companies of archers a wide open space had been left clear.

And that space was occupied by Concini and his usual retinue. There were a good twenty of them, headed by their captain Rospignac and his lieutenants: Roquetaille, Longval, Eynaus, and Louvignac. In addition, some thirty of those sinister-looking individuals whose presence in the street Pardaillan had not noticed had massed behind the retainers, to whom they answered. Not counting Concini and the officers, there were at least fifty armed men.

D'Albaran stood beside Concini, with only his ordinary troop of ten men about him. He confined himself to watching and appeared to have left Concini in charge of operations.

In all, nearly two hundred men were besieging the house. For that is what it seemed about to become: a regular siege.

Needless to say, every window giving onto the street stood wide open, and the curious had crowded into them. Those spectators, as stupidly ferocious as the idlers in the street, showed themselves hostile without knowing why.

A strange thing, noticed at once by the three besieged men: no one appeared at the windows of their own house. Every window there remained shut. Pardaillan offered this explanation, which seemed plausible enough:

— They must have cleared all the tenants out of the building.

— Most likely, Valvert agreed.

And he added, without appearing particularly troubled:

— Perhaps they mean to blow us up.

— Or roast us like common swine, Landry Coquenard suggested in a mournful tone.

— Well then, Pardaillan demanded, turning to him, what do you know of it?

— Next to nothing, monsieur, said Landry Coquenard in a woeful voice.

And he gave his account: he had been returning to their lodging. At the Pointe Saint-Eustache he had spotted the provost and his archers coming from the direction of the Croix-du-Trahoir. He had paid them little mind and gone on his way. After a while he noticed they were following behind him in the same direction. And, dull fool that he was, it had not put him on his guard. He reached the Rue de la Cossonnerie and, instinctively looking back, saw that the archers now occupied the Rue du Marché-aux-Poirées, blocking that entrance. That alarmed him vaguely. He went toward the Rue Saint-Denis, and saw yet more archers blocking the way there. He found himself caught between the two companies and began to be afraid — but still suspected no plot.

— Curse me to the consummation of ages! he added, breaking off to rail at himself.

— Continue, said Pardaillan coolly, and be brief.

At that point, ten archers had entered the street and invited the inhabitants, in the friendly tone one knows them for, to bolt their front doors and not stir. Those who said they did not live in the street were summarily told to make themselves scarce, which they did without being told twice. Landry could have withdrawn then too — easily. But at that moment Concini's henchmen arrived, and the sight of them finally revealed to him what was afoot.

— All the more reason to have run, Pardaillan pressed him, fixing him with his sharp gaze. You know full well what your old master has in store for you if he lays hands on you.

— That was my first thought, monsieur. But I said to myself: Monsieur le comte is surely up there. Perhaps he does not know what is happening in the street. He may come down at any moment — and then he is lost. I must warn him. So I went in, monsieur. And you saw how I was just in time: you were walking straight into the wolf's jaws. I confess, monsieur le chevalier, that I was grievously surprised to find you there as well.

The worthy Landry Coquenard had delivered this with the plainest simplicity, apparently quite unaware that he had just accomplished a truly admirable act of heroism.

Odet de Valvert, deeply moved by this proof of devotion, stiffened to hide his emotion. Pardaillan regarded the man a moment in silence, then said in a very gentle voice:

— You are a brave fellow, Landry.

— No, monsieur, Landry answered piteously, I am a coward. A very great coward. I assure you, monsieur, that I never go looking for trouble. And if trouble comes looking for me, I don't hesitate to take to my heels without the least shame, if I can manage it.

— And if you cannot take flight? Pardaillan asked, smiling despite himself.

— Then, monsieur, Landry said with an air of ferocious resolution, I defend my skin. And vigorously, I promise you.

And with perfect candour:

— God's belly, I set great store by my skin!

— Well then, Pardaillan concluded coolly, let us do our best to defend our skins, all three of us, since all three are threatened.

He watched from the window a little longer. The archers at both ends of the street stood waiting. Concini and his men before the door were not yet moving; Concini was in animated conference with d'Albaran, who seemed to be nodding his assent.

— What the devil are they plotting? Pardaillan muttered in vexation.

It was above all this ignorance of the enemy's intentions that was agonising. While waiting for some indication to enlighten him, Pardaillan began studying the rooftops, and voiced his conclusion:

— If we are driven to flee that way, we have ninety-nine chances in a hundred of dashing our bones on the pavement below.

— Yes, but we have one chance of getting through, Valvert observed.

— Obviously. If we have no other choice, we must run it.

— Attention! They're entering the house, Landry Coquenard warned.

Indeed, some twenty henchmen were filing in silently and in good order, two by two. Rospignac had had the courage to take their head.

Pardaillan and Valvert left the window. Landry Coquenard continued watching the street.

— If they come up here, said Pardaillan, thinking it through, this door won't hold a minute.

— We could take our stand on the staircase, Valvert proposed. It is not so wide. Between the two of us we could give them a fine time.

— Without doubt. But there are too many of them. We'd be overwhelmed in the end by sheer numbers. And besides — it's not certain they'll come up here at all. Who says they won't blow us up or set fire to the house, as you said just now? Pardaillan observed.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
