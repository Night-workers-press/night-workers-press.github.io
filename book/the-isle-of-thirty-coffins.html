The bookLe livre

For fourteen years Véronique d'Hergemont has believed her father and her small son dead. Then, on the door of an abandoned hut beside a Breton road, she sees her own girlhood signature chalked in a hand she knows, and the trail takes her to Sarek: a granite island off Finistère, ringed by thirty reefs, where a prophecy written four centuries ago has begun to come true, item by item, in the year 1917. The darkest book in the canon, and the strangest; Lupin arrives in the last third to take the nightmare apart. The prophecy is translated as verse, because the solution turns on its rhymes.

Depuis quatorze ans, Véronique d'Hergemont croit morts son père et son petit garçon. Puis, sur la porte d'une cabane abandonnée au bord d'une route bretonne, elle voit sa propre signature de jeune fille tracée à la craie d'une main qu'elle connaît, et la piste la mène à Sarek : une île de granit au large du Finistère, cernée de trente récifs, où une prophétie écrite quatre siècles plus tôt commence à s'accomplir, point par point, en l'année 1917. Le livre le plus noir du canon, et le plus étrange ; Lupin arrive au dernier tiers pour démonter le cauchemar. La prophétie est traduite en vers, parce que la solution dépend de ses rimes.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Prologue

The war has brought about such upheavals that very few people today remember what was, a few years ago, the d’Hergemont scandal.

Let us recall the facts in a few lines.

In June 1902, Monsieur Antoine d’Hergemont, whose studies of the megalithic monuments of Brittany are highly thought of, was walking in the Bois with his daughter Véronique when he was set upon by four men and struck in the face with a stick that knocked him down.

After a short struggle, and in spite of her desperate efforts, Véronique, the beautiful Véronique as her friends called her, was carried off and thrown into a car which the onlookers of this very rapid scene saw driving away in the direction of Saint-Cloud.

A simple abduction. The next day the truth was known. Count Alexis Vorski, a young Polish gentleman of a rather bad reputation but of great bearing, who claimed to be of royal blood, was in love with Véronique d’Hergemont and Véronique loved him. Rejected by the father, insulted by him more than once, he had contrived the adventure without Véronique being in the smallest degree a party to it.

Antoine d’Hergemont, who was—certain letters made public bore witness to it—violent, silent, and who by his whimsical temper, his fierce selfishness and his sordid meanness had made his daughter very unhappy, swore openly that he would take the most implacable revenge.

He gave his consent to the marriage, which took place two months later at Nice. But in the following year a series of sensational pieces of news came out. Keeping his word of hatred, Monsieur d’Hergemont carried off, in his turn, the child born of his daughter’s marriage to Vorski, and at Villefranche took passage on a small pleasure yacht he had just bought.

The sea was heavy. The yacht went down in sight of the Italian coast. The four sailors who manned her were picked up by a boat. According to their evidence, Monsieur d’Hergemont and the child had disappeared in the waves.

When Véronique had gathered the proof of their death, she went into a convent of Carmelites.

* * *

Such are the facts. Fourteen years later they were to bring on the most appalling and the most extraordinary of adventures. A true adventure, all the same, though certain details take on, at first sight, an appearance that is in some sort fabulous. But the war has complicated existence to such a degree that events happening outside it, like those which are about to be told, borrow from the great drama something abnormal, illogical and, at moments, miraculous. It needs the whole blazing light of truth to give these events the mark of a reality which is, when all is said, simple enough…

Chapter I — The Abandoned Cabin

The picturesque village of Le Faouët, in the very heart of Brittany, saw arriving by carriage, one morning in May, a lady whose ample grey coat and the thick veil wrapped about her face did not prevent one from making out her great beauty and her perfect grace.

This lady lunched quickly at the principal inn. Then, about noon, she asked the landlord to keep her bag for her, asked a few questions about the district, and, crossing the village, set off into the country.

Almost at once two roads offered themselves to her, one leading to Quimperlé, the other to Quimper. She chose the second, went down into the hollow of a little valley, climbed again and saw, on her right, at the entrance of a by-road, a signpost bearing the words: Locriff, 3 kilometres.

“This is the place,” she said to herself.

Yet, having looked all round her, she was surprised not to find what she was looking for. Had she misunderstood the directions she had been given? There was nobody near her, and nobody as far as one could see to the horizon of the Breton countryside, over the tree-bordered meadows and the rolling of the hills. A small manor house, springing out of the new green of spring, raised not far from the village a grey front in which every window was closed by its shutters. At noon the angelus bells swung in the air. Then came the great silence and the great peace.

So she sat down on the short grass of a bank and drew out of her pocket a letter whose many sheets she unfolded.

The first page bore at the top this heading:

Dutreillis Agency, Consulting Office, Confidential Information, Discretion.

Then, underneath, this address:

“To Madame Véronique, milliner, Besançon.”

She read:

“Madam,

“You cannot imagine with what pleasure I have discharged the twofold task you were good enough to entrust to me by your esteemed letter of this month of May 1917. I have never forgotten the circumstances in which it was possible for me, fourteen years ago, to lend you my effective help, at the time of the painful events that darkened your life. It was I, in fact, who was able to obtain all the certainties relating to the death of your dear and respected father, Monsieur Antoine d’Hergemont, and of your beloved son François—the first victory in a career which was to furnish so many other brilliant ones.

“It was I too, do not forget, who, at your request, and seeing how needful it was to withdraw you from the hatred and, let us say the word, the love of your husband, took the steps necessary for your entry into the convent of the Carmelites. And it was I, finally, who, your retreat in that convent having shown you that the religious life was contrary to your nature, procured you that humble place as a milliner at Besançon, far from the towns in which the years of your childhood and the weeks of your marriage had been passed. You had taste, and the need to work in order to live and in order not to think. You were bound to succeed. You have succeeded.

“And now let us come to the fact, to the double fact that concerns us.

“First of all, the first question. What has become, in the storm, of your husband, the man Alexis Vorski, a Pole by birth according to his papers, and a king’s son according to his own account? I shall be brief. Suspect, and shut up from the beginning of the war in an internment camp near Carpentras, the man Vorski escaped, crossed into Switzerland, came back into France, was arrested, accused of spying and found to be a German. A second time, when a sentence of death was inevitably waiting for him, he escaped, disappeared into the forest of Fontainebleau and, in the end, was stabbed by nobody knows whom.

“I tell you this quite bluntly, madam, knowing what contempt you felt for that creature who had betrayed you abominably, and knowing too that you were aware, from the newspapers, of most of these facts, without having been able, however, to verify their absolute truth.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
