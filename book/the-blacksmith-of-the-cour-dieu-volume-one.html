PROLOGUE

The Monks’ Ward

Chapter One

The Forge at Cour-Dieu

Daybreak was still a long way off. The bell for Matins had just rung, and the monks had gone through to the chapel. The night was cold and clear and brilliant, and the forge was blazing.

While the monks prayed, Dagobert started his day’s work.

There had been plenty of work at the monastery forge lately. Saint Hubert’s day was coming, and the prior was in the habit of inviting every hunting gentleman in the district to the feast, which meant there would be horses to shoe in three days’ time. But what had got Dagobert up an hour earlier than usual was a wrought-iron grille he wanted to finish. Dom Jérôme, the prior, meant to have it fitted inside the monastery buildings to divide one courtyard from another.

You should know that all this happened in the year of grace seventeen hundred and eighty; that Dagobert was the monastery blacksmith; and that the monastery of Cour-Dieu, built in the middle of the forest of Orléans, housed a community of Cistercian monks.

Dagobert, however, was not a monk, nor a novice, nor a lay brother. Dagobert was a layman.

He was a young man of twenty-two, built like Hercules, with a bold, masculine face that was not without its own kind of good looks.

The community was a powerful one, and half a dozen villages bent to its authority. But just as the kingdom of France contained for centuries a kingdom one square mile in size whose lord and master styled himself the King of Yvetot, so the monks had, in the middle of their religious estate, one small enclave that was not theirs. The enclave was Dagobert’s forge.

Dagobert’s father had been called Dagobert, and so had every one of his forefathers. From father to son, from uncle to nephew, the Dagoberts had been the monastery’s blacksmiths, and the Dagoberts married. It went back several hundred years, and the gentlemen of the province were fond of saying: “If age of blood makes a man noble, the Dagoberts are as noble as we are.”

Abbots came and went at Cour-Dieu. The Dagoberts handed down their blacksmith’s hammer from age to age and generation to generation, the way other men hand down a sceptre. It was a hereditary crown standing beside a working monarchy.

At the time our story begins, the last of the Dagoberts — his given name was Jean — had no father, no mother and no brother, and he was unmarried. But he was only twenty-two, and there was time yet for him to marry and carry on his strange little dynasty.

So: the night was cold, the monks were singing Matins, and the blaze of the forge threw its light out across the great trees of the forest that surrounded the monastery on every side. Dagobert hammered and hammered, and it was a wonder to watch him. His hammer rang on the anvil with a cheerful noise, and tore sprays of sparks out of the iron he was beating.

Then another noise cut across the hammer. It was a horse coming at a hard gallop.

Six months at a stretch could go by in which Dagobert saw nothing but a monk’s habit, so his curiosity was natural enough. He put the iron back into the fire, dropped the hammer beside the anvil and went to his door.

He saw a rider coming up from the direction of Sully, going flat out.

Some gentleman off to keep Saint Hubert’s day a long way from here, he thought.

But to his great surprise, when the horse came level with the forge, it stopped.

“You there, friend!” the rider called.

Dagobert came right out, put his hand to his cap, and walked over to the rider, who was by now standing inside the ring of light the forge threw out onto the road.

He was a man with a greying beard, and to Dagobert’s astonishment he had a little girl of nine or ten riding pillion behind him. She looked ready to drop with sleep and exhaustion. The horse’s chest was fringed with foam and looked no better.

“My friend,” the rider said, “is this the monastery of Cour-Dieu?”

“It is, sir.”

“And is Dom Jérôme still prior here?”

“He is indeed.”

The rider gathered the little girl into his arms and dismounted.

“Poor child,” he said, looking down at her with great tenderness. “She’s frozen.”

He carried her into the forge without another thought for his horse, which set about cropping the thin, wretched grass at the edge of the road. Then he set the little girl gently on her feet and moved her close to the fire.

Dagobert looked from the child — she had large blue eyes, full of gentleness, and beautiful fair hair falling over her shoulders in thick curls — to the rider, who seemed to be labouring under some violent feeling.

“My friend,” the man said, “would you ring at the monastery gate for me? I have to see Dom Jérôme. There is no question about it.”

“Sir,” Dagobert answered, “I could ring for an hour and nobody would open.”

“Why not?”

“Because every monk in the place, the porter included, is in the chapel singing Matins.”

“Well?”

“And the Rule forbids them to open the gate.”

“But I have to see Dom Jérôme,” the man said, with a note of real pain in his impatience.

“Matins finish at first light,” Dagobert said. “They’ll open then.”

“That’s impossible.”

Dagobert looked at him.

“Impossible,” he said again. “By the time it’s light I have to be far away from here.”

He looked about fifty. He was dressed like a member of the provincial gentry: a sword at his side, a yellow velvet coat, a red waistcoat, a three-cornered hat trimmed with silver braid, and tall riding boots. All of it was caked in mud and dust, and all of it looked as though it had come a very long way.

The little girl had sat down in front of the fire, on the small log Dagobert used for a seat when he was not working. Her pretty head had gradually tipped over onto her shoulder, and her eyes had closed.

“Poor little lady, look how sleepy she is,” the blacksmith murmured, with that open-handed pity you only find in the young. “You’d do well, sir, to let me carry her upstairs to my bed, where she could sleep properly. I’d put your horse in the stable with a good armful of hay. And you yourself, if a glass of rough wine—”

The gentleman did not answer. He went out at a run and rang at the monastery gate with the energy of a desperate man.

The gate stayed shut. Away in the distance you could hear the deep voices of the monks chanting Matins.

He rang for a quarter of an hour and more, and got nothing. Then he came back into the forge, beaten, and Dagobert saw a hot tear roll slowly down his cheek.

The little girl with the fair hair had fallen asleep.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
