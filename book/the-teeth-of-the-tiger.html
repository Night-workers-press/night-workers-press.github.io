Les Dents du tigre

Two hundred million francs and five heirs (1921)

The bookLe livre

An American millionaire dies in a Paris hotel; a police inspector staggers into the Prefecture, poisoned, and dies before he can name the man who did it; and a will leaves two hundred million francs to whichever of five people is still alive to claim them. They begin to die in the order in which the will would have paid them. Between the money and the killer stands Don Luis Perenna, a decorated hero of the Foreign Legion whom half of Paris suspects of being someone else entirely. At the centre is one of the famous puzzles of French crime fiction: an apple, and the marks of one woman's teeth. The longest book in the canon, with nothing cut.

Un millionnaire américain meurt dans un hôtel parisien ; un inspecteur de police entre en titubant à la Préfecture, empoisonné, et meurt avant d'avoir pu nommer son assassin ; un testament laisse deux cents millions à celle des cinq personnes qui sera encore en vie pour les réclamer. Elles se mettent à mourir dans l'ordre même où le testament les aurait payées. Entre l'argent et l'assassin se dresse don Luis Perenna, héros décoré de la Légion étrangère, que la moitié de Paris soupçonne d'être tout à fait quelqu'un d'autre. Au centre, l'une des énigmes célèbres du roman policier français : une pomme, et la marque des dents d'une femme. Le plus long volume du canon, sans une coupure.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — D’Artagnan, Porthos and Monte Cristo

At half past four, Monsieur Desmalions, the Prefect of Police, not being back yet, his private secretary laid out on the desk a packet of letters and reports he had annotated, rang, and said to the messenger who came in by the main door:

“The Prefect has sent for a number of people to be here at five o’clock; these are their names. You will keep them waiting separately, so that they can’t speak to one another, and you will bring me their cards.”

The messenger went out. The secretary was making for the small door that opened on his own office when the main door was thrown open again and let through a man who stopped and leaned unsteadily against the back of an armchair.

“Why,” said the secretary, “is that you, Vérot? But what’s the matter? What’s wrong with you?”

Inspector Vérot was a heavily built man, powerful in the shoulders, high-coloured. Some violent emotion must have upset him, for his face, streaked with little threads of blood and habitually flushed, looked almost pale.

“Nothing, sir.”

“Nonsense, you don’t look well at all… You’re livid… And these drops of sweat…”

Inspector Vérot wiped his forehead and, pulling himself together:

“A little tiredness… I have overworked these last few days… I wanted at all costs to clear up a case the Prefect gave me… All the same, it’s queer, what I feel.”

“Will you have a pick-me-up?”

“No, no, I’m thirsty rather.”

“A glass of water?”

“No… no…”

“Well then?”

“I should like… I should like…”

The voice failed him. He gave an anxious look, as though all at once he could not bring out another word. But, getting the better of it:

“The Prefect isn’t here?”

“No; he won’t be here until five o’clock, for an important meeting.”

“Yes… I know… very important. That’s why he sent for me too. But I should have liked to see him before. How I should have liked to see him!”

The secretary looked Vérot over and said:

“How agitated you are! Is what you have to tell him as interesting as all that?”

“Of the greatest interest. It has to do with a crime committed a month ago today… And above all with preventing two murders that are the consequence of that crime and that are to be done tonight… Yes, tonight, inevitably, if we don’t take the necessary steps.”

“Come, sit down, Vérot.”

“Ah, but the whole thing has been worked out in such a devilish way! Nobody can imagine…”

“But since you’re forewarned, Vérot… since the Prefect is going to give you full powers…”

“Yes, of course… of course… But all the same it’s frightening to think that I might not meet him. So I had the idea of writing this letter, in which I tell him everything I know about the business. It was safer.”

He handed a large yellow envelope to the secretary, and he added:

“Here, and here is a little box as well, which I’m putting on this table. It holds something that completes and explains what is in the letter.”

“But why don’t you keep it all yourself?”

“I’m afraid… I’m being watched… They’re trying to get rid of me… I shan’t feel easy until I’m not the only one who knows the secret.”

“Fear nothing, Vérot. The Prefect can’t be long now. Until then I advise you to go along to the infirmary and ask for a pick-me-up.”

The inspector seemed undecided. Again he wiped his streaming forehead. Then, stiffening himself, he went out.

Left alone, the secretary slipped the letter into a bulky file spread out on the Prefect’s desk and went off by the door that opened on his own private office.

He had hardly shut it when the door of the waiting-room opened once more and the inspector came back, stammering:

“Sir… it would be better if I showed you…”

The wretched man was livid. His teeth were chattering. When he saw that the room was empty, he tried to walk towards the secretary’s office. But a weakness took him, and he collapsed on a chair where he stayed for some minutes, spent, his voice a moan.

“What is the matter with me?… Am I poisoned too? Oh, I’m frightened… I’m frightened…”

The desk was within reach of his hand. He seized a pencil, drew a writing-pad towards him and began to scrawl some words. But he mumbled:

“No, no use, since the Prefect is going to read my letter… What can be the matter with me? Oh, I’m frightened…”

All at once he got to his feet and brought out:

“Sir, we must… we must… It’s for tonight… Nothing in the world will stop…”

With short steps, like an automaton, strained by an effort of his whole will, he went forward to the door of the office. But on the way he swayed and had to sit down a second time.

A wild terror shook him and he uttered cries, so weak, alas, that nobody could hear him. He realized it, and looked about him for a bell, a hand-bell, but he could no longer see. A veil of shadow seemed to weigh on his eyes.

Then he fell to his knees and crawled to the wall, beating the air with one hand like a blind man, and ended by touching the panelling. It was the partition wall. He followed it along. Unhappily his clouded brain now offered him nothing but a false picture of the room, so that instead of turning to the left, as he should have, he followed the wall to the right, behind a screen that hid a small door.

His hand having met the handle of that door, he managed to open it. He stammered, “Help… help…” and dropped into a sort of closet that served the Prefect of Police as a dressing-room.

“Tonight!” he moaned, believing that he was heard and that he was in the secretary’s office. “Tonight… the blow is for tonight… You’ll see… the mark of the teeth… what horror!… How I’m suffering!… Help! It’s the poison… Save me!”

The voice died away. He said several times, as though in a nightmare:

“The teeth… the white teeth… they’re closing!…”

Then the voice grew fainter still, indistinct sounds came from his pale lips. His mouth seemed to chew on emptiness, like the mouth of certain old men who ruminate endlessly. His head sank little by little on to his chest. He gave two or three sighs, was shaken by a great shiver and moved no more.

And the death-rattle began, very low, in an even rhythm, with breaks in which a supreme effort of instinct seemed to revive the wavering breath of the mind and to call up in the dead eyes something like gleams of consciousness.

At ten minutes to five the Prefect of Police came into his private office.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.
