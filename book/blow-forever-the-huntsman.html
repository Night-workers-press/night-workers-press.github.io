Chapter One

The Finest Pack in Burgundy

At twenty-five, with a handsome fortune in land — fields, meadows, woods and mills — a name, a good face, complete independence, and one passion only, an honest passion a man could own to in public, what more could anybody want?

The young Marquis de Pré-Gilbert had all of it, and thought himself accordingly the happiest gentleman in the kingdom of France and the province of Burgundy.

The château of Pré-Gilbert stood on the bank of the Yonne with a pretty vine-covered slope at its back. A fine meadow spread around it and did duty as a park, and a few hundred yards off, across the river, great woods lifted their high timber and sheltered a wonderful quantity of game, from red deer down to the modest hare. Wolves, boar, fallow deer and roe, red partridge and grey, woodcock in November and golden plover in March — the marquis had all of it on his land.

He was, as we have said, in the grip of a single passion: hunting.

The passion had long since degenerated into a disease, and the disease ran in the family. Those Pré-Gilberts who had not died on a battlefield in the king’s service had died, every one of them, in open country or under the trees, as hunting men put it. The marquis’s grandfather had been ripped open by a boar. His father had lost an eye to a stag at bay.

The largest share of the estate’s income went on keeping the finest pack in Burgundy, and the huntsman was so considerable a figure at the château that from time out of mind he had held the privilege of adding to his duties in the field the graver office of steward.

Raoul had inherited the noble and unbreakable passion that had possessed his ancestors, and young as he was, he already had a solid reputation in the country as a hunting man — no small thing, since hunting is a science in which experience is very nearly indispensable.

His huntsman was something else again: one of those Nestors of the high timber and the coppice, one of those Ulysseses of the crossroads and the thicket who make their master’s glory and their neighbours’ despair, whose knowledge passes into posterity in the form of proverbs, who are envied by every man alive and called in as final arbiter on the thorniest questions.

Jean Guillé handed down rulings from which there was no appeal on every dispute between sportsmen. He judged a hound coldly, and pronounced it good or bad after a second’s inspection, without ever having seen it work. When one of the marquis’s neighbours wanted to do honour to a visitor or a relative and show him a fine day in the field, a kill that would be talked about for years, he borrowed Jean Guillé.

The Prince de Condé, having heard of his merits, sent for him one day to Chantilly, and was so taken with him that he offered to bring him into his own service at ten times the money. Jean Guillé was still young then and had not reached the age at which ambition begins to stir in a man’s heart. He refused flat, and stayed with his master, the old Marquis de Pré-Gilbert, then still living.

The Guillés had been huntsmen at Pré-Gilbert from father to son as the marquises had been lords of it from generation to generation. The two dynasties lived on excellent terms under the same roof and in the same small kingdom. The marquises had 30,000 livres a year; the Guillés had good solid savings converted into walled vineyards and acres of land in the sun, all of it lying close about the vines and fields belonging to the château. So much so that the last of the Guillés, Jean, the famous and much-honoured huntsman, kept his post purely for love of the art and as an amusement. He could perfectly well have installed his wife Claire and his daughter Rose in his own house in the village, taken on ploughmen and vine-dressers, farmed his property himself and lived very comfortably on it.

At the time this story begins, Jean Guillé was a man of about fifty, thick-set and short, though an excellent horseman; bald, but with the high ruddy colour proper to an honest inhabitant of the Yonne slopes who knows how to appreciate the marvellous wines of his own country. His broad chest housed the lungs of a Stentor, and the Homeric power of his horn had earned him the nickname Blow-Forever. The name stuck. Little by little people forgot to call him Jean Guillé at all, and in the end, from Auxerre to Clamecy and from Avallon to Sens, nobody spoke of anyone but Monsieur Blow-Forever.

He lived in a lodge separated from the château by a kitchen garden. His wife ran the house, and his daughter Rose was the linen-keeper and the château’s steward in miniature. Footmen whose livery was wearing through, maidservants who wanted a rise in wages, tradesmen of every description and the poor of the parish all applied to Mademoiselle Rose.

Rose was a charming girl of eighteen, fair as something out of Rubens, with a lily skin, a slim supple figure, and small white hands finished with beautiful almond-cut nails. Her foot was perhaps no smaller than Cinderella’s, but it was small enough that a man might have believed Perrault’s lovely tale — had Perrault lived in those days — was written for her.

Rose and the marquis had been born under the same roof and were very tenderly attached to one another. They had spent part of their childhood together, had grown up together and shared the same games. Only, Raoul de Pré-Gilbert saw in Rose nothing but a friend, a good sister, a little girl of no importance whom one loves for her sweetness, while Rose, a great deal less blind, would sometimes catch herself sighing very quietly and thinking that chance, if it had any sense of justice, ought to have made her a woman of quality — or at the very least placed the marquis somewhere lower in the world, where he might have been free to think about her.

Unfortunately Raoul de Pré-Gilbert had made neither reflection. Every faculty and every instinct he possessed was too thoroughly taken up by his ruling passion for him to have time to think about love of any kind. When he came back from hunting, riding knee to knee with his huntsman at the head of his hounds, he would dismount in the courtyard of the château and lay a kiss on Rose’s blushing forehead, a kiss full of affection, entirely innocent and perfectly cold. And Rose would sigh, and say to herself in vexation: Be pretty enough to eat, and see whether anybody so much as notices.

Chapter Two

A Passport in Another Name

Now, in those days, though he spent his time on horseback — hunting with hounds, or out in the vines and on the slopes when he was content to work a covey of partridge with a pointer and kill a hare as it broke cover — the young marquis had begun to listen uneasily, in his double character of gentleman and rich landowner, to the low rumbling along the political horizon. The storm of ‘93 was coming on, and growing more threatening every day.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
