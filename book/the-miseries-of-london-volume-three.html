Chapter One

The Irishwoman had talked at length with the Grey Man up in the little room in the bell tower, and she clearly knew what was coming, because she raised no objection and simply climbed into the four-seat cab that Shocking, who'd gone ahead, had already found.

"To Hampstead!" the Grey Man called up to the driver.

The boy didn't ask anything either.

Why would he? He was with his mother, and with the man who'd saved him from the mill. Besides, the boy was almost a man already — he'd proven as much.

Courage and clear thinking, those two essentially manly qualities, had come to him well ahead of his years.

Ralph had first seen the Grey Man in the holding cell at the Kilburn police court. Everything the man had told him then, speaking to him in the beloved language of his own country, had come true. So Ralph trusted the Grey Man the way he trusted his mother, and when the man spoke to him as the cab rolled along, he answered readily.

"My little Ralph, will you be good and do as you're told?"

"Oh yes, sir," the boy answered.

"Will you do everything I ask?"

"Yes, sir."

The cab crossed Waterloo Bridge again, climbed back up through the better neighborhoods to Holborn, and took the road to Hampstead.

"Are we going back to Mrs. Fanoche's?" Shocking asked.

The name made both the mother and the boy flinch. Still, neither showed any fear on their faces.

"No," the Grey Man said. "We're simply going to your country house."

Shocking thought he must have misheard.

"You have a country house in Hampstead, sir?" he asked.

"Not me."

"Who, then?"

"You."

"Me?" Shocking said, stunned.

"You yourself, my friend."

"Sir," Shocking said, "I'm used to seeing you work miracles, but there are some things I don't believe even God himself could manage."

"Bah," said the Grey Man.

"Not only do I not have a country house — I won't even have a roof over my head in London tomorrow. My last paid week at the boarding house runs out tomorrow, and..."

Shocking stopped short.

"And?" the Grey Man said, smiling.

"And I haven't a penny left," Shocking mumbled, hanging his head.

"What," said the Grey Man, putting on a stern expression, "you've already spent Lord Palmure's ten pounds?"

Shocking's head sank almost to his chest.

"Well," he said, "I thought it would never run out, so I went a bit fast with it."

"After all," the Grey Man said, "a dead man has no need of a roof over his head."

"What do you mean, a dead man?"

"Exactly that."

"But I'm very much alive!" Shocking said.

"I'll prove to you shortly not only that you're dead and that there's no more Shocking left in this world, but also—"

"Ah, by Saint George," Shocking cried, "I'm a trusting sort, sir, but not that trusting."

"Wait. You'll see."

Shocking looked at the Grey Man with real unease. Just then they were passing a street lamp, and its light fell straight across the man's face.

"There now," the Grey Man said, still smiling, "you're wondering whether I've lost my mind."

Shocking didn't answer.

"And whether, instead of following me to Hampstead, you wouldn't do better to haul me off to Bedlam?"

"Well," Shocking said, simply.

"Come now, a little patience, my friend, and you'll see that everything I've told you is the plain truth."

Shocking sank into deep thought. The scene at the cemetery earlier had already rattled him badly, and the Grey Man's words were finishing the job of thoroughly confusing him. But what struck him even more, perhaps, was that these words, strange as they were, hadn't seemed to trouble the Irishwoman in the least — in fact she'd even allowed herself a faint smile once or twice.

The cab rolled on a while longer, then stopped. Shocking put his head out the door and recognized the rise leading up through the heath, and Mrs. Fanoche's house.

"But you can see perfectly well we're going to Mrs. Fanoche's," he said.

"You think so?"

"Well of course — here we are on Heathmount."

"That's true."

"And there's the house."

"Get out anyway. You'll see."

At the same moment, the Grey Man handed the Irishwoman down from the cab, and her son followed her out. Shocking climbed down after them, standing there rooted to the spot, wondering why the Grey Man, who had always been kind and warm toward him, was now making fun of him like this.

But instead of heading for Mrs. Fanoche's gate, the Grey Man had stopped at the gate right next to it — which Shocking could see perfectly well, since the fog was thinner up in Hampstead, being on higher ground, and a gas lamp stood between the two houses.

Something else would have astonished Shocking too, if Shocking had still been capable of being astonished by anything ordinary after being told flatly that he was dead: the Grey Man paid off the cab driver and sent the cab away. So they were staying in Hampstead, then.

When the Grey Man rang the bell, Shocking saw a window light up suddenly in the house at the back of the garden — a house that had looked deserted until then. A moment later gravel crunched under a man's footsteps, and soon the gate swung open.

At that Shocking found his tongue again.

"Where are we going?" he said.

"To see your country house."

"Not this again!"

"Come now," said the Grey Man, "have I ever made a habit of lying to you?"

Shocking, dumbfounded, stared at the man who had just opened the gate. It was an old servant in livery, impeccably turned out, holding a lantern, who bowed without a word to the new arrivals.

The Grey Man steered Shocking ahead of him and, still offering his arm to the Irishwoman, who held her son by the hand, the four of them went into the garden together. Once the servant had closed the gate behind them, he led the way up the gravel path to the house.

Shocking walked the whole way as if his legs might give out.

"I do believe," he muttered, "I'm dreaming."

They stepped into a broad, marble-paved entrance hall, decorated with statues and baskets of flowers. The servant opened a door on the left, and Shocking, more dazzled than ever, found himself standing on the threshold of a comfortable, luxurious sitting room. A great coal fire burned in the hearth, and in the middle of the room stood a table laid for dinner.

Whatever's happening, Shocking thought, it's a rather pleasant dream. And he breathed in the rich smells rising from the table.

Then the Grey Man said to him, "You must be hungry — we forgot all about dinner today."

"But seeing as I'm dead..." Shocking said.

"It's Shocking who's dead."

"Shocking and I are one and the same."

"You'll see otherwise shortly. But," the Grey Man added, "a gentleman as refined as you can hardly sit down to table in that sorry getup you're wearing."

"And where am I supposed to find another?"

"Your valet will take you to your dressing room, and you'll change."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
