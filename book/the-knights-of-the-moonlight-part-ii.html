Chapter One

The day after Baron Gontran de Neubourg and his three friends had read through the domino's manuscript and declared the Knights of the Moonlight officially founded, a hired coupé pulled up on the rue de la Michodière, at the corner of the boulevard des Italiens. A man stepped out.

He was a strange figure, and one worth a few lines of description.

Dressed in a heavy brown overcoat, his eyes shielded by green spectacles, this man—whose age was impossible to guess—had a face scarred with deep marks whose origin no one could have named.

Burns? The ravages of some terrible bout of smallpox?

No one could have said.

The man in the green spectacles paid the driver, ducked through a side entrance, and climbed a dim staircase, one hand on the banister. He climbed all the way to the third floor and stopped in front of a door bearing the words:

Business Office.

And, underneath:

Turn the knob, please.

He did as instructed. The door swung open onto a kind of front office with a wire grille, behind which stood a cash desk.

The man in the brown coat crossed this outer room and put his hand on the key of a second door. Then he turned back toward the grille, behind which sat a young man of about twenty.

"Well," he said, "did you see anyone, Gringalet?"

"I saw the baron," the young man answered.

"Monsieur de Neubourg?"

"Yes, sir."

"What did he say?"

"When he read your letter, he looked surprised."

"Good."

"And he asked me who this Monsieur Rocambole was."

"And you told him?"

"That you were a man of business."

"And he said?"

"He said: 'I don't know Monsieur Rocambole, and I have no idea what he could want with me, but I'll go see him, since he wishes it.'"

"Ah. Did he say when he'd come?"

"He'll be here around three."

The man in the green spectacles opened his coat and pulled out his watch.

"It's half past two," he said. "The baron shouldn't be long."

He opened the second door and went into an inner room.

This one had an entirely different character. It was no longer the office of a man of business—it was a rather elegant study, its walls hung with mauve silk, its furniture of carved oak betraying a man of taste. Two shelves held rare books; a third was laden with porcelain from Sèvres, China, and Japan. Fencing masks and foils hung above a sofa upholstered in dark green velvet. A few costly paintings were scattered here and there on the walls. A handsome piece of Boulle furniture supported a bronze by Clodion.

The man in the green spectacles passed into a third room—evidently a dressing room—and emerged a few minutes later stripped of his brown overcoat and hat, now wearing a dressing gown and a Greek cap with a violet silk tassel.

Dressed this way, he threw himself into a large armchair and drew close to the fireplace, where a good fire was burning. Then, taking up the tongs, he began stirring the coals, murmuring to himself as he worked.

"Here at last is the first affair of any real interest to come my way. For two years now I've had nothing but small fry to occupy me, and my patience was starting to wear thin."

So saying, the strange character took a thick portfolio from the mantelpiece, opened it, and drew out a sheaf of papers. These papers, which he skimmed with his eyes, were covered in a hieroglyphic hand that only the man in the green spectacles could possibly have deciphered.

He began to read through them, still murmuring under his breath.

"Baron Gontran de Neubourg," he said, "Viscount Arthur de Chenevières, Lord Blakstone, and the Marquis de Verne—accomplished men, every one of them, in every respect. But it's precisely because of that they're incapable of carrying through the mission they've set themselves. Poor fellows."

And the man of business gave an almost imperceptible shrug.

At that moment the bell mounted behind the entrance door, which announced the arrival of a visitor, rang out.

"There's the baron," thought the man in the green spectacles.

Sure enough, a moment later there came a knock at the second door. Baron Gontran de Neubourg stood on the threshold.

"Monsieur Rocambole?" he asked, looking the man of business up and down.

"That's me, sir."

The baron bowed; his host returned the courtesy with a grace that spoke of good breeding.

"Monsieur," said the baron, stepping in, "I received a letter from you this morning."

"That's true, sir."

"A letter of three lines."

"Also true."

"And those three lines said: 'Monsieur le Baron de Neubourg is urgently requested to call today upon Monsieur Rocambole, man of business, on a matter of the utmost importance.'"

"Still entirely accurate, sir."

"And you are Monsieur Rocambole?"

The man of business inclined his head.

"Well then, monsieur," said the baron, "I'm listening."

Rocambole drew up a chair for the baron.

"Please, sit down. We have a great deal to discuss."

"Indeed?"

"And matters that concern you very closely."

The baron studied his host with lively curiosity.

"Go on, then," he said.

Rocambole reached toward the mantelpiece, took up a cigar box, and offered it to the baron with exquisite grace.

There's a man of business with real polish, the baron thought. And he took the cigar offered him.

"Monsieur le Baron," Rocambole went on, "you find me quite ugly, don't you?"

"Monsieur—"

"Oh, be honest. I'm hideous."

"But, monsieur—"

"I took a gunshot to the face, and my eyes were burned so badly that I can't bear to expose them to open daylight."

"You served in the army," said the baron, "at some siege, perhaps?"

"No, monsieur. I was in the galleys. If you'd seen me walk, you'd have noticed I drag my right leg slightly."

The baron gave a start in his chair.

"Don't worry," said Rocambole, smiling. "I've become an honest man, and your purse and your watch are perfectly safe here."

"But still, monsieur," said the baron, remaining calm and polite though visibly ill at ease, "could you explain to me—"

"Why I wrote to you?"

"Yes, monsieur."

"That's exactly what I intend to do. But first I need to tell you my story, in a few words."

"Is that necessary?"

"Essential."

"Then I'm listening."

Rocambole resumed.

"Monsieur le Baron, I am one of the strangest men of the century we live in. I was handsome, once, as you are; elegant, as you are. I had two or three hundred thousand francs a year, the title of marquis, blooded horses, mistresses of quality, a mansion in the Faubourg Saint-Germain, and I very nearly married the daughter of a Spanish grandee."

"And... since then?"

"Since then, I've been a convict. But before that," the man of business went on, "I was a child of Paris—a good-for-nothing spared first by the police courts, then forgotten by the assizes.

"I started by stealing. Then I moved on to murder. I have a good dozen killings on my conscience."

The baron couldn't suppress a gesture of disgust.

"But," Rocambole continued, "one day repentance descended into my heart, and I became an honest man."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
