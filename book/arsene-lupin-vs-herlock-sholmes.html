Two duels with the great English detective (1908)

by Maurice Leblanc · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Maurice Leblanc · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

The bookLe livre

In 1906, with Lupin barely a year old, Leblanc set him against the only detective more famous than he was. Conan Doyle's side objected; Leblanc scrambled the name and kept the duel. Two cases follow, The Blonde Lady and The Jewish Lamp: a lottery ticket and a writing desk, an antique lamp and a governess. Leblanc plays fair. His Sholmes is exactly as good as his reputation, works by the same method and comes within a hand's breadth of winning, and the second story ends on a page readers have argued about for a century.

En 1906, Lupin a tout juste un an quand Leblanc le jette contre le seul détective plus célèbre que lui. Le camp de Conan Doyle proteste ; Leblanc brouille le nom et garde le duel. Suivent deux affaires, La Dame blonde et La Lampe juive : un billet de loterie et un secrétaire, une lampe ancienne et une gouvernante. Leblanc joue franc jeu. Son Sholmès vaut exactement sa réputation, travaille selon la même méthode et passe à un cheveu de gagner, et la seconde histoire s'achève sur une page dont les lecteurs discutent depuis un siècle.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Part One — The Blonde Lady — Chapter I — Number 514, Series 23

On the 8th of December last year, Monsieur Gerbois, mathematics master at the lycée in Versailles, unearthed, in the clutter of a second-hand dealer’s shop, a small mahogany writing desk that took his fancy on account of its multitude of drawers.

“Just the thing for Suzanne’s birthday,” he thought.

And since he did his best, within the limits of his modest means, to give his daughter pleasure, he haggled over the price and paid the sum of sixty-five francs.

As he was giving his address, a young man of elegant bearing, who was already ferreting about to right and left, caught sight of the piece and asked:

“How much?”

“It’s sold,” replied the dealer.

“Ah!… To this gentleman, perhaps?”

Monsieur Gerbois bowed and, all the happier to own the desk now that a fellow man coveted it, took his leave.

But he had not gone ten paces down the street when the young man caught up with him and, hat in hand, in a tone of perfect courtesy, said:

“I do beg your pardon, monsieur… I am going to ask you an indiscreet question… Were you looking for this desk in particular, rather than for anything else?”

“No. I was looking for a second-hand set of scales for certain experiments in physics.”

“So you are not greatly attached to it?”

“I’m attached to it, that’s all.”

“Because it is old, perhaps?”

“Because it is convenient.”

“In that case, you would agree to exchange it for a desk just as convenient, but in better condition?”

“This one is in good condition, and an exchange seems pointless to me.”

“And yet…”

Monsieur Gerbois is an easily irritated man of touchy disposition. He answered curtly:

“Please, monsieur, don’t insist.”

The stranger planted himself in front of him.

“I don’t know what price you paid for it, monsieur… I’ll offer you double.”

“No.”

“Triple?”

“Oh! let’s leave it there,” cried the professor, out of patience. “What belongs to me is not for sale.”

The young man stared at him fixedly, with a look that Monsieur Gerbois was not to forget, then, without a word, turned on his heel and walked away.

An hour later the desk was delivered to the little house the professor occupied on the road to Viroflay. He called his daughter.

“This is for you, Suzanne—if you like it, that is.”

Suzanne was a pretty creature, demonstrative and happy. She threw her arms round her father’s neck and kissed him as joyfully as if he had given her a royal gift.

That same evening, having installed it in her room with the help of Hortense, the maid, she cleaned out the drawers and carefully put away her papers, her boxes of notepaper, her correspondence, her collections of postcards, and a few secret keepsakes she treasured in honour of her cousin Philippe.

The next morning, at half past seven, Monsieur Gerbois went off to the lycée. At ten o’clock, Suzanne, as was her daily habit, was waiting for him as he came out, and it was a great pleasure for him to catch sight, on the pavement across from the gates, of her graceful figure and her childlike smile.

They walked home together.

“And your desk?”

“A perfect marvel! Hortense and I have done the brasses. You’d think it was gold.”

“So you’re pleased?”

“Am I pleased! I simply don’t know how I ever managed without it.”

They crossed the garden in front of the house. Monsieur Gerbois suggested:

“Shall we go and look at it before lunch?”

“Oh! yes, that’s a good idea.”

She went up first, but on reaching the threshold of her room she gave a cry of dismay.

“What’s the matter?” stammered Monsieur Gerbois.

He went into the room in his turn. The desk was no longer there.

* * *

…What astonished the examining magistrate was the admirable simplicity of the means employed. In Suzanne’s absence, and while the maid was out doing her shopping, a licensed porter wearing his badge—neighbours saw it—had stopped his handcart in front of the garden and rung twice. The neighbours, not knowing that the maid was out, suspected nothing, so that the fellow went about his business in the most perfect tranquillity.

One thing was worth noting: no wardrobe was forced, no clock disturbed. What is more, Suzanne’s purse, which she had left on the marble top of the desk, turned up on the table beside it, with the gold coins it contained. The motive for the theft was thus clearly established, which made the theft all the more inexplicable, for, after all, why run so many risks for so paltry a haul?

The only clue the professor could offer was the incident of the previous day.

“That young man at once showed sharp annoyance at my refusal, and I had the very distinct impression that he was leaving me with a threat.”

It was vague enough. The dealer was questioned. He knew neither of the two gentlemen. As for the object, he had bought it for forty francs, at Chevreuse, at an estate sale, and rather thought he had resold it at its fair value. Further inquiry revealed nothing more.

But Monsieur Gerbois remained convinced that he had suffered an enormous loss. A fortune must have been hidden in the false bottom of a drawer, and that was why the young man, knowing of the hiding place, had acted with such resolve.

“My poor father, what would we have done with that fortune?” Suzanne kept saying.

“What! Why, with a dowry like that you could have aimed at the very best matches.”

Suzanne, whose aims went no further than her cousin Philippe, who was a pitiful match, sighed bitterly. And in the little house at Versailles life went on, less cheerful, less carefree, darkened by regrets and disappointments.

Two months went by. And suddenly, blow upon blow, the gravest of events, an unforeseen sequence of lucky strokes and catastrophes!…

On the 1st of February, at half past five, Monsieur Gerbois, who had just come home, an evening paper in his hand, sat down, put on his spectacles and began to read. Politics being of no interest to him, he turned the page. An article at once caught his attention, headed:

“Third Draw of the Press Associations Lottery…”

“Number 514, Series 23, Wins a Million…”

The paper slipped from his fingers. The walls swayed before his eyes, and his heart stopped beating. Number 514, series 23—that was his number! He had bought it by chance, to do a friend a favour, for he had little faith in the favours of fate, and now he had won!

Quickly he took out his notebook. Number 514, series 23, was indeed written down, as a reminder, on the flyleaf. But the ticket?

He dashed to his study to look for the box of envelopes among which he had slipped the precious ticket, and at the very doorway he stopped short, reeling again, his heart clenched: the box of envelopes was not there, and—terrifying thought—he suddenly realised that it had not been there for weeks! For weeks he had no longer seen it in front of him during the hours when he corrected his pupils’ exercises!

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.
