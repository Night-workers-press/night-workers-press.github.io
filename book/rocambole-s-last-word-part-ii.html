Chapter One

On one of those splendid February days that only Paris seems to know how to produce, the Bois de Boulogne was thick with carriages and riders by two o'clock in the afternoon.

It was the place where the sporting set and the horse crowd came to see and be seen, to nod to each other or trade a knowing glance.

The dandy slowed his trotter to steal a glance at Mademoiselle Cerisette, out for the first time in a smart two-horse rig; the banker kept an eye on Coralie, to whom he paid five thousand francs a month and suspected of haunting the Bois every single day only to meet the young Vicomte de R..., who was busy running through his last uncle's fortune and riding his last remaining horse.

And Mademoiselle de Saint-Euverte — Joséphine, once upon a time — freshly idle since Monsieur D... had fled the scene, was making good use of her spare time by setting her sights on a South American.

In short, it was the most elegant, most mixed company you could hope to find.

That day, this whole world seemed unusually stirred up, buzzing in clusters and calling from one carriage to the next about some momentous event.

All of Europe was at peace; no revolution had broken out, and there was no talk of any great financial disaster.

No — it was both more and less than any of that.

Aspasie had just been seen.

She had appeared in her coupé, drawn by the two magnificent Irish trotters that Prince K... of Russia had once offered a hundred thousand francs for — an offer she had turned down.

Who was Aspasie?

To tell the plain truth, her real name may well have been Caroline.

But Caroline was a name for shopkeepers' daughters, and Aspasie's trade was ruining the sons of crusading families and Austrian barons.

She was thirty-two, blonde verging on red-haired, possessed of a wickedly sharp wit, once famous for her utter coldness — and it was the death of the young Neapolitan Duke Galipieri, killed in a duel fought over her, that had made her fashionable seven or eight years earlier.

Aspasie had kept a salon — a real one. She had owned the finest diamonds, the finest horses, the most charming little townhouse on the Champs-Élysées.

She had entertained artists, writers, senators, and princes.

For seven or eight years, people had praised her biting wit and singular beauty, marveled at her utter heartlessness, and kept a running tally of the ruined men she left in her wake.

Then, one morning or one evening — no one could say for certain which — Aspasie had vanished.

She had sold everything: horses, townhouse, furniture, lace, diamonds.

Young X..., who had made a scandalous fortune on the Bourse and was pouring it out at her feet, had very nearly blown his brains out in despair.

No one knew what had become of her.

Rumor had it, though, that this block of ice had finally melted in the sun, that this heart of bronze had been moved at last — that the woman who had trampled the honor of respectable families underfoot, who had made herself the minotaur devouring gilded youth, had fallen in love...

That she loved madly, with passion, with fury — like a tigress, not a woman.

That had been a year ago, and for a whole year no one had seen Aspasie anywhere — not at opening nights, not at the races, not in the Bois.

Yet a few young men swore she had never left Paris.

They claimed she was living shut away in a small house on the Place Vintimille, one of the quietest, most secluded corners of the city, and that she went out only at night, in a plain carriage, wearing one of those new veil-masks so useful for throwing off the curious.

If she was never seen around the lake, some claimed to have crossed paths with her in the deserted paths of the Bois de Vincennes, in the company of a young man impeccable in both manner and dress.

The women of the world Aspasie had once belonged to were divided on the matter.

The most jaded among them, the ones who had hung their hearts on so many hooks that nothing remained but rags, said with open envy:

"Lucky her."

The others — the young, the brazen, the naive — murmured with disdain:

"Who would have believed it! She's thrown herself away!"

Once everyone had had their say, silence fell.

After a year, Aspasie was all but forgotten — and then, suddenly, she reappeared.

She had been seen. She was being seen right now.

There she was, at two in the afternoon, in the spring-like air, in that same brown coupé with a salamander painted in monochrome on its panels in place of a coat of arms.

She sat there, sweeping the crowd with a calm, proud gaze.

Two young men trotting side by side along the riders' path stopped dead, stunned.

"It's not possible," said one.

"I must be dreaming," murmured the other.

"And yet it's really her. Aspasie."

"Of course it is!"

"Where has she come from?"

"I thought she was dead."

"So did I."

As they traded these exclamations — the same ones already traded by a thousand other people that afternoon — Aspasie caught sight of them and gave a friendly little wave with her exquisitely gloved fingertips.

The gesture was an invitation, and both men understood it perfectly.

They rode over.

"Hello," said Aspasie, leaning out of the carriage window.

"Well, my dear," said one of them, "is it really you, or just your ghost?"

"It's me."

"Alive!"

"So it would seem..."

She flashed them a smile, all dazzling teeth.

"Where have you been?"

"God alone knows."

A flash lit her eyes.

"Aspasie," said the first young man, "do you know everything that's been said about you while you were gone?"

"No, and I couldn't care less."

"They say your heart finally spoke up."

"It's true," she said simply.

"You fell in love?"

"Madly."

"And... do you still... love him?"

"I hate him!"

She said it in a low, hollow voice.

The two young men exchanged a look.

A dark flame burned in Aspasie's wide blue eyes.

"Baron," she said, turning to the first, "do you still love me?"

"Of course," he replied lightly.

"And you, Marquis?"

She was speaking to the second man, little more than a boy.

"Give the order," he replied, "and I'll obey."

"Both of you, come see me tonight."

"What — both of us?" said the baron, taken aback.

"Yes."

"That's strange..."

"No it isn't. You'll see. I've moved back into my house on Avenue de Marignan. Dinner's at seven. Come."

And she gave them each her hand.

"But why both of us?" the marquis asked in turn, sulking.

"I'm looking for an avenger," Aspasie answered, in a tone that made them both shiver.

Chapter Two

Ten o'clock had just struck on the rococo clock in Aspasie's boudoir.

And there they both were, cigars between their lips, still digesting a delicate dinner, ready to hear the sinner's confession — the twenty-year-old marquis and the thirty-year-old baron.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
