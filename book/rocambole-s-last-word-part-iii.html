Chapter One

Two nights before, when they'd set off from the club, Monsieur de Montgeron had seen nothing in the whole expedition but one of those tawdry little Parisian love affairs — the kind that make fools of the men who chase them, and of everyone unlucky enough to watch. Paris, after all, had long since stopped being a city of rope ladders, ballads, and serenades; a soldier takes off his uniform before calling on women like that, and these days poets don't reach for a guitar, they reach for a pretty scrap of paper bearing a banker's signature.

So Monsieur de Montgeron had gone along with Gustave Marion purely out of curiosity, when it had been a question of carrying off the Beautiful Gardener, convinced the whole business would end with a supper at the Café Anglais, where the lady — not exactly known for playing hard to get — would do the honors without any false modesty.

But things had turned out very differently.

And now a sharp, almost painful curiosity had woken in Monsieur de Montgeron, a burning need to understand. Who was this woman? What had Gustave Marion seen in her house that had driven him out of his mind so suddenly? Monsieur de Montgeron swore to himself that he would get to the bottom of the mystery.

During the few hours the party had spent at Saint-Cloud, at the Tête-Noire restaurant, he'd noticed that the four young men who'd accompanied Gustave Marion were so shaken by the whole affair that there was no counting on them. So when he took his leave of them, he hadn't said a word about his plan — he claimed instead that he had urgent business to settle in Paris that very evening.

Fate, it seemed, had shown some intelligence in pairing him at the club with Casimir de Noireterre. Casimir was twenty years old, no less bold than his cousin by marriage, Monsieur de Montgeron. He'd been a midshipman, at sea for two years, when a substantial inheritance had caught up with him in Rio de Janeiro, where his ship was then stationed. Casimir had done just as Montgeron had done: resigned his commission and come to Paris to live the high life. Montgeron — cousin to Casimir's sister-in-law, since Casimir had an older brother, a decent sort who lived quietly on his estates in the Périgord — had more or less made himself the boy's guardian and introduced him everywhere.

Such were the two men who now meant to unravel whatever mystery surrounded the Beautiful Gardener.

Nights follow one after another in Paris, as everywhere else, but no two are alike. The night before, and the one before that, had been clear and bright. This one, a thick yellow fog hung over Paris, breathing out a fine rain that soaked straight through to the bone.

Monsieur de Montgeron had his carriage waiting at the door of the club, on the boulevard. He handed Casimir in and said:

"I'm a careful man. Here, take this."

He pressed into Casimir's hand a handsome Corsican stiletto in a blue velvet sheath trimmed with chased silver, and added:

"Pistols and revolvers are stage props — good for nothing but getting you arrested. This is worth more."

The coachman clearly had his instructions already, for he loosened the reins and the trotter set off briskly. Instead of climbing the Champs-Élysées and cutting through the Bois, the carriage followed the riverbank and the tracks of the American railway all the way to the Sèvres bridge. Less than three-quarters of an hour later it drew up at the very spot where, two nights before, Gustave Marion had left his break.

For most of the ride, Montgeron and Casimir de Noireterre had barely exchanged a word. But once they'd left the carriage and started down the sunken lane on foot, Casimir asked:

"How are we getting in?"

"I still have the key to the gate."

"And to the house?"

"That too. Marion paid dearly enough for them that we might as well make use of them."

The distant sound of a bell reached them as they walked — the clock at the Sèvres porcelain works, striking midnight.

After a quarter of an hour, though the night was pitch dark, Monsieur de Montgeron put out his hand and said:

"There's the house."

Light still burned on the first floor. As it had two nights before, the surrounding countryside was silent. Not even a watchdog barked. Monsieur de Montgeron took the two keys from his pocket and opened the gate.

"Now follow me," he told Casimir de Noireterre, "and may God help us."

From the gate to the house — really no more than a square pavilion — it was about a hundred paces, along a tree-lined path. Montgeron and Casimir picked their way carefully so as not to make the gravel crunch underfoot. Twice along the way Montgeron stopped to listen; he thought he'd heard a faint sound. The second time, deciding he'd imagined it, he started forward again — and a black shape suddenly loomed up in front of him.

"Careful," Montgeron said, and his hand went to his dagger.

Casimir did the same. The shape came closer, and soon Montgeron, braced and ready, made out clearly the silhouette of a man.

"Who's there?" a voice said.

Montgeron didn't answer. The man came closer still, and once he was right up close, repeated:

"Who are you? What do you want?"

Montgeron's hand shot out and caught him by the throat.

"Who are you? What do you want?"

"Scream and you're dead," the viscount said, pressing the point of his stiletto to the stranger's chest.

The man seemed to be seized with terror.

"Don't kill me," he stammered. "If you're thieves, you've got the wrong house..."

"Who are you?"

"Just a poor servant."

Montgeron found it amusing to play the part of a thief in earnest.

"Plenty of servants have savings," he said.

"I haven't got any, I swear..."

But the man's shaking voice gave the lie to his words. A memory crossed Montgeron's mind.

"Even if all you had," he said, "were the hundred louis that Monsieur Gustave Marion gave you."

"You know about that?" the servant stammered.

"And the proof is that to get in here, I just used the key you sold him."

The man's manner changed at once, his fright seeming to melt away.

"Forgive me, sir," he said. "I took you for a thief."

"Ah," said Montgeron, laughing.

"But I see now that you..."

The servant bowed.

"So you've guessed why we've come?"

"More or less."

"Well then," said Montgeron, "think it over, and think fast."

"What does the gentleman want?"

"I'll give you a choice: a knife in the ribs, or another hundred louis."

"The gentleman is joking — he knows perfectly well there's no need to hesitate."

"So you're choosing the hundred louis."

"Oh, without question."

"Then talk."

"What does the gentleman wish to know?"

Montgeron pointed toward the lit window.

"What's up there?"

"Sir," the servant said, "I'm a family man, I've got three children, I'm fond of my own skin. I sold a key to Monsieur Marion, who's a young fool, but I can see you're a different sort of man, and..."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
