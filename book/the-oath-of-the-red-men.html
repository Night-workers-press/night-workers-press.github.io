PROLOGUE

Friends and Rivals

Chapter One

An Improvised Duel

One evening in January 1746, the Opéra was giving a ball.

The whole court will be there, Madame Toinon said to herself. She was a costumier and dress-hirer, and she lodged in the rue des Jeux-Neufs — the rue des Jeûneurs today — at the sign of the Harlequin’s Bat.

Then she added, aloud, “Come along, Tony, make your preparations. You’re taking me. I’ll dress you as a gentleman.”

“And you, mistress? What will you be?”

“I shall go as a marquise.”

“With beauty spots?”

“Well, naturally.”

“And hoops?”

“Out to here.”

And Mother Toinon curved both arms and carried them as far from her body as they would go, to show the breadth of the hoops she intended.

Now, Mother Toinon was a pretty brunette, buxom and smiling, hardly more than thirty-four, who passed for twenty-eight every evening of her life and was the darling of the quarter. She was a widow. She had no children, and she had refused to marry again.

But one morning she had found a poor little boy of eight on her doorstep, shivering and crying, and she had taken him in. The abandoned child did not know his father’s name or his mother’s; he knew only that people called him Tony. He seemed to have had some violent fright that had carried his memory away with it, and all Mother Toinon could ever get out of him was that masked men had tried to kill him. The costumier kept the child and adopted him.

From that day she gave up all thought of marrying again, and the sharp tongues of the neighbourhood put it about that the foundling was her own son — a slip of her first youth that the husband had never known anything about. By the time this story begins, Tony was barely sixteen, but he was tall and strong, admirably well built, with a charming face full of mischief and wit. In the street they called him nothing but Mother Toinon’s handsome shop boy.

“So you’re going to the ball?” he asked his adoptive mother.

“And why not?” she answered, giving herself a tolerably admiring glance in the little mirror above the counter. “I’m not too far gone yet for a woman of thirty-four, and powder doesn’t always suit real marquises half so well.”

Then Mother Toinon, who — as you will have gathered — was not precisely modesty in person, looked her shop boy up and down.

“And you, my little one,” she said, “do you know you’ll be charming in that fine sky-blue coat with the spangles, and the red waistcoat, and the white satin breeches I had made up not long ago for that gentleman from the provinces?”

“Oh yes,” said Tony, “and who left the whole lot on your hands because you wouldn’t give him credit?”

“Exactly.”

“And you think they’ll suit me?”

“To a marvel.”

Tony took his own turn at the glass, and was not too distressed by the examination.

“You’ll be good enough to eat,” Mother Toinon added, and the look she fixed on her adopted son was perhaps not a very maternal one.

“Shall I have to be powdered?”

“Of course you will.”

“And what time do we go?”

“Right at the beginning. Midnight. You’ll dance with me, I imagine?”

“The thing is, I don’t really know how.”

“Bah. I’ll show you.”

“And who minds the shop?”

“Babet, of course.”

Babet was Mother Toinon’s one servant: an honest, disagreeable spinster who dropped her eyes and worked hard at a blush whenever a man happened to look at her.

While they were talking, a customer came into the shop — a gentleman of about thirty, well set up, with a haughty air, one fist planted impertinently on the guard of his sword cocked up behind him. He gave Mother Toinon a wave of the hand, familiar and patronising, and even took her a little by the chin.

“Still pretty and still a widow,” he said.

“Ah, Monsieur le marquis,” answered the costumier, who was not in the least put out by the small liberties the gentleman took with her, “you’ve said that to me often, and always on this same day of the year — which proves at once that I am getting old and that you are still young.”

“I beg your pardon?” said the gentleman. “Anyone would think you turned a phrase like Monsieur de Marivaux himself, Toinon.”

“Not at all, monseigneur. I’m getting old, because it’s a long while now since you first said the same thing to me; and you are still young, because you come back, just as you used to, whenever the Opéra ball comes round.”

And Toinon struck a faintly mocking attitude.

“So we’re still amusing ourselves?” she said. “Still chasing tradesmen’s wives? Lady’s maids? Shop girls?”

“Quiet, Madame Toinon. That sort of thing was all very well once.”

“Eh?”

“I’m married.”

Mother Toinon raised her hands to heaven with a piteous expression.

“Oh, dear God,” she said. “The poor woman.”

“You don’t know what you’re saying, my good Toinon. The devil has turned hermit. I adore my wife.”

“Is she rich, at least?”

“Very rich.”

“Young?”

“Twenty.”

“Pretty?”

“Like an angel.”

“And you’re going to the Opéra ball — Lord God! Because if I’m looking at you, it means —”

“Hush,” said the marquis. “It means my wife and her sister have taken a singular fancy.”

Mother Toinon looked at him.

“The ladies,” he went on, “have got it into their heads to go to the Opéra ball tonight dressed as shepherdesses.”

“And you’ll be going with them, I suppose?”

“Naturally.”

“Dressed as a shepherd?”

“Or a faun. I haven’t quite settled. So I’ve come to ask you, my dear Toinon, to send me several complete shepherdess costumes as soon as you possibly can. The ladies will choose.”

The costumier looked at Tony. Tony had been standing quite still in the darkest corner of the shop since the marquis walked in.

“My pet,” said Mother Toinon, “you’ll go round to Monsieur le marquis’s house.”

“It would be a great deal simpler,” said the marquis, “for the boy to come with me now.”

“As you please, Monsieur le marquis.”

In the time it takes to blink, Mother Toinon had matched up her fabrics, filled three big boxes and called a porter in from her doorstep. Then she leaned to her dear shop boy’s ear.

“Come straight back,” she said. “You’ve to be powdered and got into your costume.”

The porter settled the boxes on his hooks and made ready to follow Mother Toinon’s customer.

“Which way are we going, Monsieur le marquis?” Tony asked.

“The Île Saint-Louis.”

To spare the great man the tedium of walking side by side with a porter, the boy sent the fellow off through the back streets to wait at the end of the rue Saint-Louis-en-l’Isle.

The marquis, for his part, began to question Tony as they walked. Tony was not a shy boy. He had the quick, supple, faintly mocking mind of a child of Paris; he had always liked the company of people of quality, who came in and out of Mother Toinon’s shop in numbers; and now that the gentleman had let the rein out a little, he chattered away about this and that.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
