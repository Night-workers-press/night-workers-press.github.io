CHAPTER I

—

The Inn of the Pré

⁂

People called it the Inn of the Pré, and nothing more. And that was enough, for it was as famous, as fashionable, as well patronized as the celebrated inn of La Devinière. There was not a Parisian who did not know that the "pré" in question meant the prestigious and legendary Pré-aux-Clercs, at the very heart of which it stood.

Once you had passed through the city wall, whether by the porte de Nesle or the porte de Buci, and had come up as far as the rue de Seine—which was then beginning to fill with houses—you found a little path. The inn was the sole and only house built along the edge of that narrow lane, rutted, muddy in winter and dusty in summer, which ran, on the left hand, beside the crenellated wall of the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, and, on the right hand, beside a succession of scrubby meadows.

Those meadows, dotted here and there with elms, ancient oaks, and fruit trees, were the Pré-aux-Clercs. The path was called the Chemin-aux-Clercs. Later it was to take the name of the rue du Colombier. Today it is the rue Jacob. The Inn of the Pré stood at the far end of the Chemin-aux-Clercs, not far from another path they called the chemin de Saint-Père, from the name of a little chapel to which it led. That path has become the rue des Saints-Pères. Seen from without, the inn had all the appearance of a small fortress: the place was remote, and brawls were frequent there. Within, it was arranged so that its wealthy clientele were assured of finding all the comfort and all the discretion they could desire.

On the day this tale begins, the fifth of May 1560, the Chemin-aux-Clercs presented—as on every other day, for that matter—an unceasing coming and going of people entering the fashionable inn or leaving it. Couples on amorous errands slipped away beneath the discreet arbours of the gardens laid out at the back, while men who came alone crowded into the common room.

At the moment we enter it, the room was packed with customers. Supper in those days was taken at five o'clock. It was now close upon six. Which is to say that the meals were drawing to their end, and that the diners, greatly excited by good fare and too generous draughts, were making a mighty uproar there.

A young girl appeared upon the threshold of a service door that opened onto the gardens: seventeen years old, pretty enough to set a man dreaming, lively, supple, infinitely graceful, with fine black eyes at once bold and tender, mischievous and ingenuous. She wore with admirable ease the coquettish and shimmering costume of the contadine of Bergamo.

The door, in whose frame she stood, had been cut at the far end of the wall at the back, a few steps from the fireplace on the right. In the recess formed by that fireplace a table had been set. At this table, hidden behind the boxes of shrubs and flowers, two men were seated. The first of these men—who had the torso of a Hercules—sat with his back to her. The other faced her.

He was a gentleman of some thirty years, very elegant, a very handsome fellow—too handsome, perhaps. The curtain of foliage behind which he sheltered not sufficing him, it seemed, he had kept on his cloak. And it was only when, owing to some rather abrupt movement, that cloak fell open that one could catch a glimpse of a sumptuous costume of silk and velvet. From his corner, certain of not being seen himself, he watched with a dark attention the slightest gestures of a young lord who was supping alone a few tables further off. And at times, as he stared at him, his light grey eye took on an expression of terrifying ferocity. And with a nervous, mechanical gesture he would then twist up with his fingertips the ends of a silky black moustache.

It was upon this person, before she had come in, that the young girl's eyes fell. And a shadow of vexation passed across her brow, so pure, and she made an instinctive movement of recoil. Curious, she leaned forward and followed that gaze charged with mortal hatred. And she saw the young lord to whom it was directed. Courageous, she thrust down her apprehension and came in resolutely, with a pretty gesture of bravado.

Her appearance was greeted with cheers and cries of joy that rose from every side:

"Fiorinda, the teller of fortunes!—Fiorinda the Fair!—Fiorinda the Cruel!"

"Greetings to you all, noble sirs!" she flung out gaily.

And very calm, very much at her ease, she set about moving among these men, their faces flushed with the fumes of heady wines, their eyes gleaming with desire. And in her wholly natural attitude, supremely indifferent, one would have looked in vain for any hint of coquetry. It leaped to the eye that all the men gathered in that room existed, in her sight, only as customers. Her sole concern was to ply honestly the trade that gave her a living. And that trade consisted in foretelling the future, after a summary study of the hand.

She was prudent, however, for she manoeuvred so as to avoid the man who had seemed to trouble her to the point that she had almost given up her work on his account. Unhappily, the manoeuvre was difficult: she had to skirt the table, to brush past the man with the torso of a Hercules. True, he had his back to her, and appeared solely intent upon stuffing himself with such ardour that one might have thought he was there to make up for the effects of a very long fast. True, the gentleman was so passionately absorbed in his watching that she might hope to succeed. But—

On hearing that musical name cried aloud, fragrant as a nosegay of flowers—Fiorinda—the gentleman had given a start and, forsaking every other care, had turned quickly toward her. A sudden flush crimsoned his sallow cheeks. A burning flame kindled in his eyes. And those eyes then took on an expression of passion, violent, savage, that had about it something indefinably disquieting. With his foot, beneath the table, he warned his companion, and under his breath, in an imperious tone, he commanded:

"Stop her, Guillaume Pentecôte!"

The man who answered to that singular name of Guillaume Pentecôte raised his nose from his plate. He thus revealed a frightful muzzle, seamed with scars, slashed across by two enormous moustaches. There could be no mistaking it: this one was a rogue. And a rogue of formidable aspect. He must have been trained to the swift and passive execution of mysterious orders, understood at a hint. He had not a second's hesitation: he stretched out an arm of prodigious length, brought down a broad and hairy hand upon the young girl, who was engaging upon a crosswise passage, lifted her like a feather, and set her gently down beside the gentleman, at the very moment when she believed she had escaped him.

She made a gesture of deep disgust. Her black eyes flashed a double lightning, while the red of indignation crimsoned her cheeks. She was about to protest aloud. ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
