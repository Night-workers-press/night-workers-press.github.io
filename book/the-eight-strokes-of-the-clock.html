The bookLe livre

Lupin is not called Lupin in this book. He is Prince Serge Rénine, and he offers Hortense Daniel, unhappily married and shut up in a country house she cannot leave, a bargain: eight adventures in three months, and at the eighth, when a certain clock strikes eight, she will owe him a kiss. What follows is eight short, hard, beautifully made mysteries, from a woman thrown into a dungeon at the top of a tower to Footprints in the Snow, one of the finest detective stories written in French. Complete, with Leblanc's own prefatory note.

Dans ce livre, Lupin ne s'appelle pas Lupin. Il est le prince Serge Rénine, et il propose un marché à Hortense Daniel, mal mariée et enfermée dans une maison de campagne qu'elle ne peut quitter : huit aventures en trois mois, et à la huitième, quand une certaine horloge sonnera huit coups, elle lui devra un baiser. Suivent huit énigmes brèves, dures, admirablement construites, de la femme jetée dans un cachot en haut d'une tour à une histoire de pas dans la neige qui compte parmi les plus belles nouvelles policières de langue française. Texte complet, avec la note liminaire de Leblanc.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — At the Top of the Tower

Hortense Daniel pushed her window ajar and whispered:

“Are you there, Rossigny?”

“I am here,” answered a voice that came up from the shrubberies banked at the foot of the house.

Leaning out a little, she saw a rather fat man looking up at her with a thick red face framed in a fringe of over-fair beard.

“Well?” he said.

“Well, there was a great argument with my uncle and my aunt last night. They absolutely refuse to sign the settlement my solicitor sent them a draft of, and to give me back the dowry my husband got through before he was shut up.”

“But your uncle, who wanted the marriage, is responsible under the terms of the contract.”

“Never mind. He refuses.”

“Then?”

“Then are you still determined to carry me off?” she asked, laughing.

“More than ever.”

“All above board, remember!”

“Whatever you please. You know I am mad about you.”

“The trouble is that, unluckily, I am not mad about you.”

“I don’t ask you to be mad about me, only to love me a little.”

“A little? You are far too demanding.”

“In that case, why did you choose me?”

“Chance. I was bored. My life wanted the unexpected. So I am taking a risk. Here, here is my luggage.”

She let two enormous leather bags slide down, and Rossigny caught them in his arms.

“The die is cast,” she murmured. “Go and wait for me with your car at the crossroads by the yew. I shall ride over.”

“Hang it, I can hardly run off with your horse as well.”

“He will find his own way back.”

“Excellent. Ah, by the way.”

“What is it?”

“Who exactly is this Prince Rénine who has been here three days and whom nobody knows?”

“I have no idea. My uncle met him out shooting, at some friends’ house, and invited him.”

“He likes you very much. Yesterday you went for a long ride with him. He is a man I don’t take to.”

“In two hours I shall have left this house in your company. That is a scandal which will probably cool Serge Rénine off. And now enough talking. We have no time to lose.”

For some minutes she watched fat Rossigny, bent under the weight of the bags, making off along an empty path; then she shut the window.

Outside, far off in the park, a fanfare of horns was sounding the reveille. The pack broke into furious barking. It was the opening of the season, that morning, at the château of La Marèze, where every year, towards the beginning of September, the Comte d’Aigleroche, a mighty hunter before the Lord, and the countess gathered a few friends and the neighbouring landowners.

Hortense finished dressing slowly, put on a riding habit that showed her supple figure, set on her head a felt hat whose broad brim framed her handsome face and her red hair, and sat down at her writing table, where she wrote her uncle, Monsieur d’Aigleroche, a farewell letter to be given him in the evening. A difficult letter, which she began several times and finally gave up.

“I’ll write to him later,” she said to herself, “when his anger has passed.”

And she went into the lofty dining room.

Enormous logs were blazing in the hearth. Trophies of guns and rifles decorated the walls. On all sides the guests were streaming in and coming up to shake hands with the Comte d’Aigleroche, one of those country gentlemen, heavy-looking and thick in the neck, who live for nothing but shooting. Standing in front of the fireplace, with a large glass of old brandy in his hand, he was drinking their health.

Hortense kissed him absently.

“What, uncle? You, who are usually so temperate.”

“Bah!” he said. “Once a year one may allow oneself a little excess.”

“My aunt will scold you.”

“Your aunt has a migraine and won’t be coming down. Besides,” he added in a gruff tone, “it is no business of hers. And still less of yours, my girl.”

Prince Rénine came up to Hortense. He was a young man, very elegant, with a thin and rather pale face, and eyes that took by turns the gentlest and the hardest, the most amiable and the most ironical expression. He bowed to the young woman, kissed her hand and said:

“May I remind you of your kind promise, dear madame?”

“My promise?”

“Yes, it was agreed between us that we should repeat our fine ride of yesterday, and try to get into that old barricaded house whose look had intrigued us. What they call, I believe, the Halingre estate.”

She answered with a certain dryness:

“I am very sorry, monsieur, but it would be a long excursion and I am a little tired. I shall take a turn in the park and come in again.”

There was a silence between them, and Serge Rénine said, smiling, with his eyes fixed on hers, and in such a way that she alone heard:

“I am sure you will keep your word and take me as your companion. It would be better.”

“Better for whom? For you, I suppose?”

“For you as well, I assure you.”

She flushed slightly and retorted:

“I don’t understand you, monsieur.”

“And yet I am setting you no riddle. The road is charming, the Halingre estate interesting. No other ride would give you the same pleasure.”

“You are not lacking in conceit, monsieur.”

“Nor in obstinacy, madame.”

She made an irritated movement, but disdained to answer. Turning her back on him, she shook a few hands round her and went out of the room.

At the foot of the steps a groom was holding her horse. She mounted and rode off towards the woods that continued the park.

The weather was cool and still. Between the leaves, which barely stirred, a sky of blue crystal showed. Hortense followed at a walk the winding paths, which brought her, after half an hour, into a region of ravines and steep banks crossed by the high road.

She stopped. Not a sound. Rossigny must have switched off his engine and hidden his car in the thickets that surround the crossroads by the yew.

Five hundred metres at most separated her from that circle. After a few moments’ hesitation she got down, tied her horse carelessly so that at the least effort it could get free and go back to the house, wrapped her face in the long brown veil that floated over her shoulders, and went forward.

She had not been mistaken. At the first turning she caught sight of Rossigny. He ran to her and drew her into the copse.

“Quick, quick. Ah, how afraid I was of a delay, or even of a change of mind! And here you are! Is it possible?”

She was smiling.

“How happy you are to be doing something foolish!”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix.
