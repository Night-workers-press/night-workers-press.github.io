Le Bouchon de cristal

The Lupin novel where he loses (1912)

The bookLe livre

Two of Lupin's men are taken during a burglary outside Paris, and one of them, a boy of twenty called Gilbert, is sentenced to death for a murder he did not commit. Lupin has eight months to save him, and everything he tries fails. The prize is a sheet of paper: the list of twenty-seven public men who took money over a canal scheme, held by a deputy named Daubrecq, who keeps it hidden somewhere about him or about his house. Leblanc plays his favourite trick more honestly here than anywhere else: the answer is on the page from the second chapter onwards.

Deux hommes de Lupin sont pris au cours d'un cambriolage aux environs de Paris, et l'un d'eux, Gilbert, un garçon de vingt ans, est condamné à mort pour un meurtre qu'il n'a pas commis. Lupin a huit mois pour le sauver, et tout ce qu'il tente échoue. L'enjeu est une feuille de papier : la liste des vingt-sept hommes publics qui ont touché de l'argent dans une affaire de canal, que détient un député, Daubrecq, et qu'il cache quelque part sur lui ou chez lui. Leblanc joue ici son tour favori plus loyalement que nulle part ailleurs : la réponse est sur la page dès le deuxième chapitre.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — Arrests

The two boats rocked in the shadows, made fast to the little jetty that ran out from the garden. Through the thick mist one could make out, here and there along the banks of the lake, a lighted window. Opposite, the casino at Enghien streamed with light, though it was the last days of September. A few stars showed between the clouds. A light breeze lifted the surface of the water.

Arsène Lupin came out of the summerhouse where he had been smoking a cigar and, leaning out from the end of the jetty:

“Grognard? Le Ballu?… are you there?”

A man rose in each of the boats, and one of them answered:

“Yes, chief.”

“Get ready. I can hear the car coming back with Gilbert and Vaucheray.”

He crossed the garden, went round a house that was being built, whose scaffolding showed dimly in the dark, and cautiously opened the door that gave on to the Avenue de Ceinture. He had not been mistaken: a bright glare swept round the bend, and a big open motor-car pulled up, out of which jumped two men in overcoats with the collars turned up, and caps on their heads.

They were Gilbert and Vaucheray: Gilbert, a young fellow of twenty or twenty-two, with a likeable face and an easy, powerful way of moving; Vaucheray, shorter, his hair going grey, his face pale and unhealthy.

“Well,” asked Lupin, “did you see him, the deputy?”

“Yes, chief,” Gilbert answered. “We saw him take the seven-forty for Paris, just as we knew he would.”

“So we have a free hand?”

“An entirely free hand. The Villa Marie-Thérèse is at our disposal.”

The chauffeur had stayed in his seat. Lupin said to him:

“Don’t wait here. It might attract attention. Come back at half past nine exactly, in time to load the car… supposing the expedition doesn’t come to grief.”

“Why should it come to grief?” said Gilbert.

The car drove off, and Lupin, taking the road down to the lake again with his new companions, answered:

“Why? Because it isn’t I who planned it, and when it isn’t I, I only half trust it.”

“Come, chief, I’ve been working with you for three years now… I’m beginning to learn the trade!”

“Yes… my lad, you’re beginning,” said Lupin, “and that is precisely why I’m afraid of blunders… Come along, get in… And you, Vaucheray, take the other boat… Good… Now row, children… and as little noise as possible.”

Grognard and Le Ballu, the two oarsmen, pulled straight for the opposite shore, a little to the left of the casino.

They passed first a boat in which a man and a woman sat with their arms round each other, drifting wherever the water took them; then another in which people were singing at the tops of their voices. And that was all.

Lupin moved closer to his companion and said in a low voice:

“Tell me, Gilbert, was it you who thought of this job, or Vaucheray?”

“I must say, I hardly know… we’ve both been talking about it for weeks.”

“The thing is, I don’t trust Vaucheray… A nasty character… underhand… I wonder why I don’t rid myself of him…”

“Oh, chief!”

“I mean it, I mean it. He’s a dangerous customer… and he must have one or two rather serious little sins on his conscience, besides.”

He was silent a moment, then went on:

“So you’re quite sure you saw the deputy Daubrecq?”

“With my own eyes, chief.”

“And you know he has an appointment in Paris?”

“He’s going to the theatre.”

“Good, but his servants have stayed behind at his villa at Enghien…”

“The cook has been sent away. As for the valet, Léonard, who is the deputy Daubrecq’s confidential man, he is waiting for his master in Paris, and they can’t be back before one in the morning. But…”

“But?”

“But we have to allow for a possible whim of Daubrecq’s, a change of mood, an unexpected return, and to make our arrangements accordingly so that everything is finished within the hour.”

“And you have all this on good authority?”

“Since this morning. Vaucheray and I thought at once that the moment was a good one. I chose as our starting point the garden of that house under construction which we have just left, and which is not watched at night. I told two friends to take charge of the boats, and I telephoned you. That’s the whole story.”

“You have the keys?”

“The ones for the front door.”

“Is that the villa down there, the one standing in its own grounds?”

“Yes, the Villa Marie-Thérèse, and as the two others whose gardens hem it in have been empty for a week, we have all the time we need to move out whatever we please, and I promise you, chief, it’s worth the trouble.”

Lupin muttered:

“Far too easy, this adventure. No charm about it.”

They came in at a little creek from which, under the shelter of a worm-eaten roof, a few stone steps rose. Lupin judged that shifting the furniture would be an easy matter. But he said suddenly:

“There’s somebody at the villa. Look… a light.”

“It’s a gas jet, chief… the light isn’t moving…”

Grognard stayed with the boats, to keep watch, while Le Ballu, the other oarsman, made his way to the gate on the Avenue de Ceinture, and Lupin and his two companions crawled through the shadows to the foot of the front steps.

Gilbert went up first. Feeling his way, he put in the key of the lock, then the key of the safety bolt. Both turned easily, so that the door could be pushed ajar and let the three men through.

In the hall a gas jet was burning.

“You see, chief…” said Gilbert.

“Yes, yes…” said Lupin, in a low voice, “but it seems to me that the light I saw wasn’t coming from there.”

“From where, then?”

“I’m sure I don’t know… Is the drawing room this way?”

“No,” answered Gilbert, who was not afraid to speak a little louder, “no, as a precaution he has brought everything together on the first floor, in his bedroom and the rooms next to it.”

“And the staircase?”

“On the right, behind the curtain.”

Lupin made for the curtain, and he was already drawing the stuff aside when all at once, four paces to his left, a door opened and a head appeared, the head of a man gone white, with terrified eyes.

“Help! Murder!” he screamed.

And he darted back into the room.

“It’s Léonard! The servant!” cried Gilbert.

“If he makes trouble, I’ll drop him,” growled Vaucheray.

“You’ll leave us in peace, Vaucheray, do you hear?” ordered Lupin, who had already sprung after the servant.

He crossed a dining room first, where there were still plates and a bottle beside a lamp, and he found Léonard at the far end of a pantry, struggling in vain to open the window.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix.
