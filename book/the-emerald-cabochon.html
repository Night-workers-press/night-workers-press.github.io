The bookLe livre

The book Leblanc planned and never published. In the early 1930s he meant to gather, under the title Le Cabochon d'émeraude, the Lupin stories that had appeared in magazines and never been collected; the book was never made. Here they are together, under the title he chose: five stories, among them one that Leblanc wrote twice, the second time in English for an American weekly and with a different ending, and both versions are printed. The Travelling Case, four pages from 1921, appears in English here for the first time. With an introduction on where each story came from.

Le livre que Leblanc avait projeté et n'a jamais publié. Au début des années 1930, il voulait réunir chez Hachette, sous le titre Le Cabochon d'émeraude, les nouvelles de Lupin parues en revue et jamais recueillies ; le livre ne se fit pas. Les voici ensemble, sous le titre qu'il avait choisi : cinq nouvelles, dont une que Leblanc écrivit deux fois, la seconde en anglais pour un hebdomadaire américain et avec une autre fin, et les deux versions sont données. Le Coffret de voyage, quatre pages de 1921, paraît ici en anglais pour la première fois. Avec une introduction sur l'origine de chaque texte.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

The Tooth of Hercule Petitgris — 1924

Hands behind his back, his neck sunk into his collar, his whole harsh face drawn tight with thought, Jean Rouxval strode back and forth across his vast ministerial office, while the head usher waited at the door for his orders. A furrow of anxiety marked his forehead. Jerky gestures escaped him, betraying the extreme agitation with which certain dramatic minutes of life shake a man.

Stopping short, he said in a decided tone:

“A gentleman and a lady of a certain age will call. Show them into the red drawing room. Then a gentleman will come alone, a younger man, whom you will take to the great hall. They are not to speak to one another or see one another, is that understood? And you will tell me at once.”

“Very good, Minister.”

Jean Rouxval’s political standing rested on solid qualities of energy and hard-working intelligence. The war, in which he had served from the first day to avenge his two sons, lost, and his wife, dead of grief, had given him a sense of discipline, authority and duty that was at times excessive. In every affair events drew him into, he took on as much responsibility as he possibly could, and on the strength of it claimed as many rights as he possibly could. He loved his country with a kind of held-in frenzy, which made acts that were often arbitrary look to him just and permissible. These qualities earned him the esteem of his colleagues, and a certain mistrust as well, provoked by the very excess of them. There was always the fear that he would drag the cabinet into needless trouble.

He looked at his watch. Twenty to five. He still had time to glance through the file on the dreadful business that was causing him so much anxiety. But at that moment the telephone rang. He picked up the receiver. They wanted him on the line from the Prime Minister’s office.

He waited. It took some time. At last he was put through, and he answered:

“Yes, it is I, my dear Prime Minister.”

He listened, seemed put out, and said in a slightly bitter tone:

“Good heavens, Prime Minister, of course I shall see the agent you have just sent me. But do you not think I should have got at the certainties we want on my own?… Well, since you insist, my dear Prime Minister, and since this Hercule Petitgris is, as you put it, a specialist in the matter of inquiry, he shall be there at the confrontation I have arranged… Hello?… You are right, my dear Prime Minister, the whole thing is extremely grave, above all because of certain rumours that are beginning to go about… If I do not reach an immediate solution, and if the truth turns out to be what we fear, it will be a frightful scandal and a disaster for the country… Hello… Yes, yes, set your mind at rest, my dear Prime Minister, I shall move heaven and earth to succeed… And I shall succeed… It has to be…”

A few more words were exchanged; then Rouxval hung up and repeated between his teeth:

“Yes… It has to be… It has to be… A scandal like that…”

He was turning over the means that might get him there when he had the feeling that somebody was standing near him, somebody who was not trying to be noticed.

He turned his head and stood dumbfounded. Four paces away stood a man of fairly wretched appearance, what people call a poor devil, and this poor devil was holding his hat in his hand in the humble attitude of a beggar waiting for a coin.

“What are you doing there? How did you get in?”

“By the door, Minister… Your usher was busy penning people up to the right and the left. I walked straight through.”

“But who are you?”

The man lowered his head respectfully and introduced himself:

“Hercule Petitgris… the ‘speshalist’ the Prime Minister has just announced to you, Minster, sir…”

“Ah! So you were listening?” said Rouxval, annoyed.

“What would you have done in my place, Minister?”

He was a puny, pitiable creature, and everything about his sad face drooped: the hair, the moustache, the nose, the thin cheeks, the corners of the mouth. His arms hung wearily at the sides of a greenish overcoat that seemed not to be attached to his shoulders. He spoke in a desolate voice, with a certain care, but mangling a syllable here and there the way common people do. He said “Minster, sir” for Minister, and “y’usher” for your usher.

“I even heard, Minster, sir,” he went on, “that you spoke of me as an agent. A mistake! I’m no agent of anything at all, having been dismissed from the Prefecture for ‘insipid character, drunkenness and idleness’. I quote the wording of my striking-off.”

Rouxval could not hide his astonishment.

“I don’t understand. The Prime Minister recommends you to me as a capable man, with a disconcerting clear-sightedness.”

“Disconcerting, Minster, sir, that’s the very word, and that’s why these gentlemen are willing to use me on the cases where nobody’s succeeded or could succeed, and without holding my little habits against me. What do you expect, I’m no worker. I like to drink my fill, and I’ve a weakness for a game of manille. As for character, that don’t count. Mere trifles. They reproach me with being vain and insolent to my employers? And what of it? When they’re floundering and I can see clear, have I not the right to tell ’em so and have a bit of a laugh? Why, Minster, sir, more than once I’ve turned down money to keep the right to laugh out loud. They’re so comical at that moment! The faces they pull!…”

In his drooping face, under the melancholy moustache, the left corner of his mouth curled up in a small silent laugh that bared an immense canine tooth, the canine of a wild animal. For a second or two it gave him a look of sardonic delight. With a tooth like that, the man would bite to the bone.

Rouxval was not afraid of being bitten. But the fellow gave him no confidence, and he would have got rid of him in all haste had the Prime Minister not pressed him on him so hard.

“Sit down,” he said gruffly. “I am going to question and confront three people who are here. If you have any observation to make, you will make it to me directly.”

“Directly, Minster, sir, and under my breath, as is my habit when the superior’s floundering…”

Rouxval frowned. In the first place he hated anyone who failed to keep his distance. And then, like many men of action, he had a very keen sense and fear of the ridiculous. Applied to himself, this word “floundering” struck him as at once an intolerable insult and a deliberate threat. But he had already rung, and the usher was coming in. Without waiting, he gave the order for the three people to be shown in.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
