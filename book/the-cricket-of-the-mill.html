Chapter One

The Mill Called Brin-d’Amour

The Cricket was a girl.

And this girl was trotting along with her feet in the dew, a little before sunrise, down the path that crosses the meadows and runs from the mill to the village. You will never see a prettier path, or greener meadows, or a more talkative mill, or a more countrified village, or a girl fresher, trimmer and more adorably pretty than the Cricket.

The mill stood in a fold of the valley, a little over half a mile from the Loire, hard beside the village they call Férolles-les-Prés. And they are right to call it that, for you may look as long as you like without finding a ploughed field or a vineyard anywhere near it. The place is ringed with green pasture, and the pasture is bordered with tall, melancholy poplars.

The mill itself lies right at the far end, behind the church tower, at the foot of the first slope that closes the valley. The stream that turns it has no name on any map, not even on the map of the department. It is a noisy little brook that comes out of the sands of the Sologne, and its water tastes faintly of pine resin; but it is clear for all that, limpid, and it flashes like cut glass wherever a ray of sun works its way through the willows growing along both banks.

The mill does have a name. They call it Brin-d’Amour. Why? The schoolmaster, who believes himself learned, and the parish priest, who is learned a little, could tell you no more about it than I can, and the oldest men in the district know exactly as much as the schoolmaster. The mill is called Brin-d’Amour because it has no other name.

Now at that time — say eight or nine years ago, for this story is quite a fresh one — the miller’s widow at Brin-d’Amour was a very handsome woman not quite forty, who could easily have owned to no more than thirty had it not been common knowledge that she had a grown son, and that he had drawn in the conscription ballot a good while since.

Mother Suzon, as everybody called her, had married at fifteen and been widowed at nineteen. She never married again.

It was certainly not for want of offers. If you had set her suitors in a line, one behind another, they would have made a fine procession — long enough to go from Férolles to Châteauneuf hand in hand.

To begin with, Mother Suzon was prettier, fresher, whiter of skin than the finest ladies in town. She had blue eyes that looked brown, hair as black as jet, a small tip-tilted nose full of mischief and kindness at the same time, and white teeth in an even row; and when she laughed, which was often, you would have said the good Lord had opened a corner of his paradise and set the angels playing hide-and-seek in it. Her waist was on the thick side, it is true — but where is the harm in that? Wasp waists are not grown in the fields, and they do not always bring happiness in the towns.

Besides, in the matter of money, Mother Suzon was very nearly a lady. There were forty acres of good land all round the mill without a penny of mortgage on them, and the mill was the first in the district as it was also the prettiest.

Down on the Loire, when you ask to be shown a mill, they point you to a wooden pepper-pot turning on a post, worked by the wind. When there is no wind there is no flour, and without flour how do you make bread? As for that devil of a river they call the Loire, it only gives you water when it is in flood: either it leaves you to die of thirst or it drowns you.

Brin-d’Amour, then, was the exception. Brin-d’Amour was a water mill, and the little brook turned it in every season — a model mill, going tick-tack night and day, grinding more grain on its own than all those ugly sheds perched on their anthills that never manage to brighten the dreary country they look down on.

With a dowry like that, how could Mother Suzon have failed to marry again, and marry easily, if she had had the least fancy for it? People even said that a ruined nobleman had asked for her. But people say so many things.

What was certain was that Mother Suzon had stayed a widow, and had gathered all her affection on to her son Laurent and her niece Noémi. Noémi was fourteen when Laurent drew his number.

Laurent was a fine-looking lad, quick, well set up, a hard worker and good-natured with it. He had his mother’s small feet and small hands, her blue eyes and her black hair, and with a little trouble taken over his clothes on a Sunday he cut such a figure that he could have played cock of the village. Noémi was a sharp little blonde, bright and quick and full of laughter like her aunt, so dainty you would have taken her for a fairy out of the woods; and as she brought the orphan up, Mother Suzon used to smile to herself and think, What a pretty daughter-in-law I shall have there one day.

But there. Mother proposes and son disposes.

One evening in March of the year 185—, Laurent came into the mill with a spray of coloured ribbons in his hat. It was the evening of the ballot. Mother Suzon began to laugh, and Noémi, the little imp, laughed louder still, for both of them supposed he was playing a joke on them. That very morning he had drawn a good number. Why should he be playing the conscript?

Then, having laughed, the two women suddenly began to cry. Laurent really was a conscript. He meant to go in another man’s place.

The other man was his foster-brother, a thorough young scamp whose parents were worth even less than he was. But the boy’s mother had nursed Laurent; Laurent was fond of his foster-brother, and when he had seen the young man’s number come up he had agreed to serve for him.

The harm was not past mending, all the same. There was good money at the mill, and buying a substitute would hardly ruin Mother Suzon for the sake of her scatterbrained son. But Laurent wanted to go. He threw his arms round his mother’s neck, drew her aside and said, “Let me go. For one thing, I’ll see something of the world. If I find I can’t bear being away from you, I’ll write and tell you, and you can buy me out. And then, you see, I’m wildly in love with Noémi, and she’s only fourteen, and there’s no thinking about that for another two years.”

And in spite of everything, Laurent went.

He had been with the colours two years by now, which means that Noémi was sixteen on the day our story begins. And now that you know the name of the mill, and the name of the miller’s widow, and the story of her son, let us follow the Cricket — that is to say Noémi — as she goes down to Férolles-les-Prés with a light step, one September morning, at the sound of the Angelus, caring very little that she is wetting her small feet in the lucerne that has overrun the path.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
