Don Juan's Last Night

La dernière nuit de Don Juan

A dramatic poem in two parts and a prologue (1921)

The bookLe livre

The last thing Rostand wrote, published after his death. Don Juan has bargained ten more years out of the Commander; when they run out, the Devil arrives as a puppet-master and offers to show him his conquests again. The joke turns cruel: Don Juan cannot remember one of them apart. A short, savage dramatic poem about a man who spent his life collecting people and discovers he never met any of them — the anti-Cyrano, written by the man who invented panache.

Le dernier texte de Rostand, publié après sa mort. Don Juan a arraché dix années de sursis au Commandeur ; le délai expiré, le Diable se présente en montreur de marionnettes et propose de lui repasser ses conquêtes. La plaisanterie tourne au cruel : Don Juan ne parvient à en distinguer aucune. Un poème dramatique bref et féroce sur un homme qui a passé sa vie à collectionner des êtres et découvre qu'il n'en a jamais rencontré un seul — l'anti-Cyrano, par l'inventeur du panache.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

PROLOGUE

Nothing is visible but a narrow, dimly lit staircase whose spiral is lost above and which plunges down into a chasm. A green, sulphurous glare splashes the lower steps.

When the curtain rises, the Statue of the Commander appears, coming down with a heavy tread. It holds by the arm Don Juan, magnificently calm.

DON JUAN

Let go of my wrist; I can walk down by myself.

(He recites a name at each step.)

Ninon... Laure... Agnès... Jeanne...

(A dog is heard whining. Don Juan listens.)

Ah, there — my spaniel

crying for me. He was an admirable animal,

sir.

(He goes on down.)

Armande... Elvire...

(He stops.)

Ah, allow us to stop,

and, my lord Commander, allow me — lending, if you please,

an ear to the voice of the faithful servant

who used to hold such honest language to me up there —

to hear the cry of his grief.

THE VOICE OF SGANARELLE, from above

My wages!

DON JUAN, to the Statue

Might I go back up, sir, for a few moments,

to pay him what I owe him?

THE STATUE

Yes. I'll wait.

DON JUAN

A thousand thanks.

THE STATUE, alone

Will he come back?

DON JUAN, coming down again

There — we're quits.

He has the kick in the backside he deserves.

THE STATUE

You came back?

DON JUAN

It did me good.

Ah, I shall burn the better for it.

THE STATUE

You are afraid of nothing,

Don Juan. And my old cuirass-bearer's heart

is susceptible to courage. Come — I pardon you.

Go back up.

DON JUAN

You should have told me sooner.

But I feel something snatching at the bottom of my cloak.

A claw is settling on the brocade hem.

It's too late.

Monsieur the Devil, I presume?

THE STATUE

Don Juan, day is about to break, and that metal cry

obliges me to get back to my pedestal already.

Try to get yourself out of that Claw.

DON JUAN

Certainly.

But kindly leave the tomb open on your way out.

Let's talk, Claw. It isn't altogether displeasing to you, at bottom,

that this excellent marble has deigned to let me go.

Grant me five years? Or ten? Ten, I'd prefer.

I have a fair amount of harm left to do up there.

Ah, that decides you? Between ourselves, let's agree

that I still have very few names on my list.

It's worth your while making a pact with me, Claw.

I'm the one who makes the Act be committed most;

the best beater in your hunt. And besides —

come, now, let go of that cloak! — I'm

something other than a Doctor Faustus, who asks

for nothing but a nice little German working girl,

and who, cut to the heart at having, like a fool, got her with child,

calls in an Angel to defend him at the last curtain!

The spectre's fingers have marked my arm with five flames.

I should rather like to show that tattoo to the women!

Let go of that scrap of cloth, my lord, and I'll go far.

More than one Spanish Infanta's sleep is in need

of my going to trouble it inside its white mosquito net.

Being the corrupter, I am your vicar.

But do let go!

At last! Ten years will be enough.

Your Grace will come and fetch me in ten years.

Let it count on me: I am counting on it.

Rose... Lise... Angélique... Armande...

Hey! Sganarelle!

PART ONE

Ten years later. A palace in Venice. A great hall open onto the Adriatic, with marble steps going down into it. In the middle, a table laid and lit by candelabra.

SCENE I

DON JUAN, SGANARELLE

DON JUAN

Arabella... Lucinde... Isabelle... Isabeau...

SGANARELLE

The ten years are up, sir.

DON JUAN

What beautiful weather!

I've just come from the Grand Canal.

SGANARELLE

Oh?

DON JUAN

On the pink and brown water

every boat trails a carpet, and the lagoon,

like a Potiphar's wife watching a cloak escape her,

seems to hold the boat back by its carpet.

But in this deserted corner the green water, more secretive,

sleeps under a sky of sulphur and turquoise,

the way a sea-green virtue sleeps before I pass.

I have always had a taste for still water. Do you know

why the Adriatic interests me so much?

SGANARELLE

No.

DON JUAN

She's married.

SGANARELLE

Oh?

DON JUAN

She is the Doge's wife.

The Doge is her husband; I am her lover.

I'm the one who understands you, Lagoon!

SGANARELLE

Obviously!

DON JUAN

I want to throw her a ring too — with the left hand! —

so that these waters may go to the bad along with me.

SGANARELLE, in horror

The ruby?

DON JUAN

No. The glass one.

SGANARELLE

Ah?

DON JUAN

Yes.

SGANARELLE

Hers?...

The one from — ? But then?...

DON JUAN

Yes.

SGANARELLE

Finished?... Old?... Over?...

DON JUAN

Venice!... Ah, this is the city of the fragile.

The column is stucco, the stone is lace,

the wall is mirror, and the street is water!

And when two lovers exchange a ring,

that ring, Sganarelle, has the wit to be made of glass!

SGANARELLE

The ten years are up, and you —

DON JUAN

I persevere.

SGANARELLE

Tonight?

DON JUAN

A ball.

SGANARELLE

You'll come home?

DON JUAN

No. Better than Hannibal,

I take advantage of the victory — after the ball!

SGANARELLE

Sir, if the hour comes, so much fine insolence —

DON JUAN

When you speak of the hour, it strikes.

SGANARELLE

Oh!

DON JUAN

Quiet!

Let's listen to it coming loose from the campanile.

SGANARELLE

Is the pleasure of calling a church tower a campanile

worth loitering for ever under this sky, sir?

DON JUAN

I like the white shoes of the girls of Venice,

and having for a go-between a gondolier

who sings, makes verses and grows familiar.

The ladies of Venice use a cedar bath

that would put Hippolytus at Phaedra's mercy!

Venice is a place full of opportunities:

regattas, balls... and processions.

I love Venice! And besides, its lion resembles me —

the lion at whose foot a flight of doves gathers,

and who gives up, with a great bitter disdain,

reigning over the sea in order to reign over love!

Yes — like you, mad and profound City, wanting

to live on my own reflection, I built on water!

SGANARELLE

This city is deadly.

DON JUAN

And even if you were,

city where all the adventurers come to end,

who want, in dying, to break the finest glass of all —

I refuse to run away under a sterner sky.

A city of love saw my first day;

my last day must see a city of love.

Only one epitaph is permitted to Don Juan:

"He was born in Seville and died in Venice!"

What I say of it, in any case, is only to frighten you:

my opinion is that the Devil must have forgotten us!

SGANARELLE

Us!

DON JUAN

No — you aren't included, that's true. You inherit!

SGANARELLE

Ah! Inherit what?

DON JUAN

Having been close to me. Your merits

will carry more conclusive weight among great lords

when you say: "I come from Monsieur Don Juan's household!"

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
