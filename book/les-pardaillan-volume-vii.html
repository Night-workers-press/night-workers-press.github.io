From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I

Paris — Henry IV on the throne of a France at peace — a bright May morning, warm with sunlight.

The window of a modest bourgeois house on the rue de l’Arbre-Sec swings open. A young woman appears on the balcony. The warm rays of the sun settle like an impalpable dust of gold upon the golden cloud of her abundant hair. Eyes bluer and purer than the blazing azure sky; a slender figure; forms of incomparable harmony; an artless dignity in every posture; a frank and admirable directness of gaze; a veil of melancholy spread across that snow-white brow — all of her compels attention and holds it, all of her charms and captivates.

As though drawn by some irresistible force, her lovely head lifts, timidly, furtively, toward the house across the way.

Up there, at the garret dormer, a young cavalier appears. His hands clasped, an air of rapture about him, he fixes upon her a deep gaze laden with mute adoration.

The girl flushes, pales… her chaste breast rises with emotion… She holds his gaze for one instant, then slowly, as though reluctant, retreats indoors and draws the shutter closed.

Below, in the street, a poor wretch has taken shelter in the shadow of a doorway and raises toward that radiant apparition a gaunt, ravaged ascetic’s face, from beneath whose overhanging brows two glassy visionary eyes gleam.

At the sight of that gracious girl, those mad eyes quicken, grow human, take on an expression of tender mystical gentleness. That sombre countenance lights with a celestial joy. And the poor wretch, too, joins his hands in a gesture of supplication and murmurs:

— How beautiful she is!…

As he pronounces these words, something shapeless — a heap, an enormous ball of fat — rolls up from nowhere with surprising agility and halts before the man at prayer. It is swathed in a monk’s frock carelessly hitched up on one hip, surmounted by a second small jovial ball outrageously ruddy of complexion. Two basset-hound legs, short and bandy-kneed, serve as columns; two immense flat feet form the solid pediment of this monument of flesh. And it speaks — in a bass voice that seems to rise from some unknown depth — and with no mockery in its tone:

— Caught you again, Brother Ravaillac!… Lost in your dark visions, as ever!

Brutally dragged from his reverie, Ravaillac — Jean-François Ravaillac — starts violently. His features resume their absent expression; the spark of life kindled in his eye is abruptly extinguished; and, dropping his gaze to the ground, without apparent vexation, without surprise, without pleasure, with a dull indifference, he says softly, politely:

— Good day, Brother Parfait Goulard.

At that moment the young woman closes her window without having taken the curiosity to glance below.

Ravaillac sighs, and without affectation moves off in the direction of the nearby rue Saint-Honoré, drawing Brother Parfait Goulard with him — that worthy being delighted at the encounter and lending himself willingly to the manoeuvre.

The monk, however, has caught a sidelong glimpse of the girl. He has noted the sigh of the man he called Brother Ravaillac. But he lets nothing show, and his great good-natured face remains perfectly merry.

As they go, they pass a personage who must be some powerful lord, to judge by his haughty bearing and the richness of his costume.

This lord is haggling sharply with a respectable matron who has every appearance of a small bourgeoise.

Passing near the monk, the brilliant lord sketches a furtive gesture, to which the monk responds with a wink.

Neither the worthy matron nor Ravaillac notices this exchange of mysterious signals.

The great lord and the bourgeoise continue on their way and come to a stop before the steps of the young woman’s little house. They go on haggling with animation, and neither of them takes heed of a shadow crouched in a doorway nearby, which — though they speak in low voices — misses not a word of their exchange.

The young cavalier had remained at his dormer window, leaning on his elbows.

Perhaps he was savouring his happiness. Perhaps he was waiting patiently for some fortunate chance to grant him one more glimpse of a ribbon’s edge, or the shadow of his beloved silhouetted against the panes… Lovers, as is well known, are insatiable. This one, wholly absorbed in his dreams, saw nothing beyond the balcony where she had appeared to him.

Below that balcony, however, their discussion apparently concluded, the matron had mounted the three steps and was fitting her key into the lock.

By chance, the lover’s eyes left the blessed balcony for an instant and fell upon the street. A cry of anger escaped him at the sight of the lord who had not moved:

— That accursed ruffian Fouquet, again!…

He leaned out so far one might have thought him about to plunge head-first into the street. And he gnashed:

— What is he doing there, at her door?… Whom is he thus soliciting?…

For indeed, at that moment, the man our lover had just named Fouquet was calling to the matron who was about to enter the house. She came down one step and extended her hand. A farewell gesture?… A bargain sealed?… Earnest money given?… That he could not have said. He thought he glimpsed a purse… But the gesture had been so swift, the sleight-of-hand so deft!… In any case, he knew the matron — for, withdrawing sharply from the window, he was white as chalk and stuttering:

— Dame Colline Colle!… Ah! by all the demons of hell, I will know the truth!… Woe to the damned Fouquet!…

And he hurled himself like a whirlwind down the stairs.

At that precise moment, three bravos came to a halt before his door. They had the swagger of swashbucklers born, with formidable rapiers clanking at their heels. To look at them, one divined devil-may-care desperadoes who feared nothing and no one. And yet they stood irresolute before the door, not daring to lift the knocker.

— Eh vé! said one of them, with a Provençal accent, go on then, Gringaille — you’re a Parisian, you can talk your way through anything…

— Indeed! replied the one addressed. Your own tongue is no less ready, Escargasse… Still, it strikes me that Carcagne there is the likeliest of us three to carry it off with any honour… He has such amiable manners, such polish!…

The man of polished manners said in his turn:

— A singular pair of scoundrels you are, to want to expose me alone to the chief’s wrath… Do you not know, you worthless rogues, that he has expressly forbidden us to present ourselves at his door without his leave?… Do you think I am minded to be flung out of the window merely to preserve your damned hides?…

— We must nonetheless let him know that Signor Concini wishes to see him this very day.

— May the plague strangle that one! Much need he had to saddle us with such an errand!

— Eh vé! Let us go together.

— At least there will be three of us to receive the shower.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
