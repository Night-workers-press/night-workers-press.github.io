The bookLe livre

Remi is eight when he learns that the woman who raised him is not his mother. Her husband comes home from Paris crippled and penniless and hands the foundling over, for forty francs, to a travelling showman: old Vitalis, with three performing dogs and a little monkey in a general's red coat. From then on the book is a journey: on foot across France, down a canal, through a winter on the roads, into a coal mine on the day the water breaks in, and at last to London. English readers have mostly known it in a 1916 translation that cut Malot's forty-two chapters to thirty-three. This is the whole of it.

Rémi a huit ans quand il apprend que la femme qui l'a élevé n'est pas sa mère. Son mari revient de Paris estropié et sans le sou, et cède l'enfant trouvé, pour quarante francs, à un montreur ambulant : le vieux Vitalis, ses trois chiens savants et un petit singe en habit rouge de général. Dès lors le livre est un voyage : à pied à travers la France, sur un canal, par un hiver sur les routes, dans une mine de charbon le jour où l'eau fait irruption, et enfin jusqu'à Londres. Le lectorat anglophone l'a surtout connu dans une traduction de 1916 qui ramenait les quarante-deux chapitres de Malot à trente-trois. Voici le livre entier.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — In the Village

I am a foundling.

But until I was eight, I believed that I had a mother like all other children; for whenever I cried, there was a woman who held me so gently in her arms, rocking me, that my tears would stop.

I never went to bed without a woman coming to kiss me goodnight; and when the December wind plastered the snow against the frosted windowpanes, she would take my feet between her two hands and sit there warming them, singing me a song whose tune, and a few of its words, I can still remember.

When I quarrelled with one of my playmates, she would get me to tell her all my troubles, and she nearly always found kind words to comfort me or to take my side.

Because of all this, and a great deal more besides—the way she spoke to me, the way she looked at me, her caresses, the gentleness she put even into her scoldings—I believed that she was my mother.

This is how I found out that she was only my foster mother.

My village—or, to put it more accurately, the village where I was brought up, for I never had a village of my own, no birthplace, any more than I had a father and mother—the village, in short, where I spent my childhood is called Chavanon. It is one of the poorest in the centre of France.

It owes its poverty not to any sluggishness or idleness in the people who live there, but to its very situation, in a region where the land is poor. The soil has no depth, and to yield good harvests it would need manure or other dressings, which are not to be had in those parts. So there are few cultivated fields to be seen there (or at least there were few in the days I am speaking of).

The house where I spent my early years stands in a fold of the land, on the bank of a stream whose quick waters run down to lose themselves in one of the tributaries of the Loire.

Until I was eight, I had never seen a man in that house. Yet my mother was not a widow. Her husband was a stonemason, like a great many other working men from those parts, and he worked in Paris; he had not been home since I was old enough to see or understand what went on around me. Only now and then did he send news of himself, by one of his workmates who was coming back to the village.

"Mother Barberin, your man is well. He asked me to tell you that work is going strong, and to give you this money here. Will you count it?"

And that was all. Mother Barberin was content with such news: her man was in good health; there was plenty of work; he was earning his living.

You must not suppose, because Barberin had stayed so long in Paris, that he was on bad terms with his wife. No quarrel had anything to do with his absence. He lived in Paris because his work kept him there; that was all. When he was old, he would come back to live with his old wife, and the money they had put by would keep them from want when the years had taken away their strength and their health.

One November day, as evening was falling, a man I did not know stopped at our gate. I was on the doorstep, breaking up a bundle of brushwood. Without pushing the gate open, but raising his head to look at me over the top of it, the man asked whether this was where Mother Barberin lived.

I told him to come in.

At the sound of our voices Mother Barberin came hurrying out, and just as he was stepping over our threshold, she came face to face with him.

"I've brought news from Paris," he said.

They were very simple words, and we had heard them more than once before; but the tone in which he said them was nothing like the tone that used to go with the words "Your man is well, work is going strong."

"Oh, God!" cried Mother Barberin, clasping her hands. "Something has happened to Jérôme!"

"Well, yes, it has, but you mustn't make yourself ill with fright. Your man has been hurt, that's the truth of it; but he isn't dead. He may be crippled, though. For now he's in hospital. I was in the bed next to his, and as I was coming home, he asked me to stop by and tell you."

Mother Barberin wanted to know more, and she asked the man to stay to supper.

He sat down in the chimney corner and, while he ate, told us how the accident had happened. Barberin had been half crushed when some scaffolding collapsed; and because it had been proved that he had no business being where he was when he was hurt, the builder was refusing to pay him any compensation at all.

"All the same," he said as he finished his story, "I've advised him to take the builder to court."

"Going to law costs a lot of money."

"Yes, but think of it when you win!"

Mother Barberin would have liked to go to Paris, but so long and costly a journey was a fearful undertaking.

The next morning we went down to the village to ask the priest's advice. He would not let her go without first finding out whether she could be of any use to her husband. He wrote to the chaplain of the hospital where Barberin was being looked after, and a few days later he received an answer: Mother Barberin was not to make the journey, but she should send her husband a sum of money, because he was going to bring a lawsuit against the builder he had been working for when he was hurt.

The days went by, and then the weeks, and every so often letters came, and every one of them asked for more money to be sent. The last, more pressing than the rest, said that if there was no money left, the cow must be sold to raise some.

Only those who have lived in the country among peasants know how much distress and grief there is in those three words: "sell the cow."

To the naturalist, a cow is a ruminant. To the rambler, she is a creature that looks well in the landscape when she lifts her black muzzle, wet with dew, above the grass. To the town child, she is where milky coffee and cream cheese come from. But to the peasant she is much more than that, and much better. However poor he may be, and however large his family, he can be sure of never going hungry so long as there is a cow in his shed. With a halter, or even a simple twist of willow knotted round her horns, a child leads the cow along the grassy lanes where the grazing belongs to nobody, and in the evening the whole family has butter in its soup and milk to moisten its potatoes. Father, mother, children, big and small alike: everybody lives off the cow.

Hector Malot (1830–1907)

Hector Malot was a Norman, born near Rouen, who wrote for grown-ups and children alike. Sans Famille (1878) has been read by French children ever since it appeared; Romain Kalbris (1869) is the novel in which, nine years earlier, he first sent a boy out alone on the roads of France. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

Hector Malot était un Normand, né près de Rouen, qui écrivit pour les adultes comme pour les enfants. Sans famille (1878) est lu par les enfants de France depuis sa parution ; Romain Kalbris (1869) est le roman où, neuf ans plus tôt, il lançait pour la première fois un garçon seul sur les routes de France. Les classiques en anglais moderne donnent en entier, dans l'anglais d'aujourd'hui, les classiques français que le lecteur anglophone ne connaît qu'en traductions anciennes ou abrégées. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
