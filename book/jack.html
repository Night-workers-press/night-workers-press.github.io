The bookLe livre

Paris, December 1858. Ida de Barancy, a kept woman who calls herself a countess, wants to place her illegitimate son with the Jesuits; they will take him only if his mother keeps out of sight, so she sends him instead to a shabby boarding school off the Champs-Élysées, where his only friend is Madou, a deposed African boy king turned school drudge. Then she falls in love with the school's literature master, a poet no publisher will touch, who hates the child on sight and decides that the dreamy, bookish boy shall become a workman. Daudet dedicated Jack to Flaubert as a book of pity, of anger and of irony, and it is all three. Not a children's book.

Paris, décembre 1858. Ida de Barancy, femme entretenue qui se fait appeler comtesse, veut placer son fils naturel chez les jésuites ; ils ne le prendront que si la mère se tient à l'écart, et elle l'envoie donc dans un pensionnat miteux près des Champs-Élysées, où son seul ami est Madou, petit roi africain détrôné devenu domestique de l'école. Puis elle s'éprend du professeur de littérature de la pension, un poète qu'aucun éditeur ne veut, qui prend l'enfant en haine au premier regard et décide que ce garçon rêveur et liseur sera ouvrier. Daudet a dédié Jack à Flaubert comme un livre de pitié, de colère et d'ironie, et il est tout cela. Ce n'est pas un livre pour enfants.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Part One — Chapter I — Mother and Child

“With a K, Father Superior, with a K! Jack, not Jacques. The name is spelt and pronounced the English way… like this: Djack… The child’s godfather was English, a major general in the Indian Army… Lord Peambock… Perhaps you know him? A most distinguished man, of the very highest nobility, oh, but you know, Monsieur l’Abbé, the very highest… And what a waltzer!… He died, as it happens, in the most dreadful way, at Singapore, a few years ago, in a magnificent tiger hunt that a rajah friend of his had got up in his honour… They’re real monarchs, it seems, these rajahs… That one especially is very famous out there… Now what is his name?… wait a moment… Good heavens! I have it on the tip of my tongue… Rana… Rama…”

“Forgive me, madam,” the Rector broke in, smiling in spite of himself at such volubility and at this perpetual skipping from one idea to the next… “And after Jack, what are we to put?”

Leaning on his elbows on the desk where he had been writing a moment before, his head tilted a little to one side, the worthy priest was studying, out of the corner of an eye sharpened by mischief and ecclesiastical shrewdness, the young woman who sat before him, with her Jack (with a K) standing at her side.

She was an elegant creature, faultlessly dressed in the height of fashion for the day and the season—it was December 1858—and in the softness of her furs, the richness of her black gown and the quiet originality of her hat there was even the easy luxury of a woman who keeps her own carriage, and passes from her spotless carpets to the cushions of her brougham without having to suffer the humdrum interlude of the street.

She had a very small head, which always makes a woman look taller, and a pretty face, downy as a fruit, lively and smiling, lit up by a pair of clear, guileless eyes and by very white teeth, which she showed at every opportunity. Her features were extraordinarily mobile, and something in that pleasant face—perhaps the lower lip, a little slack from a perpetual need to talk, perhaps the narrow forehead under the glossy bands of her hair—suggested an absence of reflection, a rather limited mind, and explained the parentheses this pretty creature was forever opening in her conversation, like those little Japanese baskets so exactly graded in size that each one fits inside the next, and the last of which is always empty.

As for the child, just picture a little fellow of seven or eight, lanky, shot up too fast, dressed in the English style, as the K in his name of Jack required: bare legs, a bonnet with a silver thistle, and a plaid. The costume may have suited his age, but it seemed at odds with his long frame and his neck, which was already sturdy. His muscular calves, blue with cold, stuck out on every side of his ridiculous get-up in a clumsy spurt of rebellious growth. He was embarrassed by it himself. Awkward and shy, his eyes lowered, every now and then he cast a despairing glance at his bare legs, as though in his heart he were cursing Lord Peambock and the whole Indian Army for getting him rigged out like this.

In looks he took after his mother, but with something finer, more distinguished about him: the whole transformation of a pretty woman’s face into the face of an intelligent man. He had the same eyes, but with more depth in them; the same forehead, but broader; the same mouth, but set tighter by a more serious expression.

Across the woman’s face, ideas and impressions slid by without leaving a trace or a line, in such a hurry, each chased away so quickly by the next, that her eyes always seemed to keep a look of surprise at their flight. In the child, on the contrary, you could feel that thought had come to stay; and his rather too thoughtful air might even have been worrying, had it not gone with a certain listlessness of posture, a languor in his whole small being, the coaxing, timid ways of a boy brought up in his mother’s skirts.

At that moment, leaning against her, one hand slipped into her muff, he was listening to her talk, full of silent admiration, and every so often he looked at the priest and at everything around him with a curious, constrained, fearful air.

He had promised not to cry.

Every now and then, though, a stifled sigh, like the tail end of a sob, shook him from head to toe. Then his mother’s eyes would come to rest on him, and seemed to say:

“You know what you promised me…” At once the child would force back his sigh and his tears; but you could feel a great sorrow in him, that cruel sense of exile and abandonment which a first boarding school brings to little ones who have been kept at home by the fireside longer than most.

This survey of mother and child, which the priest had made in a few minutes, might have satisfied a superficial observer; but Father O…, who had been at the head of the aristocratic Jesuit college at Vaugirard for more than twenty-five years, knew too much of the world, knew Parisian high society and all its shades of speech and bearing too well, not to have guessed that the mother of the new pupil before him was a client of a rather special kind.

The self-possession with which she had walked into his study, too conspicuous to be genuine, her way of leaning back as she sat down, that young, slightly forced laugh of hers, and above all that overflowing torrent of words, beneath which she seemed to be hiding the awkwardness of some secret thought: everything about her put the priest on his guard. Unfortunately, in Paris the different worlds are so mingled, and shared pleasures, fashions and promenades have made the dividing line between the fashionable women of good society and of bad so thin and so easily crossed—between a lorette, a kept woman, who knows how to behave, and a marquise who lets herself go—that even the most expert eye can be deceived at first sight; and that was why the priest was studying this woman so closely.

What most disconcerted his scrutiny was the disjointedness of her talk. How was anyone to get his bearings among all these whims, these about-turns, these leaps of a squirrel in a cage? Still, his judgement, which she was perhaps trying to throw off the scent, was already half made up. The mother’s embarrassment when he asked her what the child’s other name was, besides Jack, settled it.

She blushed, grew flustered, and hesitated for a second.

“Of course,” she said, “do forgive me… I haven’t introduced myself yet… Whatever am I thinking of?”

Alphonse Daudet (1840–1897)

In his lifetime Alphonse Daudet was often called the French Dickens. He published Jack in 1876, between Fromont and Risler and The Nabob, and dedicated it to his friend Gustave Flaubert: a big, dark novel of a neglected child, which English readers have known, if at all, in translations made in Daudet's lifetime. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

De son vivant, Alphonse Daudet fut souvent appelé le Dickens français. Il publia Jack en 1876, entre Fromont jeune et Risler aîné et Le Nabab, et le dédia à son ami Gustave Flaubert : un grand roman sombre sur un enfant abandonné à lui-même, que le lectorat anglophone n'a connu, au mieux, que dans des traductions faites du vivant de Daudet. Les classiques en anglais moderne donnent en entier, dans l'anglais d'aujourd'hui, les classiques français que le lecteur anglophone ne connaît qu'en traductions anciennes ou abrégées. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
