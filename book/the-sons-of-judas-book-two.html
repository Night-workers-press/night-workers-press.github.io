Epilogue

I

Three months have gone by since the day that excellent Mr Thompson installed Callebrand and his daughter in a pretty apartment in the faubourg Saint-Denis, through Father X—. Three calm months, full of happiness for Marthe, for her old father and for Raymond. Only yesterday Callebrand recovered the secret of the malleability of metals. Raymond, who is completely cured, woke one morning wanting to know exactly what was left of his fortune. He had run through a great deal and squandered a great deal in the days when he was in love with Alea. His notary rendered him accounts that were exact and perfectly clear. Raymond has seven or eight thousand francs a year left.

For the Raymond of the old days, that is poverty. For the Raymond he has become, for the Raymond who loves Marthe and means to make her his wife, it is wealth. They are to be married. They will go and live in a pretty little red-brick house in the country, in the middle of the woods, on the edge of a pond, in that melancholy Sologne so steeped in a rough, rustic poetry that artists and thinkers and writers dream of it for the blessed hour when they can rest at last. They will have a fine farm, and Marthe will bring up her children to love the fields, which lifts the soul and makes a man better than he was.

Callebrand listens to his two children drawing up the programme of their life to come, and sighs, and lifts his eyes to heaven.

“Father,” says Marthe, “why are you sighing?”

“Because I would like to give you a princess’s dowry, my child,” the old man answers.

“Ah, good!” says the girl, smiling and throwing Raymond a tender look. “I know what you are going to tell us. You have found the malleability of metals, haven’t you? And if you only had two or three hundred thousand francs to work the discovery… because it would take that at the very least…”

“At the very least,” says the old man, sighing again.

But while Marthe was speaking the door has opened softly, and a good fat face, thoroughly jovial and thoroughly guileless, has stopped on the threshold, smiling. Marthe and Callebrand turn round. The good fat face, which is none other than Mr Thompson’s, comes straight up to Callebrand and says:

“You shall have, not three hundred thousand francs, but six hundred thousand, my dear Mr Callebrand.”

The old man steps back. Distrust is written all over his face. But Mr Thompson takes him by the hand.

“My dear Mr Callebrand,” he says, “don’t condemn me without hearing me out. I have just sold my factory and turned my fortune into cash. I leave for London tomorrow. I am going home to my own country, to a small estate deep in Lancashire where I mean to die in peace. So what I am offering you is not a partnership, and still less a loan at interest. It is restitution. Baul, my partner, was the cause of every misfortune you have suffered, and I have worked to make them good. Present yourself in three months, to the day, at the office of Bardin, my notary. He will count out to you, from the price of the factory — which will not be paid over until then — the sum of six hundred thousand francs.”

Marthe and Callebrand very nearly fall at Mr Thompson’s feet. And Mr Thompson, the honest man, the fine soul washed clean by repentance, presses them both to his heart.

A new arrival has come into the drawing room during this touching scene: Father X—. The worthy priest holds out his hand to Mr Thompson and says:

“You are a worthy man, and God will reward you.”

That evening, at nine o’clock, the honest Mr Thompson — who has left Callebrand a note drawn up in proper form for the sum of six hundred thousand francs, payable at the office of Bardin, notary in Paris;

Mr Thompson, who has indeed sold his factory, after first having Mr Baul, whose madness is reckoned incurable, admitted to the asylum at Bicêtre;

Mr Thompson, we say, settles himself comfortably into a first-class carriage on the Northern Railway. The Calais and Dover train is about to leave.

Mr Thompson is radiant, and he is thinking about Callebrand.

“The fool!” he murmurs. “In three months it will be too late for him to work his discovery — the secret of which I have possessed since last night as well as he does, thanks to that mysterious door into his laboratory. In three months I shall have taken out patents long since, under another name, in free England, set up my workshops and built a gigantic factory. But after all, he will have six hundred thousand francs… which is very handsome of me! And to think there may be people who will call me a thief!”

And Mr Thompson laughs with all his heart at the thought of that simpleton Callebrand, and of that worthy priest who was actually capable of taking him, Thompson, for an honest man.

The whistle has already sounded and the train is on the point of moving off when a railwayman pulls the door open.

“In here, madam — there is one seat left!” says the man.

A woman climbs into the carriage. Mr Thompson, who has an eye for a handsome woman, looks at her.

And an inexplicable shiver suddenly runs through his whole body.

II

The night is black, the wind is blowing in a fury, the sea is heavy. The packet has sailed from Calais all the same, at one in the morning; the mail service does not stop for weather. The passengers have gone to their berths. Some of them are asleep. The rest are being sick. The deck is deserted.

Deserted except for one man, who is walking up and down at the stern with a jerky, uneven step, in the grip of a violent agitation. Now and then the man stops and mutters between his teeth:

“Thompson, you old fool, a man does not fall suddenly in love at your age, in under four hours on a train, with a woman he has never set eyes on before. In love, at fifty-five!”

And Mr Thompson worries at his whiskers, tears at his hair, swears like a heathen and tries to drag his mind away from the thought that has taken hold of it by thinking instead about Callebrand’s invention, which he fully intends to exploit on his own account. But Mr Thompson is lying to himself, and the lie does not last long. The woman who got into his carriage in Paris is there in his mind, and will not leave it. She is aboard the packet; and with his mind and his heart in a turmoil he could not put a name to, Mr Thompson walks the deck like a soul in torment while the strangest and most senseless dreams go through his fevered brain.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
