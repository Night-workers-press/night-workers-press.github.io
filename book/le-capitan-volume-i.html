Into this walks a penniless Gascon with a good sword and no sense of self-preservation. Capestang is Zévaco's most likeable hero, and this is his best entrance.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — Giselle of Angoulême

A strange terror weighs upon Paris. Sinister rumors spread abroad, like those rumblings of the sky that herald a storm. At times, howling mobs pass by, with the faces of riot. The burgher furbishes his old partisan from the days of the League. The nobility is on its feet to reclaim its feudal privileges. Guise conspires. Condé conspires. Angoulême conspires. Luynes wishes to govern. Richelieu wishes to govern. The throne of the Bourbons totters and is perhaps about to fall.

And before these gusts of unleashed ambition clashing one against another, there is, in the depths of the Louvre—deserted and bleak—only a poor little king of fifteen, all alone, abandoned, pale and sorrowful as the people.

And, like the people, Louis XIII trembles and asks himself:

“Who is to become the master?… Guise? Condé? Angoulême? Which of you will set his foot upon my head?”

Now, people, king, and conspirators are bound together by one same vast and scattered hatred; they shudder with a common dread, and, ready to tear one another apart, they lift their eyes to the blazing figure that looms over the Louvre, over Paris, over the kingdom. And then the same imprecation rumbles upon every lip, from the king down to the meanest churl—save upon those of the queen mother, Marie de Médicis. This figure is that of a man who commands, decrees, ordains, reigns, crushes, terrorizes. He is infernal luxury; he is power without limit; he is pride without bridle; he is the orgy… he is crime itself.

He passes like one of those incomprehensible meteors that streak across the spaces of history, leaving behind them a trail of blood and fire, then burst and gutter out in some supreme catastrophe…

And this man is Concino Concini…

The queen’s lover!

On the morning of the fifth of August in that year 1616…

In the Rue de Tournon, a mansion with the air of a royal fortress, with its courtyard full of guards, its monumental staircase astir with bedizened lackeys, its sumptuous antechambers thronged with courtiers: this is the dwelling of Concino Concini, Governor of Normandy, Marquis d’Ancre, Marshal of France, and Prime Minister of Louis XIII…

The audience chamber, a vast room where the art of Italy and the art of France have lavished their masterpieces—paintings, furniture, marbles, and bronzes. Here is Concini!

He is of middling height, vigorous, nervous, of a rare and exquisite elegance. His handsome face is lit by the eyes of a cat, now of a strange gentleness, now flashing. He wears the bold, troubled mask of the great adventurers. It is perhaps the soul of a Nero or a Cesare Borgia that quivers in those willful gestures, in those attitudes of pride.

He leans over someone who, half-bent, listens to him avidly. And while, among the crowd of petitioners, men wonder what is brewing behind that cabinet door—with what festival the master will dazzle Paris, or beneath what tax he will crush it—here is what Concini says in a muffled voice:

“Hatred, yes, Rinaldo, that is something! I have it in my very marrow. Yes, I hate unto damnation that Duke of Angoulême. The others—the Guises, the Condés—are nothing but rabble starved for honors or for money. He is the formidable adversary. I shall kill him, or he will kill me. Rinaldo, I would give ten years of my life to hold Angoulême and, with my own hands, tear out his heart—but…”

“Go on, then, monseigneur!” sneered the man, with an insolent and obsequious familiarity.

“But this hatred,” Concini resumes in an ardent voice, “this hatred I bear the Duke of Angoulême—why, it vanishes when love speaks within me. That girl, I must have her, do you see! Fortune, honor, power, hatred—there is nothing left when the image of Giselle rises up in me. Rinaldo, I die if Giselle is not mine. Rinaldo, passion burns my blood, tears my heart, and floods my brain…”

“Patience, monseigneur—she will be found, this Giselle of yours!”

“Oh! if only I were sure of it! If only I could hope! Money, Rinaldo, gold, offices, anything you wish, if you find her!… Who can she be? Of great family, beyond doubt—but which?…”

“We shall learn it, monseigneur. Patience, I tell you!”

“Ah!” growls Concini, with a violent gesture. “To have done no more than glimpse her! To know nothing of her but this name of Giselle, this adored name I stammer through my tears in my long sleepless nights!… I want—do you hear me?—I want to know who she is, I want you to find her! Go, search, spend without counting, loose a thousand spies upon Paris—go, my Rinaldo, and do not appear before me again save to cry: ‘Live, hope, love—Giselle is found!’”

“Very well, monseigneur. Let me sum up. On the side of hatred: to make certain whether the Duke of Angoulême has had the audacity to return to Paris, as is said; and then, to set a fine trap for him. On the side of love: to take the field after our fair unknown, with this name of Giselle for my only guide.”

“Find her, Rinaldo, find her! And I shall make you a count!”

Rinaldo bows to the ground.

“Monseigneur,” he says coldly, “your Giselle shall be found—I swear it upon the title of nobility you have just conferred on me!”

Concini turns pale. He puts his hand to his heart, and trembles, shaken by a long shudder. Rinaldo has withdrawn. In the courtyard of the mansion he mounts his horse and murmurs with a sneer:

“By God! I would wager my brand-new nobility that it is she I saw yesterday near Meudon! But, the devil! I must be sure! Were I to give Concini a false joy… I know him, my illustrious master: he would make me Count of the Bastille and leave me to rot in my county.

“Come then! To Meudon!”

To Meudon. Behind the last house of the village lies an old abandoned park, thick-grown, overrun with untrammeled growth. Near the gate stands a chestnut horse, all saddled, held by the bridle by an old servant. And, coming toward the horse, a young girl leaning on the arm of a gentleman of proud bearing—his temples gray, his face pale with that peculiar pallor of men who have long lived in a dungeon, yet full of concentrated vigor, still young, seeming about forty.

The young girl, with a bold grace, wears a riding habit of blue velvet; her fair and luminous beauty is of the kind that astonishes, overwhelms, and inspires sudden, devastating passions. But what strikes, charms, and dazzles still more than the nobility of her brow, the splendor of her hair, the deep azure of her eyes, the harmony of her figure and form—what stamps this beauty with a character all its own—is that air of unspeakable dignity in her bearing, that admirable candor of gaze, that intrepidity of soul that shows in her every gesture, her every word, her whole person.

“Farewell, Father,” says the young girl, halting near the gate.

“Farewell, my darling child,” replies the gentleman, clasping her in his arms. “What would become of me if you were not here, my fair warrior, my own true blood! If my destiny bears me at last to that throne the Bourbons stole from my race, it is to you I shall owe my reign. You are a true Valois, my noble and daring messenger, my beloved Giselle! Forever on horseback, through a thousand dangers! Only yesterday you came back from Orléans, bringing me those precious papers. And here you are upon the road again!”

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
