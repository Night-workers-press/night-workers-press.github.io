Chapter One

The Huntsman’s Complaint

“What a shame, my lord, to have travelled a fortnight through country as fine as this and never once let the hounds loose, never once gone out after anything.”

“My good Bouquin, war makes its own demands. Once we’ve beaten the Austrians hard enough to dictate a treaty of peace, we’ll ask for leave and go home to Pouzauges, where the stag and the boar are plentiful enough to keep our kennels busy the whole year round.”

“That’s very well said and very well thought, my lord,” Bouquin replied in a grumbling tone, “but it was hardly worth dragging your hounds two thousand miles to trail them along behind a baggage wagon day and night, coupled two by two with their tails down. That’s all we’ve done since I got here. Every few minutes we push in among the trees, we cross a thicket, we come out onto a plain twenty-five miles across where a beast would be in view the whole way; everywhere I look I see a young boar’s tusks here, a full-grown stag’s antlers there, the flick of a wild goat’s tail further on. The hounds howl, my horn dances on my shoulder all by itself, my ears are full of horn calls — the find, the chase, the kill — and nothing comes of any of it. We go on marching at the head of these stupid dragoons, who shrug their shoulders — the godless idiots! — at the sight of our best hounds out of the Vendée and our finest hounds out of Saintonge.”

Bouquin — an old huntsman, as the reader will have guessed, sixty years old and still full of fire and hunting courage — delivered this speech in a thoroughly bad humour, then withdrew into his majestic silence and cast a look of supreme disdain at the company of dragoons riding behind their commander.

That commander was the Count de Main-Hardye, captain of dragoons, in charge of a cavalry rearguard making its way across the plains and endless forests of Bohemia to rejoin a French army corps under Marshal de Belle-Isle, then camped outside Prague, besieging it. The count was a young man of twenty-eight or thirty, good-looking, quick, brave to the point of recklessness and adventurous to the point of madness. Above all he had, to the highest degree, that noble passion for hunting which was already dying out among the gentlemen of France, though the year was only 1750. Plenty of them owned land laid out perfectly for it; but war, and still more often the intrigues of the court, kept them away from that land for most of the year. He hunted every day of the six months’ leave he asked of the king each year. During the other six he still managed to get out once or twice a week — at the king’s great hunts, or on the estates of the princes.

Three months earlier an order from the king had reached him in the middle of a big hunting party with the local gentry. He was to rejoin his regiment at once. It formed part of an army corps operating in Bohemia, fighting alongside Prussia against Austria and Russia. Being the gentleman he was, the count had pulled off his hunting coat on the spot and put on his uniform, exchanged his hunting knife for his sword, and hung his horn and his boar-spear above the drawing-room chimneypiece, condemning them — not without regret — to a rest whose end he could not foresee.

The old huntsman stood there hangdog, his eyes dull, wondering how long the country would have to go without the deep baying of his great white-and-tan hounds. The count set his foot in the stirrup and said: “Bouquin, my friend, it’s possible I shan’t be back for a year. It’s equally possible I’ll be back within the month. You will take scrupulous care of my kennels. You will hunt the wood of Jarry every Sunday and the scrub at Pouzauges every Wednesday. You will run my bassets after the young rabbits in the park at Bienvenue, and you will take great care never to run down a full-grown stag. And one more thing, and if you get this wrong I’ll have your ears: let the neighbours shoot on my land as rarely as you can manage. I won’t have the game shot out.”

These instructions delivered, the count had ridden away to his regiment.

He arrived the day before a battle and two days before a siege. The battle won and the besieged town taken by storm, he was left in garrison in a small village on the border of East Prussia — a place of no importance in itself, but one the enemy could have turned to excellent account if they had taken it. Marshal de Belle-Isle entrusted him with its defence and rode off to lay siege to Prague.

For a week the Count de Main-Hardye kept his guard up, holding his men to a severe discipline, confining them to quarters, expecting at any moment to be attacked by a corps of Austrian infantry camped twenty-five miles away. Then, on orders from above, that corps pulled back another twenty-five miles — and an idea grew all at once in the count’s brain.

What if I hunted? he thought.

The village and the surrounding country were admirably placed for it. Thick woods, young scrub, valleys that carried sound, level stony plains, ponds by the dozen, pools and streams where the hounds could drink — nothing was wanting. Game abounded. Roe deer and does were the small stuff, for besides stag and boar there were wolf, elk and bear in plenty. This luxury of game had two causes: first the lie of the land, and second the total absence of huntsmen anywhere near. The advantage carried its drawback, for the simplest of reasons: there is always a catch. A complete absence of huntsmen naturally implied a complete famine of hounds. And without a pack, how does one hunt?

The count was on a roll. He had found one; he might very well find a second. And so he did.

What if I sent for my own hounds? he said to himself. It’s a long haul, but it can be done in eighteen days with a few forced marches. It may very well be that I winter here, in which case the king’s service will be easy on me. And if I’m moved to another garrison, I’ll be unlucky indeed not to land in hunting country. In Bohemia you can hunt anywhere.

Whereupon Monsieur de Main-Hardye called his valet and dictated the following letter:

My dear Bouquin, on receipt of this you will procure a cart as long as a street. You will put into it fifteen of my best Vendée hounds and twenty-five of my tallest staghounds, together with my kennelman Letaillis, and you will harness it with two good Limousin horses. That done, you will sit up on the driver’s seat beside the valet who brings you this letter, and you will take the road for Germany. When your horses are worn out you will replace them. If my steward is short of money, sell a hundred hectares of land at once. So long as the woods remain to us, that is all we need. Bring me my horn and my hunting knife.

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
