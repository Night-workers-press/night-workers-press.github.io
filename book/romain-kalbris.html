The bookLe livre

Romain Kalbris is the son of a fishing family on the coast of Normandy, and all he wants is to go to sea. His father drowns in a storm trying to bring a stranded ship into harbour; his uncle Simon, a miser, starves him; so he runs away, along the shore, across Normandy with a wandering painter, and into a travelling menagerie where a small girl called Diélette goes into the lion's cage every night. The two children escape together and walk to Paris in the snow. Nine years before Sans Famille, Hector Malot sent this other boy out alone on the roads of France.

Romain Kalbris est fils d'une famille de pêcheurs de la côte normande, et il ne rêve que de partir en mer. Son père se noie dans une tempête en tentant de ramener au port un navire échoué ; son oncle Simon, un avare, l'affame ; alors il s'enfuit, le long du rivage, à travers la Normandie avec un peintre ambulant, puis dans une ménagerie foraine où une petite fille, Diélette, entre chaque soir dans la cage du lion. Les deux enfants s'échappent ensemble et gagnent Paris à pied, dans la neige. Neuf ans avant Sans famille, Hector Malot lançait déjà un autre garçon, seul, sur les routes de France.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I

You must not conclude from my present position that I had Fortune for a godmother. My ancestors, if that is not too grand a word for them, were fishermen. My father was the last of eleven children, and my grandfather had had a hard time bringing up his family, for in that trade, even more than in others, the earnings bear no relation to the work: toil and danger you can count on; a little money is a matter of luck.

At eighteen my father was taken by the Naval Register. This is a kind of conscription by which the State can call on the service of every seaman for thirty-two years… from eighteen to fifty. He went away unable to read or write. He came back a chief petty officer, which is the highest rank a man can reach without going through the government’s naval schools.

Because Port-Dieu, our village, lies close to the Channel Islands, the State keeps a navy cutter stationed there, whose task is to stop the Jersey men from coming over to take our fish, and at the same time to make our own fishermen keep to the fishing regulations. It was to this cutter that my father was sent to serve out his time. It was a favour, for however used a man may be to making his ship his homeland, he is always glad to come back to the place where he was born.

Fifteen months after his return I made my entrance into the world, and since it was a Friday in March, and the day of the new moon, everyone agreed that I would have adventures, that I would make voyages by sea, and that I would be very unhappy unless the influence of the moon worked against the influence of the Friday. Adventures I have had, and they are just what I mean to tell you about; voyages by sea I have made; as for the struggle between the two influences, it has been a hard one, and it is you who will say, at the end of my story, which of them won.

To foretell adventures and voyages for me was simply to recognise that I was a true child of the family, for the Kalbris men had been seamen from father to son, and, if the legend is true, they were seamen as far back as the Trojan War. It is not we ourselves, of course, who claim this origin, but certain learned men, who maintain that there are some hundred families in Port-Dieu, the seafaring families to be exact, who are descended from a colony of Phoenicians. What is certain is that with our black eyes, our swarthy skin and our fine noses we have nothing of the Norman or the Breton look about us, and that our fishing boats are an exact copy of the ship of Ulysses as Homer describes it: a single mast with a square sail. That rig is very common among the islands of the Aegean, but in the Channel it is found nowhere else.

Our own memories did not go back so far, and their very sameness made them rather muddled. When anyone spoke of a relative, the story hardly varied: while still a child he had gone to sea, or overseas, among peoples whose names are hard to remember, and he had died in a shipwreck, in battle, or in the English prison hulks. In the churchyard there were many crosses bearing the name of a girl or a widow, and few bearing the name of a boy or a man; the men did not die at home. Still, like every family, we had our heroes. One was my mother’s father, who had sailed with Surcouf; the other was my great-uncle Flohy. As soon as I could understand what was said around me, I heard his name ten times a day. He was in the service of a king in India who had elephants; he commanded troops against the English, and he had a silver arm. Elephants, a silver arm, and none of it a dream!

It was the thirst for adventure born in every Kalbris that made my father sign on for another tour of duty a few years after his marriage. He could have gone as mate of one of the schooners that sail every spring for the Iceland fishery, but he had grown used to the navy, and he loved it.

I do not remember his going. My only memories of that time are of stormy days, of nights of wind and thunder, and of the hours I used to spend outside the post office.

How many times, at night, my mother made me pray before a candle she had lit! To us a storm at Port-Dieu was a storm everywhere, and the wind that shook our house seemed to be shaking my father’s ship at the same moment. Sometimes it blew so hard that we had to get up and tie the windows shut, for ours was a poor family’s house; although it was sheltered on one side by a fall of rock from the cliff, and on the other by a deckhouse that had once been the saloon of a wrecked three-master, it stood up badly to the gales of the equinox. One October night my mother woke me. The hurricane was terrible: the wind howled, the house groaned, and gusts came in that made the candle flame flicker until they blew it out; in the lulls we could hear the waves battling against the shingle and the seas booming like gunfire in the hollows of the cliff. In spite of that tremendous noise I soon fell asleep again on my knees. Suddenly the window was torn from its hinges and hurled into the room, where it smashed into a thousand pieces, and I felt as if I were being carried off in a whirlwind.

“Good God!” cried my mother. “Your father is lost!”

She had faith in forebodings and in mysterious warnings, and a letter she received from my father some months after that night of storm made her faith stronger still: by a strange coincidence, in that very month of October he had been caught in a gale and in great danger. A sailor’s wife sleeps a sad sleep. Dreaming of shipwreck, waiting for a letter that does not come: her life is spent between those two fears.

In the days I am speaking of, letters were not delivered as they are now. They were simply handed out at the post office, and when the people they were addressed to were too slow in coming for them, they were sent round by a boy from the school. On the day the mail came in from Newfoundland the office was besieged, for from spring to autumn all the men are away at the cod fishery, and a stranger arriving in the village might think he had landed on that island Ariosto tells of, from which men were banished; so the women were eager for news. With their children in their arms, they waited for the names to be called out. Some laughed as they read, others wept. Those who had no letter questioned those who had; when the men are at sea, nobody can say, “No news is good news.”

Hector Malot (1830–1907)

Hector Malot was a Norman, born near Rouen, who wrote for grown-ups and children alike. Sans Famille (1878) has been read by French children ever since it appeared; Romain Kalbris (1869) is the novel in which, nine years earlier, he first sent a boy out alone on the roads of France. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

Hector Malot était un Normand, né près de Rouen, qui écrivit pour les adultes comme pour les enfants. Sans famille (1878) est lu par les enfants de France depuis sa parution ; Romain Kalbris (1869) est le roman où, neuf ans plus tôt, il lançait pour la première fois un garçon seul sur les routes de France. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
