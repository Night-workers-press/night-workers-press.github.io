Chapter One

My dear readers,

This isn't a novel. It's a confidence I'm sharing with you, and it gives me the pleasure of talking with you directly—a pleasure that's rare enough, alas, for a novelist.

One evening, ten or twelve years ago now, there were three of us in a private room at the Pavillon d'Armenonville in the Bois de Boulogne: my friend Gustave Claudin, Bergerette, and myself.

You all know that witty journalist, that amiable and inexhaustible talker they call Gustave Claudin. And once I tell you that Bergerette was twenty, with dazzling teeth and one of those genuine, easy laughs that suit youth so well, you'll know her just as well as we did.

Claudin—who in those days was not yet a Chevalier of the Legion of Honour, editor of some solemn newspaper, and a quasi-official personage as he is today—was smoothing his black moustache and holding forth on one of those paradoxical theories whose secret he'll carry to his grave. Bergerette sat rolling little pellets of breadcrumb between her dainty fingers and flicking them at his nose. As for me, despite Claudin's fireworks and Bergerette's bursts of laughter, I sat there as gloomy as the villain in a melodrama, fourth act, on the eve of his comeuppance.

Monsieur Delamarre—of the firm Delamarre, Martin Didier & Co., a former royal bodyguard, a former director of the Banque de France, and at that time the editor of the newspaper La Patrie—had sent for me that morning and told me:

"Politics is dead calm, the assizes are idle. We haven't got so much as a little war or a nice juicy criminal trial to feed our readers, and the October renewal is coming up fast. So put together one of those great serial machines that straddle two quarters and keep the wavering subscriber on the hook—something to amuse his wife and daughters."

Which, translated, meant: "Whip me up a nice little hundred-instalment novel, if you please. Give me the title tomorrow, and we'll start in a week."

That's why I was gloomy.

"My dear fellow," Claudin said, "the thing's really quite simple. Just redo one of those novels that caused such a sensation ten years or so back."

"Easy for you to say!"

"The Count of Monte Cristo, for instance!"

"With Dumas as my collaborator, then?"

"The Mysteries of Paris, if you'd rather."

"I don't know a word of slang."

"You'll learn..."

"Where, and how?"

The waiter who'd been serving us came in just as I said this.

This waiter deserves a two-line sketch. He was a man of fifty, a touch stout, with crinkled white hair and a majestic bearing. Give him a ceremonial key to wear at his back, and you'd have taken him for a royal chamberlain.

The word "slang" clearly had an effect on him. Up to that point he'd been serving us with real eagerness, all the more so since we were nearly the only guests in the pavilion, and Bergerette, good-natured girl that she was, had been running up quite a bill with cheerful abandon. But at the word "slang," his face, cheerful until then, clouded over. He eyed us with suspicion, served us absent-mindedly, and we had to ring for him several times just to get a plate. By dessert, he'd vanished.

At that time, a ministerial circular had banned slang from the stage. Claudin said to me:

"That'll be some sacked censor who's traded his scissors for a napkin."

Another waiter brought our coffee, and we thought no more of the first one.

It was drizzling, one of those fine, steady rains that October carries in under its misty wing.

"What shall we do with our evening?" Bergerette asked. "I'd like to go somewhere."

"It's raining..."

"What about a carriage?"

"Ours is open-topped, you know that. Besides, it's just a passing shower—let's wait it out."

"A little game of lansquenet?" Claudin suggested.

"Just the three of us?"

"Well, why not!"

Just then we heard a carriage pull up, and I went to the window. It was a smart cab carrying five people, and those five turned out to be friends of ours whom the rain had caught out in the Bois.

"Come and play lansquenet with us!" I called down to them.

They heard me, and a quarter of an hour later we were all seated around a table draped in blue baize.

Everyone has played lansquenet at some point; everyone knows what a strange, whimsical game it is, prone to the wildest swings of fortune. There's almost always, over the course of a hand—and often over an entire evening—one card that turns up more often than the rest. Sometimes it's an ace that keeps the banker winning without fail; sometimes it's a queen that dogs him with endless bad luck. That night, one card kept coming up so often that anyone playing against it considered his money as good as lost before the hand was even played out. That unlucky card was none other than the jack of hearts. Galuchet, as it was nicknamed, was cleaning everyone out.

At midnight, we were still playing. We ordered champagne and cigars. It was the same waiter who'd served us at dinner who answered the bell. He'd lost his suspicious look, and his smile—faintly majestic—had returned to his lips.

The game went on. It was Bergerette's turn to deal.

"Watch," she said, "I'm not afraid of Galuchet."

The first card she turned was a jack of hearts. The second was another jack of hearts.

"A double!" someone cried.

"Another double!" she said.

To our utter astonishment, she'd turned up two more jacks of hearts. No one dared hold their bet any longer.

"It's a sure thing now, though," the little sinner said, smiling. "There are no more jacks of hearts left."

So they held their bets. A third double of jacks of hearts came up.

Just then the white-haired waiter was stepping through the doorway of the little salon, carrying an enormous tray of glasses and bottles.

"The jacks of hearts are having the luck of the devil tonight!" Claudin exclaimed.

Suddenly the waiter let out a cry, and the tray crashed to the floor. Terrified, he turned and fled, muttering:

"The jacks of hearts! The jacks of hearts! Always the jacks of hearts!..."

The noise of shattering glass brought the proprietress of the establishment upstairs, along with the headwaiter, whom the white-haired man had very nearly knocked flat on the staircase, so headlong was his flight.

"I did tell you," said the lovely Madame Leblond to her headwaiter, "to let that man go. You know perfectly well he's out of his mind!"

Chapter Two

This scene brought on, as you can imagine, one explanation after another. The headwaiter and the other waiters on staff each had their own version of events.

The headwaiter told us:

"This man came here about a week ago looking for work. He seemed decent enough, so we took him on, and for the first two days he did his job perfectly well.

"On the evening of the second day, he asked permission to go into Paris. He was supposed to come back on the last train. The gates of the Bois close at midnight, and since he hadn't come back, we assumed he'd spent the night in Paris.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
