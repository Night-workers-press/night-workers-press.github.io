Chapter One

The building site was deserted.

Amid the rubble of the demolished house, threading among the fresh-cut stone laid ready for the building that would replace it, blazed the bivouac fire lit by the old soldier who kept watch over the site and its materials.

Night had fallen dark, the sounds of the great city were dying away, and the last carriage from the last ball had gone home.

For this was happening only a few days ago, in the heart of modern Paris, a stone's throw from the boulevard and the Vendôme column, on the very ground where Tahan had once displayed his art treasures and Basset his cases of fine pearls and diamonds.

Had Paris been put to fire and sword? Had some barbarian horde swept down from the north, conquered the queen of cities, and sown ruin and misery in its wake? Was this reddish glow, thrown up over a heap of rubble, the night-fire of conquerors?

It was the very image of desolation, of chaos.

A little farther off lay the feverish calm of a Paris asleep after a night of pleasure.

The barbarian horde that had reduced the Rue de la Paix to a heap of ruins was nothing more than a company of masons and harmless Limousin laborers.

Paris had been conquered by the Limousin, and the Rue Turbigo was being driven through.

Had daylight come, one could have seen a long breach running from the Boulevard des Capucines all the way to the Rue de Choiseul.

On one side the old houses were crumbling to dust; on the other, new buildings were rising by degrees, bristling with scaffolding that carried a whole legion of workmen of every trade.

But at this hour it looked more like a battlefield after the dead had been buried.

Everywhere silence and darkness, everywhere rubble; and across this ravaged stretch of city, two men kept watch by a fire built of worm-eaten beams and broken shutters.

One of them was an old soldier; the other a poor devil of a mason who lay stretched before the fire, wrapped in a ragged scrap of blanket.

The veteran was a Crimea man, who had left a leg with the Russians; his mustache was still black and his face wore a proud, melancholy air.

He might have been the god Mars condemned to eternal rest.

The mason was a young man of barely twenty, with long chestnut hair, blue eyes, and an open, gentle face that was not without a certain force in it.

Though he had worked hard all day at his rough trade and should have been worn out with fatigue, he could not sleep.

He tossed and turned in his blanket, now and then lifting his head and searching the darkness for some point, some mysterious fixed thing.

Then a heavy sigh would escape him; his eyes would close, but sleep would not come.

"Hey, Limousin," said the veteran, taking his pipe from his mouth for a moment, "do you know you're a strange one?"

The young man gave a start.

"How do you mean, old-timer?" he said, half sitting up and looking at the veteran.

"Your mates go off every evening," the old soldier went on. "Some head for Batignolles, others for La Chapelle or Belleville—everyone goes back to his lodgings..."

"And I stay here, is that it?"

"As if the boss needed you to guard the site! Aren't I enough—me, who's paid to do it?"

"If I stay here," said the mason, "it's because, like my mates, I've got no lodgings."

"So you don't draw your pay like the others?"

"Yes, I do."

"Then you're a spendthrift, a drunkard?"

"No, old-timer."

"Maybe you send your money to your mother?"

"I send her half of it, and I've got plenty left to live on and take a room like the others—but I'd rather sleep in the open air."

"It's not exactly warm, though."

"I know. But I'm not afraid of the cold."

"Well," said the veteran, "then why don't you sleep? Here we are, eight or ten nights running together, and you barely close your eyes for a couple of hours."

"It's that I can't sleep," said the Limousin, with another sigh.

"You've got some trouble, my boy?"

"Maybe so, old-timer."

"Could it be you're in love?"

At that question the Limousin gave a genuine start.

"Who told you that?" he said sharply.

The veteran began to smile.

"As you can see," he said, "I'm not exactly an old-timer yet. I was only twenty-six when the Russians took my leg. That was fourteen years ago—so I'm not even forty."

"Well," said the Limousin.

"Love's had its way with me, same as with anyone," the veteran went on, "and it could still have its way with me again, given the chance."

"Ha!" said the mason, smiling.

"I'm even good for advice, if it comes to that, and since you can't sleep, my boy, why not tell me your little story? Who knows—I might even lend a hand."

The Limousin sighed again.

"You see, old-timer," he said, "when a worm falls in love with a star, there's nothing to be done."

"You talk like the schoolmaster back in my village," said the veteran, laughing. "So you're the worm, are you?"

"Yes."

"And where's the star?"

"Up there."

And with that the Limousin pointed toward one of the houses on the Rue Louis-le-Grand—one the new Rue Turbigo had left standing—whose windows looked out over the site.

"True enough," said the veteran, "that's not the sort of neighborhood where fellows like you and me can easily find a lady friend. But bah, you never know—and to talk your own language, I'll tell you there are caterpillars that turn into butterflies and fly off toward the stars."

The Limousin sighed once more.

"Oh," he said, "even with wings, she'd still be too high for me."

"So she's a woman of quality?"

"A princess, maybe. Every day at two o'clock, when the sun's out, I go over there, to a corner of the site, climb up on a pile of wood, and peer through the planks. I watch her get into a fine carriage to go for her drive."

"Is she alone?"

"No, there are two men with her. She seems to hate them, to be afraid of them, and there are moments when I feel like jumping over the fence with my hammer, climbing into the carriage, and knocking them both senseless. She'd be glad of it, I know she would."

"You're mad, my boy!"

"That doesn't change the fact that she smiled at me once."

"At you?"

"Yes, really."

"Through the planks?"

"No—it was when we were tearing the old house down. I was knocking out the opening of a window across from hers, and I'd stopped work to look at her. She was leaning on her windowsill, gazing out over the rooftops, looking for all the world like a swallow shut in a cage, longing to fly. All at once she noticed I was watching her, and she gave me a smile."

The Limousin's voice had grown unsteady, and by the firelight the veteran saw a tear slide down his cheek.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
