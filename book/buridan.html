From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I. The Rose Garden

Near the Temple, almost within the sinister shadow of that black and silent fortress upon whose approaches no man dared venture, there lay a flowering enclosure of an exquisite and unlooked-for gaiety, full of birdsong—something like a pretty primrose nestled at the foot of a monstrous toadstool.

It was called the Courtille-aux-Roses, a charming name for that poetic garden where, when the fair season came, roses of every hue would burst indeed into magical clusters.

Within the enclosure stood a dainty little house, a very jewel, with its steep gabled roof crowned with pinnacles, its turret, its ogival windows of coloured glass—a dwelling that breathed happiness. And there, on that bright morning caressed by wanton breezes, there, in a chamber adorned with fine tapestries and richly carved furniture, was an adorable pairing of youth and beauty… two lovers! She, delicate, slender, charming enough to steal the heart away; he, slim, proud, and most elegant in his somewhat threadbare costume.

At the back of the room, a woman already old, of sallow complexion and oily smile, watched over them with a shifty gaze.

"Farewell, Myrtille… until tomorrow," murmured the young man.

"Tomorrow!" answered the girl. "Tomorrow, alas! Can I be sure I shall see you again tomorrow—or ever—when you rush toward so terrible a danger? Oh, if you love me, Buridan, give up this madness!"

Her arms about her beloved's neck, her fair hair loosed in waves of gold, her azure eyes brimming with tears, she implored him:

"Remember that this evening my father will be here! Remember that this evening I mean to confess our love to him!"

"Your father, Myrtille!" said the young man, with a start.

"Yes, Jean, yes, my dear betrothed—this evening my father shall know everything!"

"Your father!… But this father whom I do not know, who does not know me—will he consent to have me? Who can say?… And what is he, this father of yours? O Myrtille, in the six months since you first appeared to me in this secluded enclosure, since the evening you let your sweet gaze fall upon me, how many times have I not striven to catch one glimpse of this man who is your father! In vain! Ever in vain!"

The old woman with the shifty look came forward.

"Master Claude Lescot," she said, "is forever over hill and dale in the distant land of Flanders, about his tapestry trade. But this evening, surely, he will be here, as he has sent me word…"

"And I shall tell him everything!" Myrtille went on. "If you knew how he loves me, how he showers me with his tenderness! When I tell him that I want you for my husband, that I shall die if I am not yours, he will be right glad, believe me, to lay my hand in yours!"

"Until tomorrow, then!" said the young man gaily. "And may the worthy Claude Lescot make welcome this Buridan, who then will fancy himself admitted into the paradise of the angels!"

"Beloved!… But is it truly on a day such as this, on the very eve of our happiness, that you would… oh, swear to me you will not go… oh, he shakes his head!… Gillonne, my good Gillonne, help me to persuade him!"

The old woman drew near and laid her withered hand upon the young man's arm.

"So," she said, "you are resolved to speak with Monseigneur Enguerrand de Marigny?"

"This very morning. And since you have surprised this secret, old woman, since your tongue itched and you must needs speak of it to your young mistress, make amends for your fault by telling her the truth: that I run no danger whatsoever."

"No danger!" growled Gillonne. "Madman! One must be possessed of the devil to set oneself against Monseigneur Enguerrand de Marigny! Listen, Jean Buridan, listen: do you not know that the first minister is more powerful than the king himself? Woe to him who dashes himself against such a rock! That man is broken to pieces. For this Marigny knows all, sees all, can do all! One after another, his enemies fall by dagger or by poison. And he has besides the axe and the rope. His eagle eye will read in your very conscience the design you will have breathed only to your own thought, in the silence of the deep nights. His rough hand will seize you in the surest of hiding-places and, all quivering, cast you to the headsman."

Gillonne made the sign of the cross.

"Do you hear?" faltered Myrtille.

A cloud darkened the young man's brow. But, shaking his head:

"Were Enguerrand de Marigny more powerful still, were he escorted by a hundred devils of the most horned and most cloven-hoofed, nothing can keep me from the meeting appointed me by my two brave friends, Philippe and Gautier d'Aulnay. And even had I never promised my aid to those two loyal gentlemen, I hate Marigny as he hates me. The time has come at last that, face to face…"

"Listen!" cried Gillonne.

A pealing of bells came ringing through the air. Myrtille flung her arms about her beloved's neck.

"Jean!" she said in a dying voice, "for pity's sake, do not go!"

Other bells began to ring… then others still, everywhere, throughout Paris, until the air was filled with a vast droning hum.

"There is the king coming forth from his Louvre!" cried Buridan. "It is the hour! Farewell, Myrtille!"

"Buridan! My beloved betrothed!"

"Until tomorrow, Myrtille! Tomorrow, love! Today, vengeance! Tomorrow, the Courtille-aux-Roses! Today, Montfaucon!" And tearing himself from her despairing embrace, he blew a last kiss from his fingertips to Myrtille and rushed out of doors.

Distraught and sobbing, Myrtille fell to her knees before an artless image of the Virgin… At that moment Gillonne, with a furtive step, slipped out of the house into the enclosure, and from the enclosure out onto the road. A man was there who, from a corner of the hedge where he lay concealed, came briskly forward.

"Is it done, Gillonne?"

"Yes, Simon Malingre. And here is the thing." The old woman drew from a pocket a little casket, which the man opened with dread.

And strange indeed was that which the casket contained! It was a figure of wax, adorned with a diadem and clothed in a royal mantle! A pin was driven into its breast, at the very place of the heart! Then Gillonne, her eyes on the watch, her voice sunk low, murmured:

"You shall tell your master, the noble Charles, Count of Valois: this figure is the first spell wrought by the sorceress Myrtille, to the end of killing the king. Myrtille has prepared another, which shall be found in her chamber. Go, Simon Malingre, and repeat these words faithfully to the Count of Valois!" Then Simon Malingre hid the casket beneath his cloak and darted off, skirting the hedgerows. And Gillonne, a livid smile upon her thin lips, went back into the Courtille-aux-Roses and made her way to the chamber where Myrtille knelt praying to the Virgin for her betrothed…

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
