Into that vacuum steps Fausta — the most formidable antagonist Zévaco ever invented, a woman who intends to be pope. The saga's great villain arrives, and Pardaillan finally meets his equal.

Dans ce vide entre Fausta — la plus redoutable antagoniste que Zévaco ait inventée, une femme qui entend être pape. La grande figure du mal fait son entrée, et Pardaillan rencontre enfin son égale.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I — VIOLETTA

On the morning of 12 May 1588, six noblemen were riding at full gallop up the heights of Chaillot. At the summit, their leader halted. Pale with despair, he turned back toward Paris and gazed upon the city for a long while.

A hoarse sob tore from his throat. He stiffened, and flung out these words, which the wind carried away:

"Ungrateful city! Treacherous city! You whom I loved more than my own wife! Tremble, for I shall not re-enter your walls except through a breach!"

At that instant, two horsemen appeared: the first seemed past thirty, magnificently vigorous, with one of those bold and mocking, icy and brilliant physiognomies that leave an indelible impression; the other was eighteen, slender, graceful, marvellously handsome.

The five loyal companions surrounding the fugitive, seeing these two strangers halt, tried to drag him away. But he, raising his arms to heaven, cried:

—A curse upon me! All forsake me. Oh! Who now will take pity on me?

—I will! replied a ringing voice.

The fugitive saw the younger of the two strangers stepping forward… Then a sudden terror seized him:

—You! You! Charles! My brother—have you risen from the grave to crush me?

—You are mistaken, replied the stranger. I am not the man your remorse conjures up; I am not Charles IX. I am his son. I am Charles, Duke of Angoulême.

—Ah! growled the fugitive. So you are the child of Marie Touchet and Charles! You are the bastard of Angoulême! What have you come to ask of Henri III, King of France?

—I shall tell you. I left Orléans to speak to your face! A week ago, Sire, I came of age. On that day, my mother led me to her chamber and uncovered a portrait I had always seen veiled in crêpe: I recognised Charles IX. Then my mother knelt. She told me how the man she had adored had died. I learned of my father's appalling death agony! And I set out to say to the Duke of Guise: Traitor and rebel, what have you done with your king? I set out to cry to Catherine de Medici: Infamous mother! Heartless mother, what have you done with your son? I set out to find Henri de Valois, King of France, and cry to him: What have you done with your brother?…

At this last apostrophe, the King, with a violent jerk, made his horse rear back; then he collapsed upon himself, shaken by a deathly trembling.

An outcry erupted among the five noblemen. At the same instant, they drew their swords… At that moment, the Duke of Angoulême's companion leapt into the midst of the furious group, drew a long rapier, and, perfectly calm:

—Gentlemen, he said, this is a private matter. Let uncle and nephew settle it between them—or else I shall be forced to believe that you belong to the family. And in that case, I shall be obliged to consider myself part of it too!

The swords were about to clash when the King made an imperious gesture. The noblemen checked themselves:

—We shall meet again!… Provided, of course, that monsieur does not hide his name! they growled.

—Gentlemen, said the stranger coldly, my name is the Chevalier de Pardaillan!

The chevalier did not appear to have noticed the prodigious effect produced by his name. He withdrew to one side, as though this violent scene had ceased to interest him. He set about observing a troop of cavalry that, issuing from Paris, was approaching Chaillot—without any great haste, as it happened.

The Duke of Angoulême had not moved. Sombre as a figure of remorse, Henri III turned to him.

—Young man, he said, all that was lacking to my misfortune was to meet you on the road to exile. Pray to Heaven that on the day I reclaim my throne, I may forget that you insulted me in my wretchedness!

—On that day, you will see me rise upon the steps of that throne! I shall tear the royal mantle from your shoulders! Until then, I cannot hate you; you are entitled only to my pity! Paris drives you out; you are nothing but the phantom of a king, haunted by the phantom of a victim. Go then, Sire! For they are already in pursuit of you… Look!… Until you are King of France once more, the son of Charles IX grants you mercy!

Henri III, livid with rage, tried to stammer a few words, which were lost in a sob. But his loyal followers, catching sight of the main body of horsemen emerging from Paris, seized his horse and dragged him away.

Charles d'Angoulême stood lost in thought, his eyes fixed on Paris. What was passing through that soul? Why did this young man not follow with a last look of hatred the king to whom he had just flung such defiance?

Little by little, by degrees, the last reflections of the violent emotions that had just stirred him faded from his face, which then brightened with a very gentle smile.

In a voice of ecstasy, he murmured:

"Paris!… Yes, I come seeking vengeance there… but I also come seeking love!… Paris! It is there that I shall find you again, dear unknown girl who stole my soul. Violetta… sweet violet of love."

At that moment, the Chevalier de Pardaillan approached him and touched his shoulder. With a sweeping gesture, he encompassed all of Paris. And, looking the son of Charles IX in the eye:

—A throne for the taking, Monseigneur!… he declared.

Charles d'Angoulême gave the start of a dreamer torn from the sweetest of visions; and he stammered:

—Pardaillan! Pardaillan! What are you saying?

—I am simply saying that Henri de Valois is no longer King of France, that Henri de Guise is as yet only King of Paris, that Henri de Navarre casts this way his falcon's eye in search of prey—I am saying that makes three men for the same crown… and that it would be a fine thing if that crown could serve me, by placing it upon your head, to pay my debt of gratitude to your mother!

With these words, Pardaillan struck off along a path that skirted Paris and crossed the hamlets of Le Roule and Monceaux before reaching the village of Montmartre.

"Violetta!" murmured the young man. "If only I did indeed have a throne to offer you!…"

And, dazzled and trembling at the prospect now opening before him, Charles d'Angoulême flung himself after his companion, just as the main body of horsemen that had issued from Paris was climbing the slopes of Chaillot. The man riding at the head of these pursuers was a man of thirty-eight, magnificent in dress and stature, handsome of face, haughty of gesture, sombre of expression, his forehead scarred by the gash of an old wound: it was Henri de Lorraine, Duke of Guise.

—Gentlemen, he said, halting, the King is already far away. We must give up any hope of bringing him back to his subjects…

—Say the word, said a nobleman beside him, give me ten good horses, and I'll bring him back alive… or dead!

—Maurevert, have you lost your mind? said the Duke in the same tone. Let him flee! Hullo—what is that hellish apparition?

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
