The bookLe livre

Chico the dwarf knows a way into the conspirators' grotto that none of them suspects, and Pardaillan wants it. Chico wants Juana. Between the two negotiations lies the most purely enjoyable volume of the cycle: Spanish intrigue, secret doors, and a small man who turns out to be the bravest person in the book.

Chico le nain connaît un accès à la grotte des conjurés qu'aucun d'eux ne soupçonne, et Pardaillan le veut. Chico, lui, veut Juana. Entre ces deux négociations se loge le volume le plus franchement réjouissant du cycle : intrigue espagnole, portes dérobées, et un petit homme qui se révèle le plus brave du livre.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I

JUANA'S IDEAS

We have mentioned that Pardaillan, making the most of the time during which the conspirators were withdrawing, had had a rather animated exchange with Chico.

Pardaillan had asked the little man whether there existed some secret entrance, unknown to those presently gathered in the grotto, through which he, Pardaillan, might come and go as he pleased.

The dwarf had been reluctant at first. To him, venturing alone and armed with nothing but a dagger into that den was tantamount to suicide. He could not fathom why the French lord, who had just escaped a hideous death by a miracle, would expose himself thus, seemingly for the sheer pleasure of it.

But Pardaillan had insisted, and since he had a way about him—an entirely irresistible way—of asking certain things, the dwarf had finally relented and led him down a passage where there was, he claimed, an entrance that no one but himself knew of.

We have seen that he was not mistaken, and that indeed neither Fausta nor the conspirators knew of this entrance.

While Pardaillan was in the hall, the dwarf, horribly anxious, fretted in the passage, his hand resting on the spring that worked the invisible door—seeing nothing, hearing nothing of what was happening on the other side of the wall against which he leaned—yet suspecting that there would be a battle, and waiting in anguish for the agreed signal to open the door and secure the retreat of the man he now regarded as a great friend.

When Pardaillan struck the wall with the three agreed knocks, the dwarf hastened to open and welcomed the triumphant chevalier with demonstrations of a joy as boisterous as it was sincere, which gently moved him.

—I truly believed you would not come out of there alive, he said, once he had calmed a little.

—Bah! replied Pardaillan with a smile. My hide is too thick—I am not so easily struck down.

—I do hope we are going to leave now? said Chico, who trembled at the thought that the Frenchman might take it into his head to expose himself yet again, quite needlessly in his view.

To his great satisfaction, Pardaillan said:

—Upon my word, yes! This place may be agreeable enough for creatures of the night, but it holds no attraction and is far too inhospitable for honest folk like Chico. Let us be off!

The sun was rising radiant when Pardaillan, accompanied by Chico, made his entrance at the Inn of the Tower.

In the vast kitchen fireplace, a bright fire crackled, and the housekeeper Barbara, so as not to lose the habit, grumbled and groused about young mistresses who insist on having their own way, and who, after spending the better part of the night on their feet, are the first to rise and adorn themselves in their finest attire, getting in the way of honest, conscientious servants hard at their work.

For indeed little Juana had come down first, having been unable to find the rest she had hoped for.

She was very pale, little Juana, and her eyes, ringed and bright with fever, betrayed great fatigue—or perhaps tears shed in abundance. But however anxious, weary, and bewildered she might be, vanity had not yielded its place in her. And so it was dressed in her richest and finest garments, carefully coiffed, daintily shod, that she went to and fro, her eye and ear forever trained upon the front door, as though she were expecting someone.

Thus she saw perfectly well, and at first glance, Pardaillan come in, Chico at his side, looking triumphant. And at once a smile blossomed upon the pomegranate-red flower that was her lips, her pale cheeks turned rosy, and her anxious eyes, as though misted with tears, recovered all their brilliance, as if by enchantment.

—Ah! Monsieur le Chevalier, here you are, back at last! she cried. Do you know that your friends, Don Cervantes and Don César, are terribly worried about you?

—Well now! said Pardaillan with a smile. I shall reassure them… presently.

But, strange thing—Juana, who had so urgently pressed Chico only hours before to save the chevalier if he could, Juana, who had lavished sincere promises of gratitude and attachment, Juana did not say a single word to the dwarf, whose triumphant air changed to consternation. She did not even seem to see him—or rather, she did. She cast him a glance. But a withering glance, as though she had some unworthy treachery to reproach him with.

Without concerning herself further with the dwarf, Juana asked:

—Seigneur, do you wish to go up and rest at once? Would you care for something to eat first?

—Juana, my pretty one, I wish to take some refreshment first. Have me served the slightest thing—a slice of pâté, with two bottles of French wine.

—I shall serve you myself, Seigneur, said Juana.

—An honour to which I am most sensible, my beautiful child! While you are about it, do see, if they are not sleeping, that Messrs. Cervantes and El Torero are put at ease on my account.

—At once, Seigneur!

Quick, light-footed, and happy, Juana darted up the staircase to inform the French lord's friends of his unhoped-for return, having first signalled to a maid to lay the table.

When Juana had disappeared, Pardaillan turned to Chico and burst into frank laughter—his good, bright, ringing laugh. And as the dwarf regarded him with an air of grieved reproach, he said:

—You do not understand, do you? That is because you do not know women!

—What have I done to her? murmured the dwarf, increasingly bewildered.

Pardaillan shrugged his shoulders and said:

—What you have done is save me.

—But she is the one who begged me to!

—Precisely!

And as the dwarf opened eyes like saucers, Pardaillan laughed with all his heart.

—Do not try to understand, he said. Know only that she loves you.

—Oh! said Chico, incredulous. She did not say a word to me. She scorched me with her eyes.

—That is precisely why I say she loves you.

The dwarf shook his head sorrowfully. Pardaillan took pity on him.

—Listen, he said, and understand, if you can. Juana is glad to see me alive…

—You see—

—But she is furious with you.

—Why? I only did as she asked!

—Exactly!… Juana would certainly have preferred that I not be killed. But she would have preferred that it not be you, specifically, who saved me.

—Because?

—Because I am your rival. The woman who loves will not tolerate one's not being jealous of her. Had you truly loved Juana, you would have been jealous of her. Jealous, you would not have saved me! That is what she tells herself. Do you follow?

—But had I not saved you, she would have turned her back on me. She would have called me a murderer. So then?

—So then, it is better that things are as they are. Do not fret. Juana loves you—or will love you, 'sblood! Do you trust me? Yes or no?

—Yes, as a matter of fact.

—Then leave it to me and stop looking like a lovesick wretch. Your affairs are going well, I promise you.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
