From the opening pagesLes premières pages

CHAPTER XXXIV

———

The Fortune of Capestang

It was thus that Condé was arrested in that very Louvre where, mere hours before, he had believed himself certain to enter as master. Vitry delivered him into the hands of the Marquis de Thémines, who, with a score of gentlemen, had been waiting in the antechamber for this scene to reach its conclusion. Thémines led the prince away under escort and had him placed in a closed carriage: half an hour later, Henri II de Bourbon, Prince de Condé, was nothing more than Number 14 in the Tower of the Treasury, at the Bastille.

Vitry had departed with a strange look cast at Capestang. At a sign from Louis XIII, Luynes likewise withdrew, and he too directed the same sidelong glance at the chevalier. In that double look, Capestang could read all the envy he inspired. Old Marshal d'Ornano, who was the last to leave the royal cabinet, murmured in his ear:

— Young man, if you do not come out of here the king's great favourite, I advise you to flee Paris at full gallop and to put a good hundred leagues between your breast and the daggers that are about to be sharpened.

And turning to the king:

— Sire, he added with a sort of bluntness, it is not Monsieur de Condé who should have been arrested.

— Ha! Ha! Who then? Come, speak, my old friend. Who should have been arrested? Guise? Eh?

— No, Sire: Concino Concini! said the marshal coldly.

He bowed, then withdrew slowly, as though he had waited, hoped, for a cry, an order. But the king remained mute. The marshal departed with a shrug of his shoulders. For the second time, Louis XIII and Capestang found themselves alone together. But this time it was the adventurer who was troubled and the king who was radiant. Louis XIII could still hear those words ringing—words he had cried out in a voice that had astonished even himself:

— Captain, arrest the prince!

His first act as king! The first gesture of his power!

And so he had commanded. And he had been obeyed! A single thunderclap of his voice had sufficed for a prince of the blood to be seized and flung into a dungeon! A single royal gesture had sufficed for Paris in revolt, Paris in ferment, like a storm-tossed sea, to grow calm.

These thoughts agitated this adolescent of fifteen years and filled him with pride. He was like those children to whom someone has just handed an elaborate toy—who marvel when they push a spring and see it work, who wonder at the result without understanding the mechanism. Louis XIII gazed upon Capestang with the same admiring gratitude of a child toward the one who brought him the toy.

— First of all, said the king, tell me about this company of fifty guards that my cousin de Condé had managed to arm, according to what Monsieur de Luçon came to tell us this evening.

— Well, Sire, said Capestang, I would rather tell you things as they happened, from the moment I encountered Cogolin in the Rue de Vaugirard.

— Cogolin? What is Cogolin?

— My squire, Sire. Well then, here is the story from the beginning. It takes place, Sire, in a wretched tavern in the Rue de Vaugirard, which—

— Wait, chevalier! Louis XIII interrupted suddenly, striking the hammer upon a bell three times. Perhaps we shall be more comfortable, for telling and listening, in the dining hall.

Louis XIII had for some moments been searching his mind for what proof of friendship he might offer the chevalier whom he had humiliated with the name of Capitan, and who stood there looking altogether ill at ease, as though bearing some grudge. The signal the king had just given no doubt corresponded to an order already familiar, but on that evening it was interpreted with special magnificence. Indeed, after a few minutes, the door was thrown open wide, and Capestang, dumbfounded, saw an officer enter in full ceremonial dress, sword in hand, who cried:

— The meats of His Majesty!

Behind the officer, four halberdiers. Behind the halberdiers, four officers of the mouth bearing a table. Behind the table, four more halberdiers. The officers of the mouth set the table down in the middle of the cabinet. The halberdiers ranged themselves along the walls, where they stood motionless as caryatids. The table bore, upon its dazzling cloth, gleaming place settings, golden goblets, two candelabra with six wax tapers each, crystal decanters in which the rubies of old Burgundy wines glowed, and a whole array of dishes covered by their silver cloches.

Capestang stood stupefied by this sumptuous spectacle, and involuntarily there rose in his memory the image of that overturned crate upon which, in the depths of the garret, Cogolin had served him one of those succulent hams that double the appetite and triple the thirst. Still, as he was exceedingly hungry, he cast a covetous glance at the splendid table, sniffed the fragrances rising from it, sighed, and thought:

— It is, after all, a glorious thing to be a king. Were I king, I could sit at this table and sample some of these dishes which must be royal indeed, since they dress them in silver. But I am only the Chevalier de Capestang… Bah! I shall watch the king eat—they say it is a great honour—and that will give me an appetite.

If Capestang was astonished, there was someone more astonished still: and that someone was the king! Until now, whenever he had called for his dinner or supper, he had been led to a dining room where he sat before a rather poorly served table. For a moment, Louis XIII stood quite pensive before these honours to which he was unaccustomed.

— Why is my supper being brought here? he asked. And why with this ceremony?

— Sire, replied a silken, caressing voice, in my capacity as superintendent of the palace, it was I who gave the order to bring His Majesty's table here. And as for the ceremony, it is that which was always observed in the presence of great kings!

And Louis XIII saw bowing before him the very man whom Ornano had counselled him to arrest: Concino Concini! Capestang had started. A shiver of anger shook him. Almost in spite of himself, he carried his hand to the hilt of his rapier. But already Concini, without appearing to have seen him, was backing out of the room, bent double, his every step and posture forming one long, continuous bow until he had disappeared. Louis XIII, with a gesture, ordered the halberdiers and officers of the mouth to withdraw as well.

— But, Sire, the lieutenant of the guard observed respectfully, who will serve Your Majesty?

— Bah, monsieur! I shall do as my father did on the evening of the battle of Arques: I shall serve myself! Sit there, across from me, Monsieur le chevalier, and let us sup, for you must be dying of hunger, and I am in a fury of it.

The news spread at once through the Louvre that the king was familiarly sharing his table with the young Chevalier de Capestang, and from that moment, more than one gentleman took up position to waylay the new favourite as he left and pay him court. Concini had swiftly crossed through two or three chambers. He was livid. As in his fits of fury, his lips foamed.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
