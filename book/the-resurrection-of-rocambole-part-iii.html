Chapter One

It had been three days since Baron Philippe de Morlux had last seen his brother Karle, and five since he'd had any news of his son Agénor. The baron was consumed with anxiety. But like all weak natures who dread danger and dare not go out to meet it, he hesitated to send word to the viscount. He hesitated even more to answer his mother-in-law, who hadn't seen Agénor either, even though the young man was supposed to have left for Rennes.

"If Monsieur le Baron would care to read the evening paper, he'll find something interesting in it — something all of Paris has been talking about since last night."

"What is it?" the baron asked, indifferent.

"A riot at Saint-Lazare, sir."

Morlux started at the name. He dismissed the valet, and once the man had gone, seized the paper and scanned it eagerly. His brother Karle had kept him too well informed for him not to recognize, in the young woman referred to only as "A...," that unfortunate child of his own blood, arrested along with a group of thieves and thrown into prison. And the paper said the girl was dead. Dead — Antoinette! Which was to say, murdered. Murdered by the same poisoners who had killed her mother. All his life, Morlux — weak, self-interested — had been the tool of that ruthless man they called the Viscount Karle. All his life he had bent to his brother's will, to that iron yoke. Now and then he'd tried to rebel; now and then some flicker of decency had stirred in his tormented heart. But a burst of Karle's laughter had always smothered it.

Now, though, a face he had spent days trying in vain to forget — a face that haunted his waking thoughts and even his dreams, a face wrecked by long remorse — seemed to rise before him, still crying out:

"Repent! Repent!"

It was the face of Dr. Vincent, the instrument of his first crime. And Morlux thought of that poor girl his son loved, whose hardworking, penniless youth, whose beauty and virtue, his son had described to him. He pictured her laid out pale and cold in her coffin, a victim of his brother Karle's bloody fears. And suddenly, thinking of his own son, the baron thought with horror:

"Agénor could die of this."

But as he sank into the vague terrors that remorse brings, the door opened and the Viscount Karle walked in. The elder Morlux was calm, smiling, moving with the spring of a young man.

"Morning. How are you?" he said breezily. Then, seeing his brother pale and haggard: "What's wrong with you?"

The baron held out the newspaper and pointed to the item headlined: A Drama at Saint-Lazare.

"Word of honor," said the viscount, smiling wider still, "there's never any way to be first with news these days. What business is it of the papers, anyway?"

"So you already knew?"

Karle de Morlux looked at his brother with an expression that seemed to say: this man is an idiot. Then he dropped into an armchair beside the bed, pulled out his cigar case, and began smoking calmly.

"You're so calm," the baron said.

"I wasn't yesterday," Karle replied.

"Ah."

"I'd even call it, quite honestly, a terrible day."

"So you knew what had happened?"

"I was expecting it. But even the cleverest schemes miscarry sometimes, and there's no tool so well forged that it can't break in your hand."

"I don't understand," the baron stammered.

"You know I was using a certain Timoléon."

"Yes."

"He very nearly betrayed us."

"For money?"

"No, out of fear. Picture it — the fool convinced himself we had serious enemies, people who'd sworn to save Antoinette. A certain Rocambole, an escaped convict. Have you ever heard of the club of the Knaves of Hearts?"

"Never," said the baron.

"That man's imagination ran wild. He saw Rocambole everywhere. Though, to be fair, there was some basis for it."

"Oh?"

"Aren't you being looked after by a mulatto I saw here?"

"Yes."

"Well, the night before last, that mulatto was mistaken for Rocambole."

And Karle de Morlux recounted, quite comfortably and with perfect composure, the events of two nights before and the attempted arrest at the Chemin-des-Dames. The baron listened with mounting dread.

"And what makes you so sure none of it's true?" he finally asked.

"The logic of the facts."

"Explain."

"Either Rocambole exists, or he doesn't. And you'll see the conclusion I draw from that, in the manner of a certain Monsieur de la Palisse."

"Go on," said the baron, gradually reassured by his brother's composure.

Karle continued. "If Rocambole exists, he's either weaker than Timoléon claimed, or he never had a hand in our affairs at all. What did we want? To be rid of Antoinette, correct?"

"Yes."

"Well, she's dead. The goal is achieved, and Rocambole is beaten."

"But are you certain she's dead?"

Karle de Morlux laughed. "You think a prison administration amuses itself by publishing false news?"

"Fair point. So who poisoned her?"

"Timoléon took care of it, for fifty thousand francs, which you'll pay to him or to whoever comes on his behalf — because I'm leaving Paris within the hour."

"You're leaving?" the baron exclaimed. "Where are you going?"

"Russia."

Morlux noticed then that his brother was dressed for travel.

"My carriage is waiting downstairs," the viscount said. "I'm catching the Cologne train, which leaves at noon sharp."

"But what could you possibly be going to do in Russia?"

"Honestly, my dear fellow," Karle replied coolly, "you haven't an ounce of memory. Antoinette has a sister."

"Ah, that's right."

"Who's working as a governess in Russia."

"Agénor told me."

"Speaking of Agénor," the viscount said, "let me give you news of him."

"You know where he is?"

"Of course. He's in Angers, in a hotel, in bed, recovering from a sword wound an officer gave him. Don't worry," the viscount added, seeing his brother turn pale, "he won't die of it. But it'll keep him out of our way for at least three weeks, and he'll forget his dear Antoinette."

"But brother," Morlux murmured, "isn't one more crime enough? Have you never feared punishment?"

"Punishment is for fools who let themselves get caught," the viscount said.

"Brother, brother — I'm afraid."

"Afraid of what?"

"Of God," the baron said, raising a hand.

Karle shrugged. "And I'm afraid of the guillotine, do you hear me? So I take my precautions."

"But would that poor child's death by poison really have sent you to the scaffold?"

"Perhaps. Didn't she already know her mother's name? Doesn't one revelation lead to another?"

The baron bowed his head. Karle went on:

"The one in Russia doesn't know anything yet."

"Ah."

"At least, that's what a letter I had stolen from Antoinette's rooms seems to suggest."

"And the old governess — where is she?"

"Still in Auteuil. A bit mad these days. She'll be dead before long."

"But since the other one knows nothing," the baron said.

"She might find out one day."

"Who knows? She'll likely never come back to France."

"That's where you're wrong."

"Oh?"

"I'll even tell you she's already on her way."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
