The Lerouge Case

L'Affaire Lerouge

The 1866 novel that founded detective fiction

The bookLe livre

Thursday 6 March 1862, Bougival: five women report that no one has seen the widow Lerouge for two days. The door is forced. What follows is the first detective novel — the scene read for evidence, the reconstruction from small facts, the amateur who sees what the police cannot, the innocent man the case is built against. Gaboriau published it in 1866; Père Tabaret and the young Lecoq were deducing two decades before Sherlock Holmes existed.

Jeudi 6 mars 1862, Bougival : cinq femmes signalent que personne n'a vu la veuve Lerouge depuis deux jours. On force la porte. Ce qui suit est le premier roman policier — la scène lue comme un indice, la reconstitution à partir de petits faits, l'amateur qui voit ce que la police ne voit pas, l'innocent contre qui le dossier se monte. Gaboriau le publia en 1866 ; le père Tabaret et le jeune Lecoq déduisaient vingt ans avant l'existence de Sherlock Holmes.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

I

✦ ✦ ✦

On Thursday the 6th of March 1862, two days after Mardi Gras, five women from the village of La Jonchère presented themselves at the police station in Bougival.

They reported that for two days no one had caught sight of one of their neighbours, the widow Lerouge, who lived alone in an isolated cottage. They had knocked several times, to no avail. With the windows and door all tightly shut, it had been impossible to get so much as a glimpse inside. The silence, the disappearance — both had alarmed them. Fearing a crime, or at the very least an accident, they were asking that "Justice" be good enough, in order to set their minds at rest, to force the door and enter the house.

Bougival is a pleasant place, its riverbanks crowded every Sunday with boaters; offences are committed there in some number, but serious crimes are rare. The police commissioner therefore refused at first to respond to the petitioners' appeal. Yet they persisted so effectively, they pressed their case for so long and with such determination, that the weary magistrate gave way. He sent for the gendarmerie sergeant and two of his men, requisitioned a locksmith, and, thus accompanied, followed the widow Lerouge's neighbours.

La Jonchère owes what modest celebrity it possesses to the inventor of the sliding railway, who for several years has been conducting public demonstrations of his system there — with more perseverance than success. It is an unremarkable hamlet, perched on the hillside that overlooks the Seine between La Malmaison and Bougival. It lies some twenty minutes from the main road running from Paris to Saint-Germain by way of Rueil and Port-Marly, and is reached by a steep lane, unknown to the roads authority.

The little party, gendarmes at its head, followed the broad embankment road that contains the Seine at this point, and presently, turning right, entered the side path, flanked by walls and sunken deep between them.

After a few hundred paces they arrived before a dwelling as humble as could be, yet of decent appearance. This house — a cottage, really — must have been built by some Parisian shopkeeper with a taste for natural beauty, for every tree had been carefully felled. Deeper than it was wide, it consisted of a ground floor with two rooms and an attic above. Around it stretched a barely tended garden, poorly protected from marauders by a dry-stone wall of roughly a metre in height, which was crumbling in places even so. A light wooden gate, turning on wire hinges, gave access to the garden.

"This is the place," said the women.

The police commissioner came to a halt. During the walk, his following had swelled rapidly with every idler and curious bystander the neighbourhood could supply. He was now surrounded by some forty onlookers.

"No one is to enter the garden," he said.

And to make certain of being obeyed, he posted the two gendarmes on guard at the entrance, then advanced with the gendarmerie sergeant and the locksmith. He himself knocked hard, several times, with the loaded knob of his cane — first at the door, then in succession at every shutter. After each knock he pressed his ear against the wood and listened. Hearing nothing, he turned to the locksmith.

"Open it," he said.

The man unbuckled his kit and readied his tools. He had already inserted one of his picks into the lock when a great clamour broke out among the bystanders.

"The key!" came the cry. "Here's the key!"

Indeed, a boy of about twelve, playing with a companion, had spotted an enormous key in the ditch bordering the road; he had picked it up and was bringing it forward in triumph.

"Give it here, lad," said the sergeant. "We'll see."

The key was tried; it was the right one. The commissioner and the locksmith exchanged a glance heavy with dark misgiving.

"This is bad," the sergeant murmured.

And they went inside, while the crowd, held back with difficulty by the gendarmes, stamped impatiently, craning their necks and stretching up over the wall in the hope of seeing — of catching some glimpse of what was about to unfold. Those who had spoken of a crime had not, unfortunately, been wrong, as the commissioner was convinced the moment he crossed the threshold. Everything in the first room declared with grim eloquence the presence of criminals. The furniture — a chest of drawers and two large cupboards — had been forced and staved in. In the second room, which served as a bedroom, the disorder was even greater. One might have thought a furious hand had taken pleasure in turning everything upside down.

And there, near the fireplace, face in the ashes, lay the body of the widow Lerouge. One side of her face and her hair were burned, and it was a wonder the fire had not caught her clothes.

"Scoundrels," the gendarmerie sergeant muttered. "Couldn't they have robbed her without killing her, the poor woman?"

"But where was she struck?" asked the commissioner. "I see no blood."

"There, between the shoulder blades, Commissioner," the gendarme replied. "Two proper blows, I'll say that. I'd wager my stripes she didn't even have time to cry out."

He leaned over the body and touched it.

"She's stone cold," he went on. "And already past stiff, I'd say. The blow was struck at least thirty-six hours ago."

The commissioner wrote up a summary report as best he could on a corner of the table.

"Now is not the time for speeches," he told the sergeant, "but for finding the guilty parties. The justice of the peace and the mayor must be notified. Beyond that, someone must ride to Paris with this letter for the prosecutor's office. An examining magistrate could be here within two hours. In the meantime I shall proceed with a preliminary inquiry."

"Am I to carry the letter?" asked the sergeant.

"No. Send one of your men — I need you here, to keep those curious people in order and to find me the witnesses I shall require. Everything here must be left exactly as it is. I will set up in the front room."

A gendarme sprinted off toward the Rueil station, and the commissioner at once began the preliminary examination prescribed by law.

Who was this widow Lerouge — where was she from, what did she do, how did she live and on what means? What were her habits, her morals, her associations? Were any enemies known to her; was she miserly; did she have a reputation for keeping money? These were the things the commissioner needed to establish.

But if witnesses were plentiful, they were no better informed for it. The depositions of the neighbours, questioned one after another, were hollow, incoherent, incomplete. Nobody knew anything about the victim, who was a stranger in the district. Many of those who came forward, moreover, had come less to give information than to receive it. Only a market gardener who had been a friend of the widow Lerouge and a milkmaid who supplied her were able to offer any details — modest enough, but precise.

ContextContexte

Émile Gaboriau (1832–1873)

Gaboriau invented the detective novel. His Monsieur Lecoq was reading footprints and reconstructing crimes two decades before Sherlock Holmes existed — Conan Doyle has Holmes dismiss Lecoq by name, which is how we know he had read him. English readers have known Gaboriau almost entirely through nineteenth-century translations that nobody has revisited. The Gaboriau Project gives the father of the genre a modern English voice, starting where the genre started.

Gaboriau a inventé le roman policier. Son Monsieur Lecoq lisait les empreintes et reconstituait les crimes vingt ans avant l'existence de Sherlock Holmes — Conan Doyle fait congédier Lecoq par Holmes, nommément, et c'est ainsi qu'on sait qu'il l'avait lu. Le lectorat anglophone n'a connu Gaboriau qu'à travers des traductions du XIXe siècle que personne n'a reprises. Le Projet Gaboriau redonne au père du genre une voix anglaise moderne, en commençant là où le genre a commencé.
