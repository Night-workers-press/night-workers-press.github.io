The bookLe livre

October 1880, in the Jura. Claire de Beaulieu, proud and of old family, has been engaged since girlhood to her cousin, the Duc de Bligny; when her family loses its fortune, the duke sells his title to the daughter of a chocolate millionaire, Claire's enemy since their convent school. To save her pride, Claire gives her hand on the spot to the neighbour who has loved her in silence, Philippe Derblay, the ironmaster, and on their wedding night tells him she still loves another man. He takes her at her word. A duel between two prides, which comes to a head, as a society novel of 1882 should, with a public insult, a challenge and pistols.

Octobre 1880, dans le Jura. Claire de Beaulieu, fière et de vieille noblesse, est fiancée depuis l'enfance à son cousin, le duc de Bligny ; quand sa famille perd sa fortune, le duc vend son titre à la fille d'un millionnaire du chocolat, l'ennemie de Claire depuis le couvent. Pour sauver son orgueil, Claire accorde aussitôt sa main au voisin qui l'aime en silence, Philippe Derblay, le maître de forges, et le soir des noces lui avoue qu'elle en aime encore un autre. Il la prend au mot. Un duel entre deux orgueils, qui culmine, comme il sied à un roman mondain de 1882, dans une insulte publique, un défi et un duel au pistolet.

Chapter I

On a clear day in October 1880, a young man in elegant shooting clothes was sitting at the edge of one of those fine oak woods that cover the first slopes of the Jura with their cool shade. A big brown spaniel, lying in the heather a few paces from his master, kept his watchful eyes fixed on him, as if to ask whether they would not soon be moving on.

The sportsman seemed in no hurry to set off again. He had propped his gun against a tree trunk and tossed his empty game bag on the bank of the ditch, and now, with his back to the sun and his chin in his hand, he let his eyes wander over the magnificent panorama spread out before him.

On the other side of the road, at whose edge he had stopped, a two-year-old coppice stretched along the high wood, its sparse clumps of shoots standing up like islands of green among the bracken and the tall yellow grass. The wooded ground sloped gently down towards the valley, giving a view, among the meadows, of the village of Pont-Avesnes, which lifted the slate spire of its old church, shaped like a candle-snuffer, above the red roofs of its houses. To the right stood the château, ringed by wide dry moats planted with fruit trees. The Avesnes, a thin thread of water that the inhabitants ambitiously call “the river”, glittered like a silver ribbon between the stunted willows that leaned over its banks, their leaves trembling.

Further off, the works stretched their black walls along the bottom of the hill, the stacks of their blast furnaces spitting red smoke that the wind swept away; the rocky flanks of the hill were pierced with wide openings where the ore was got. Above these diggings the vineyards showed green; they produce a thin white wine with a taste of gunflint, commonly sold as Moselle. The pale blue sky was flooded with light, and a haze as transparent as a light veil floated over the heights. A deep peace lay over this smiling countryside. And the air was so clear that the muffled beat of the forge hammers carried across the distance from the valley up to the forest.

Lulled by the calm that enfolded him, the young sportsman sat motionless. Little by little the landscape had ceased to hold his eyes. A deep sense of well-being had stolen over him, and his ideas were melting into a delicious vagueness. Smiling, he followed his thoughts as they wandered far off into the past. The sun, moving on in its course, gilded the reddening crowns of the high wood; a heavy warmth rose from the heather, and the silence of the woods grew more hushed.

He was suddenly jolted out of his reverie. A cool nose had just come to rest on his knees, while two eyes with a human look in them made him a mute appeal.

“Aha!” said the young man. “Bored, are you, old fellow? Come, don’t be impatient; we’re off again.”

And, getting up with a sigh, he slung his game bag over his shoulder again and tucked his gun under his arm; then, crossing the road, he jumped a little ditch and went into the coppice.

The brown spaniel was already beating the long grass. All at once he stopped beside a bramble patch, on point, one paw raised, neck bent, as still as if he had been turned to stone. His tail stirred feebly, and with his eyes he seemed to be calling his master, who quickly took a few steps forward. At that moment a big hare burst from its form and bolted, showing its yellow rump and streaking away like a bullet. The young man put his gun to his shoulder and fired in haste. When the smoke of the shot had cleared, he saw, without surprise but with annoyance, his hare disappearing into the high wood.

“Missed another!” he murmured.

And, turning to the spaniel, who was waiting for him with a resigned air, he said: “What a shame, eh? And you pointed him so well!”

At that same moment a shot rang out in the high wood, a hundred metres from the young sportsman. Then, after a minute’s silence, footsteps were heard in the sapling thicket, the branches parted, and a strapping fellow in a blue linen shooting smock, big boots and an old hat appeared at the edge of the wood. In one hand he held his gun; in the other he carried, by the hind legs, the hare that had just come out of its form so briskly.

“It seems you have been luckier than I?” said the young sportsman with a smile, going towards the newcomer.

“Ah! So it was you who fired, sir?” said the man in the smock.

“Yes, and very clumsily, for the creature started up right between my legs and I sent my shot after it at twenty paces.”

“No, that was hardly brilliant!” the man in the smock went on ironically. “But how does it come about, sir, that you are shooting in this part of the forest?”

“Why, I am shooting here,” said the young man, a little surprised, “because I have the right to…”

“I think not: these woods belong to Monsieur Derblay, who allows no one whatever to enter them.”

“Ah! The ironmaster of Pont-Avesnes?” the young man replied, a little haughtily. “If I am on his land, it is without knowing it, and I am extremely sorry. I must have strayed. You are Monsieur Derblay’s keeper, no doubt?”

“And you yourself, who are you?” said the man in the smock, without answering the question he had been asked.

“I am the Marquis de Beaulieu, and I beg you to believe that I am not in the habit of poaching.”

At these words the man in the smock coloured deeply and bowed with deference.

“Please forgive me, Monsieur le Marquis. If I had known whom I was dealing with, I should not have taken the liberty of accosting you and asking you to account for yourself. Go on with your shooting, I beg you; it is I who will take my leave.”

While the other man was speaking, the young marquis studied him more closely. For all his rustic clothes, he had a fine air about him. His face, framed by a black beard, was handsome and intelligent. His hands were fine and well kept. What was more, he had just slung over his shoulder a gun of rich simplicity, such as only English gunmakers know how to make.

“Thank you,” the marquis replied coldly, “but I have not the honour of knowing Monsieur Derblay. I know only that he is an awkward neighbour with whom we are on bad terms. I am quite determined not to fire one more shot on his land. I have only been at Beaulieu since yesterday. I hardly know the ground, and my love of shooting has carried me beyond our boundaries. But I shall not be caught here again.”

ContextContexte

Georges Ohnet (1848–1918)

Georges Ohnet was one of the most widely read French novelists of his time, and Le Maître de forges (1882) was his great success: quick, proud, passionate, and built like a play. English readers have known it, if at all, in translations made in the 1880s. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.

Georges Ohnet fut l'un des romanciers français les plus lus de son temps, et Le Maître de forges (1882) son grand succès : vif, fier, passionné, construit comme une pièce de théâtre. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a connu, au mieux, que dans des traductions des années 1880. Les classiques en anglais moderne donnent en entier, dans l'anglais d'aujourd'hui, les classiques français que le lecteur anglophone ne connaît qu'en traductions anciennes ou abrégées. Aucun de ces livres n'est une première traduction ; chacun est complet, et neuf.
