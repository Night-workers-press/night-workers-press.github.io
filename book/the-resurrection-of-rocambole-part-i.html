Chapter One

The bell of the prison had just rung for the midday rest. The guards, worn out from the heavy labor, went looking for shade, since the June sun was blazing down on Toulon. Some of the convicts had taken refuge under the hull of an old ship, others sheltered behind timber beams. A few, defying the heat, lay flat on their bellies on the scorching ground of the Arsenal. Still others paced back and forth in silence, two by two, chained together in their shared disgrace.

"117," said a sort of giant with a dull face and shoulders like a bull's, "I'll play you for the links of my chain, five points of écarté."

"Fine," answered a still-young man, trim in build, with aristocratic hands and a proud, disdainful face.

The giant went on.

"You want to sleep. I want to go under the hull and listen to Monsieur Cocodès's stories — that's what the others call him. If you win, I'll let you sleep. If you lose, you come listen to the stories."

117, who almost never spoke, gave an approving nod, and the two of them sat down on a beam, as far apart as their chain allowed. The giant pulled a greasy deck of cards from his cap and set it down in front of him.

"Who deals?" he said.

He drew a jack. 117 drew a queen and dealt. The giant scored the king and swept the trick. 117 said nothing, and his face showed nothing but perfect indifference. On the next hand, the giant scored the point and said happily:

"Four to nothing!"

117 didn't so much as blink. Then he turned up the king in his turn, swept the trick, and in two more hands the game was won. Seeing the giant's crestfallen face, he said simply:

"Want your revenge?"

The convict's dull eyes lit up; a broad smile spread across his brutish face, and he said to 117:

"You're a good sort... thanks!"

The game started over, and the giant lost again.

"No stories from Cocodès for me," he murmured, resigned.

The convict known in the prison only as 117 stretched out on the beam and closed his eyes. The giant, whom the others called Milon, stayed sitting, casting an envious glance at the half-dozen pairs sheltering under the hull as if under a tent. Then, to pass the time, he took out his cards and started playing solitaire.

Meanwhile the convicts under the hull were chatting among themselves.

"So where's this Cocodès, then?" one of them said.

"I told you he wasn't coming today," answered a green cap.

He added, mockingly:

"These rich boys, these fine gentlemen off the boulevard — with money, they can laugh at the prison. Over nothing at all you'll find them in the infirmary, sleeping in sheets, sipping broth."

"After six months they get unchained," said another, "and put on the half-chain."

"Ah, well," grumbled an old convict who'd just finished a month of double chains for insubordination, "as long as the world's the world, there'll never be equality — not even here."

"He's rich, this Cocodès," the man went on, the same one who'd claimed the fellow they were waiting for was in the infirmary. "His father's a banker, and they send him a hundred francs a month. The warden's taken him on as a secretary, and he comes and goes around town whenever he pleases."

"I heard tell," said another convict, "that there was a fine lady from Paris, a real high-class piece, as they say up there, who came all the way down and took a room at the Hôtel de France just to see him. Seems the young man was living it up. Always front row at the theater, dolled-up girls painted up like picture cards, nights at the Café Anglais, Sundays at the races..."

"So what did the dandy do, to get sent down here to eat prison slop with the rest of us?"

"Forged his boss's signature. He was a notary's clerk."

The old green cap, who was in a sour mood, shrugged.

"I don't care about that, and Cocodès's stories, which you lot swallow like fools, don't amuse me half as much as a story I've got half-figured and would love to know for certain."

"What story?" the others asked, curious.

"117's."

"Nobody at the prison knows it, and if you can figure it out, you're smarter than the rest of us."

"How long's he been here?" asked a newcomer.

"Ten years."

"Where'd he come from?"

"Nobody knows. You know he doesn't talk."

"Could be some prince who fell on hard times," said a credulous convict. "Wouldn't surprise me."

"He's got the airs of a great lord — makes the guards downright uneasy."

"Sure, but they keep a mighty close eye on him too."

"And every morning the warden makes sure to ask whether 117's in his bunk."

"He's never tried to escape, though."

"No," the green cap went on. "Early on they chained him up with a runner. The runner showed him a file.

"'If you're game,' he told him, 'we'll bolt tonight.'

"117 just shrugged, and the next day he asked to be chained with Milon instead."

"Oh, that brute!" said one convict, meaning the giant. "117 must get pretty bored with a partner like that."

"They're good friends, actually," said the green cap.

"They say Milon's innocent?" asked a very young man.

"He says so. We all say so..."

At that, the guards all burst out laughing. Then, suddenly, one of the convicts cried out:

"I knew it — I knew Cocodès wasn't really sick, and that he wouldn't abandon his mates!"

Every head turned, every eye looked out beyond the hull, and a cheer of joy went up. A tall young man was strolling over, puffing on a fat cigar despite the rules, hands in his pockets, sauntering along like a real man about town.

"Long live Cocodès!" the convicts shouted.

"Morning, friends, morning," replied the object of this ovation, in a tone of easy condescension.

He wore the prison uniform, but with small modifications. His red cap was lined with fine cotton; under his jacket he wore a shirt of fine linen, and his loose trousers neatly hid the half-chain, which he'd hooked to a small belt of patent leather.

"Morning, Cocodès," said the green cap. "They said you were sick?"

"I am, my friends. I went into the infirmary this morning."

"But the doctor found you fit for duty?"

"Not at all! The doctor, who's a friend of mine, prescribed rest, decent food, and a little stroll at a pleasant hour of the day."

"You joker, you!"

"What can I say, my good friends," Cocodès went on, "a man has to bear his troubles with patience. I've only got four years left to serve, and I'm arranging things so those four years fly by."

"Cheeky little bug," grumbled the green cap. "Aren't you ashamed to say that in front of me, when I'll die here?"

"Why don't you make a break for it?"

"Bah! I'm an old hand at coming back — I've already run five times, and they always catch me. Besides, I've got no means. I'm not a banker's son! Once you're out, you've got to live. Last time they caught me, I'd just stolen a loaf of bread from a baker... and even that loaf was stale."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
