The Distant Princess

La Princesse lointaine

A play in four acts (1895)

by Edmond Rostand · translated by Sofia E. V. Hamettpar Edmond Rostand · traduit par Sofia E. V. Hamett

ForthcomingÀ paraître

The bookLe livre

A dying poet has fallen in love with a woman he has never seen. He has heard her described, he has written songs about her, and he has hired a ship to carry him across the Mediterranean to find her — a crew of starving ex-pirates who have fallen in love with her by proxy, a doctor who wishes he had stayed home, and a monk who thinks the whole doomed voyage is the best thing that ever happened to any of them. Sarah Bernhardt commissioned it and played the Princess herself.

Un poète mourant s'est épris d'une femme qu'il n'a jamais vue. On la lui a décrite, il lui a écrit des chansons, et il a frété un navire pour traverser la Méditerranée et la rejoindre — un équipage d'anciens pirates affamés tombés amoureux par procuration, un médecin qui regrette d'être parti, et un moine qui tient ce voyage perdu pour la meilleure chose qui leur soit arrivée. Sarah Bernhardt la commanda et joua elle-même la Princesse.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

ACT ONE

The deck of a ship that has plainly come through a long and terrible crossing. There has been a storm: sails in rags, yards snapped, rigging in a tangle, a patched mast leaning. There has been a fight: bloodstains, weapons scattered. Night ending. Grey transparent shadow. A paling sky. Stars thinning out. A violet sea under scarves of vapour. A horizon of fog.

Through the act, the light changes imperceptibly.

SCENE I

THE SAILORS: BRUNO, BISTAGNE, MARRIAS, PÉGOFAT, TROBALDO, FRANÇOIS, etc., THE PILOT, then THE SHIPMASTER and BROTHER TROPHIME

(Curtain up: sailors sprawled or sitting in every direction, tragic-faced, pale, wasted; they look worn out with hardship and hunger. Some, wounded, are roughly bandaged with rags. Two of them, upstage, are swinging a third, motionless, by the head and the feet.)

THE TWO SAILORS: PÉGOFAT and BRUNO, upstage.

One... two... three... heave!

(They pitch the body over the rail. The splash is heard.)

PÉGOFAT

Done.

BRUNO

One more shipmate

who'll never swim your harbour, Tripoli!

PÉGOFAT, taking off his cap toward the dead man

Goodbye, lad!

BRUNO, looking into the distance

Dawn soon. A flush of red.

(They come back down.)

FRANÇOIS, waking and stretching

Who's just gone over?

BRUNO

Audriu the Cutthroat.

FRANÇOIS

Cursed fever!

(He looks at the wrecked deck.)

Well — the sea made a job of it!

BISTAGNE, lifting his head

And the wind, don't forget! Not a bowline left!...

BRUNO

Not a halyard!

FRANÇOIS

That mast could come down. Better to cut it away.

BISTAGNE

Me, I'd like to eat.

BRUNO

Nothing left in the stores!

FRANÇOIS, getting up

Ah! my wound!...

(He staggers.)

Ho! ho!... We can hardly stand!

Let one more Barbary ship come at us now

and we won't be able to fight!...

BRUNO

We'll fight!

Because we have to get there! Nothing stops that!

God help any ship that picks a quarrel with us!

BISTAGNE

When do we finally sail into Saracen water?

THE PILOT

Soon, I hope. But the weather's been so foul!

Ah — if I had the needle that tells you north!

And the stone you rub it on!...

BISTAGNE, shrugging

What rubbish!

THE PILOT

No. There are men who have one, in a gourd:

you rub it. Iron is in love with the stone.

Then the needle turns and says north. It's simple.

ALL THE SAILORS

Ha! ha! — Idiotic!... Listen to him! — A needle!

PÉGOFAT

Bah! never mind needles, let the tub sail on!

— Look, the weather's clearing, our troubles are over!

BRUNO

You think so? Well then, what about thirst?

FRANÇOIS

And hunger?

BISTAGNE

Yes — what we've been through!

PÉGOFAT

Heaven help us!

TROBALDO, appearing astride a yardarm

The joke of it would be if she turned out ugly!

ALL THE SAILORS

Oh no, she's very beautiful! — She is! —

TROBALDO

By God,

she'd better be, Bistagne!

BISTAGNE

And more than a bit,

son, after the dangers we've run for Her!

Or else I take it personally!

BRUNO

She has to be beautiful!

ALL

She is! — She is!

MARRIAS

I'm certain of it!

AN OARSMAN

Ah, but!

It would gall me to be rowing toward a monster!

PÉGOFAT, laughing

He thinks about it while he rows, the Marseillais!

THE OARSMAN

Constantly!

BISTAGNE

A princess is always beautiful. Don't you worry.

THE PILOT, shrugging

You talk of nothing but Her.

PÉGOFAT

We're so tired!

Look at us: we talk of Her, and we're almost cheerful.

THE PILOT

Will they even show you this bird of yours?

BRUNO

The Prince promised to bring us in front of Her

if we arrive, and to tell her it was us

who carried him all the way to her knees!

THE PILOT

And you think a princess talks to the likes of us?

PÉGOFAT

No. But we'll see her. That's already something.

In every Christian country they talk of nothing but Her!

A SHIP'S BOY

And of her eyes!

THE PILOT, turning on him

You want to see her eyes, do you?

THE BOY

Well, yes!

PÉGOFAT

The master!

(The shipmaster has been standing there a while, listening.)

THE SHIPMASTER

To see them we first have to get there;

and Joffroy Rudel, our good prince, has to live!

THE SAILORS

Is he worse? — Poor man!

BRUNO

What rotten luck!

THE MASTER

Look, they've shut the cabin door.

His friends are watching him. He must be resting.

PÉGOFAT

Last night he was still singing!

BISTAGNE

It's an astonishing

thing, the ease with which he makes up songs!

FRANÇOIS

What do you call that thing he gets the sounds out of?

THE PILOT, with an air of authority

That's called a lyre.

FRANÇOIS

Ah!... a lyre! — Well,

it makes a pretty noise.

BISTAGNE

Very sweet while you're rowing!

PÉGOFAT

And when there's hauling to do, it gives you a lift!

THE MASTER

Hush — the Prince's chaplain.

PÉGOFAT

Ah, yes!... the padre!

(Brother Trophime, in a patched and threadbare robe, comes out of the cabin, looks up at the sky, and goes upstage to kneel.)

BRUNO

A priest who doesn't get in the way.

FRANÇOIS

Good sort. Honest.

BISTAGNE

Ah, if only every priest had his wide sleeve!

THE MASTER

The lamps of heaven have put out their last

pale fires...

BISTAGNE

Day's breaking.

(A whiter light spreads.)

BROTHER TROPHIME, kneeling

Virgin of sailors,

you who turned the savage sea into a gentle one,

slide this ship into harbour like a great swan.

Virgin, and after that, if he lives, Sir Rudel

will set a ship for you on the altar at Tortosa,

a copy in silver of the one we wandered in,

with her rudder, her sails and her oars!

THE PILOT

Pfft!... all that!... If I had my needle!

BISTAGNE

Animal!

In any case it can't do any harm.

(Erasmus comes out in his turn. A doctor's gown in tatters. Bare-headed, looking sorry for himself. The sailors snigger.)

BRUNO

Now the old quack, showing his face.

FRANÇOIS

The medico.

BISTAGNE

Not up to much!

TROBALDO, shrugging

A freshwater doctor!

SCENE II

BROTHER TROPHIME, ERASMUS, THE SAILORS, upstage

BROTHER TROPHIME, going to Erasmus

Master Erasmus — how is he?

ERASMUS

Worse and worse.

The Prince is asleep. Sir Bertrand is watching him.

(Looking at the horizon.)

Well, Brother Trophime, well — nothing to be made out

but fog!

(Furious.)

Me. Me, a physician of Salerno.

I ask you: what am I doing in the middle of this?

My chair, my fire, my books — where are they?

The sea wind that reduced my gown to rags

has carried off, one after another, every cap I own!...

BROTHER TROPHIME

The Prince?...

ERASMUS

Eh! and why did that idler of idlers,

that poet, come and throw himself into this?

When I entered his household — a mild, frail prince —

it was to live quietly under his roof.

It was to look after him on land, not at sea.

I find this voyage extremely bitter!

(Pacing, with mounting rage.)

Ah, may hell roast and the devil spit

those cursed pilgrims out of Antioch

who were the first to speak of her, at the castle,

one evening at supper, at the hour when the carver's knife

was going into a fat bird —

Rostand in English

Rostand is known in English for one play. Cyrano de Bergerac has never been out of print, never off the stage and never short of translators; everything else he wrote — six more plays, two books of poems, a great deal of occasional verse — is almost entirely missing. What English versions exist are mostly Edwardian, made within a few years of the originals by different hands, and they read now like translations of a translation: a fog of thees and thous laid over a writer who was, in French, fast, funny, theatrical and modern. Rostand in English is the first attempt to change that properly — one translator, one series, one voice.

Rostand n'est connu en anglais que pour une pièce. Cyrano de Bergerac n'a jamais cessé d'être imprimé, joué, retraduit ; tout le reste — six autres pièces, deux recueils de poèmes, quantité de vers de circonstance — manque presque entièrement. Les versions anglaises existantes sont pour l'essentiel édouardiennes, faites à quelques années des originaux par des mains différentes, et se lisent aujourd'hui comme des traductions de traduction : un brouillard d'archaïsmes posé sur un écrivain qui était, en français, rapide, drôle, théâtral et moderne. Rostand in English est la première tentative d'y remédier vraiment — un traducteur, une collection, une voix.
