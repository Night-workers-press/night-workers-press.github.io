Chapter One

The day after Miss Ellen went to see the Reverend Peters Town — while the Grey Man was slipping away right in the middle of Whitehall, a stone's throw from Scotland Yard, the police headquarters — a very different scene was unfolding on the Thames.

Around nine that evening, a man was making his way down past Charing Cross station, along the sunken boardwalk that led to one of the steamboat landings.

This man was none other than Shocking — but a very well-dressed Shocking, the kind of man everyone would have taken for a gentleman, if not quite a lord.

The steamboats ran fairly late into the evening, until ten or eleven o'clock; only the ones that went as far as Greenwich stopped early, at seven in summer and five in winter.

Since the night was cold, though, there were few travelers on the landing stage.

Two women and a man were waiting there alone when Shocking arrived.

The penny-boat could be heard whistling somewhere on the far side of Westminster, its black plume of smoke just visible through the fog.

Shocking was bundled up warmly in a brand-new waterproof. Even so, he kept blowing on his fingers and letting out the occasional emphatic "brrr!"

One of the two women on the landing, who looked rather wretched, was saying to her companion, "I only hope there's room by the boiler so we can get a bit warm."

Shocking had never much cared for solitude — in fact he could be quite the talker when the mood struck him. Hearing the woman's wish, he stepped closer.

"You needn't worry, my dear," he said. "There's never much of a crowd aboard at this hour, in weather like this."

"It's just that I'm so cold," she said.

Shocking looked over the clothes the woman was wearing: a shabby wool dress and a scrap of shawl — that was all. No stockings on her feet, a battered wreck of a hat on her head, and a poor kerchief crossed over her neck, no doubt hiding the fact that she had no linen underneath.

"Are you going far?" Shocking asked.

"To Rotherhithe, below London Bridge. I'd have walked, honestly — I've been waiting nearly a quarter of an hour for the penny-boat," the woman went on, "but I'm quite worn out. I've been walking all day today."

"Really?" said Shocking, who wanted nothing more than to chat.

"I've been back and forth three or four times since this morning, between Southwark, where I live, and the City."

"Four good long walks," said Shocking. "That's eight or nine miles at least, there and back."

"About that," said the woman.

Then she added with a sigh, "And all for nothing."

The penny-boat was just then arriving, and it pulled up to the landing. Shocking had no time to press the woman further about the purpose of these four trips made in a single day.

He hopped down from the landing onto the little steamer, where there were barely a dozen people aboard, which let the woman who'd been complaining of the cold go sit right by the boiler. Seeing this, Shocking sat down beside her and picked the conversation back up.

"So," he said, "you've been to the City four times?"

"Yes, sir. And for nothing."

Shocking waited for her to explain. Clearly she wanted nothing more herself, because she went on at once.

"I went to Whitecross."

"The debtors' prison?"

"That's the one. My husband's in there."

"Poor man," said Shocking. "Is it much money?"

"Oh, no, sir — and a charitable soul who came to see me yesterday gave me the sum needed to free him."

"So you got him out, then?"

"I haven't managed it yet, sir."

"How's that?"

"Oh, it's quite a story — you'll see how unlucky poor people can be sometimes, chased by the worst kind of misfortune."

"I'm listening," said Shocking, while the steamer sped on down the Thames.

"My husband's name is Paddy," she went on. "He was put in prison at the request of a certain Pussex, a baker who lived in our neighborhood for a long time and has now retired to Rotherhithe. That's where I'm going now — it's my last hope."

"But," said Shocking, "I thought all you had to do was show up at the debtors' prison with the money, and the prisoner would be released on the spot."

"That's what I thought too," said the woman. "I was given the money last night. So I got up first thing this morning, and I was there almost before it was light.

"The gatekeeper, a Mr. Golmish, shut the window in my face and told me, 'It's too early. Come back at noon.'

"I went home, because I have two children and I hate leaving them alone too long."

"And you went back at noon?"

"Yes, sir. That time they let me in, and I saw my husband. But when I tried to pay, they told me only Mr. Cooman, the governor, could take the money — and that Mr. Cooman, who never leaves the place, happened to be out of Whitecross that very day, because he was having breakfast with the Lord Mayor and the aldermen in the great hall of the Guildhall.

"They told me he wouldn't be back until two, and I had to go away again."

"Poor woman," said Shocking.

"At two I came back."

"And you found Mr. Cooman?"

"Yes, sir. But when I showed him my money, he said it wasn't the right amount — and the truth is, someone had added a zero by mistake, so instead of ten guineas it read a hundred.

"It did no good arguing that His Honor was wrong. His Honor was a bit worse for the breakfast, and he had me thrown out.

"That was the third time I went home without my husband."

"And you went back a fourth time?"

"Yes, sir. I remembered perfectly the man who'd had my husband arrested — a bailiff named Calmiche, who lodges right next door to us, on Adam Street.

"So I went back to Southwark and found Calmiche, and told him the whole story. He agreed I was right, that there'd been a mistake in the books, and he offered to come with me.

"The bailiff argued to His Honor, Mr. Cooman, that it was impossible a poor devil like my husband could ever have owed a hundred pounds.

"His Honor said, 'Very well — let the creditor sign a receipt for ten, and he can go.'"

"And that's why you're going to Rotherhithe?"

"Yes, sir."

While Shocking talked with the woman — who was, as one might guess, the very same woman Miss Ellen had called on the day before — the penny-boat had already passed London Bridge and was soon to reach the Rotherhithe landing.

The man who had boarded at Charing Cross at the same time as Shocking and the two women had stayed up at the bow the whole way. But now he came over and looked closely at Shocking.

"Well, by St George, patron saint of free England!" he said suddenly. "I'm not mistaken — it's Lord Wilmot!"

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
