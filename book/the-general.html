The bookLe livre

Before he wrote about himself, Alexandre Dumas wrote about his father. Born in Saint-Domingue in 1762, the son of a Norman marquis and an enslaved woman, Thomas-Alexandre Dumas enlisted as a private in the Queen's Dragoons and was general-in-chief of one of the armies of the Republic at thirty-one. He held a bridge alone against Austrian cavalry, quarrelled with Bonaparte in Italy and again in Egypt, spent twenty months in the prisons of the King of Naples, and died at Villers-Cotterêts in 1806, when his son was three and a half. The first twenty chapters of the Mémoires, complete, published as a book of their own.

Avant d'écrire sur lui-même, Alexandre Dumas a écrit sur son père. Né à Saint-Domingue en 1762, fils d'un marquis normand et d'une femme réduite en esclavage, Thomas-Alexandre Dumas s'engage comme simple soldat aux dragons de la Reine et commande en chef l'une des armées de la République à trente et un ans. Il tient seul un pont contre la cavalerie autrichienne, se brouille avec Bonaparte en Italie puis en Égypte, passe vingt mois dans les prisons du roi de Naples et meurt à Villers-Cotterêts en 1806, quand son fils a trois ans et demi. Les vingt premiers chapitres des Mémoires, complets, publiés en volume séparé.

Chapter I — My Name Is Disputed

My birth—My name is disputed—Extract from the registers of civil status of Villers-Cotterêts—The club at Corbeil—My father's marriage certificate—My mother—My maternal grandfather—Louis-Philippe d'Orléans, father of Philippe-Égalité—Madame de Montesson—Monsieur de Noailles and the Académie—A morganatic marriage.

I was born at Villers-Cotterêts, a small town in the department of the Aisne on the road from Paris to Laon, two hundred paces from the Rue de la Noue, where Demoustier died, eight kilometres from La Ferté-Milon, where Racine was born, and twenty-eight kilometres from Château-Thierry, where La Fontaine was born.

I was born there on the 24th of July 1802, in the Rue de Lormet, in the house that belongs today to my friend Cartier, who will be good enough to sell it to me one day, so that I can go and die in the room where I was born, and pass into the night of the future at the same spot where I came out of the night of the past. I was born there on the 24th of July 1802, at half past five in the morning; which makes me, at the moment I begin these Memoirs, that is to say on Monday the 18th of October 1847, forty-five years and three months old.

I am one of the men of our time who have had the most things disputed. They have disputed even my name of Davy de la Pailleterie, which I never cared much about, since I have never used it, and since it will be found after my name of Dumas only in the official deeds I have signed before a notary, or in the civil acts in which I have figured as a principal or as a witness.

So I ask permission, so that all argument on the subject may cease, to transcribe my birth certificate here.

Extract from the registers of civil status of the town of Villers-Cotterêts.

"On the fifth day of the month of Thermidor, Year X of the French Republic.

"Birth certificate of Alexandre Dumas-Davy de la Pailleterie, born this day at half past five in the morning, son of Thomas-Alexandre Dumas-Davy de la Pailleterie, general of division, born at Jérémie, on the island and coast of Saint-Domingue, residing at Villers-Cotterêts, and of Marie-Louise-Élisabeth Labouret, born at the said Villers-Cotterêts, his wife.

"The sex of the child has been recognised as male.

"First witness: Claude Labouret, maternal grandfather of the child; second witness: Jean-Michel Deviolaine, forest inspector of the fourth communal district of the department of the Aisne, twenty-sixth conservancy, residing at the said Villers-Cotterêts.

"At the request made to us by the child's father, and have signed:

"Al. Dumas, Labouret and Deviolaine.

"Certified according to law by me, Nicolas Brice-Mussard, mayor of the town of Villers-Cotterêts, performing the functions of registrar of civil status.

"Signed: Mussart."

I have underlined the words his wife, because, while disputing my name of Davy de la Pailleterie, those who disputed it relied on the fact that I was a bastard.

If I had been a bastard, I should simply have accepted the bar sinister, as more famous bastards than I could ever have been have done, and, like them, I should have worked so hard with body or mind that I should have come to give my name a value of its own. But what can I say, gentlemen! I am not one, and the public will have to do as I do, that is to say, resign itself to my legitimacy.

Then they fell back on my father. In a club at Corbeil, in 1848, a gentleman very well dressed, I must say, and who I was assured belonged to the magistracy, which I should never have believed if the assurance had not come from people worthy of belief; a gentleman who had read, in I do not know what biography, that it was not I but my father who was the bastard, this gentleman told me that if I did not sign my name Davy de la Pailleterie, it was because my father had never borne that name, seeing that he was not the son of the Marquis de la Pailleterie.

I began by calling this gentleman by the name one calls people who say such things to you; but as the name I gave him seemed to be as much a matter of indifference to him as if it had been his own family name, I wrote to Villers-Cotterêts for a second extract from the registers of civil status to be sent to me, concerning my father, as a first one had already been sent me concerning myself.

So I ask the reader's permission to put this second extract before his eyes; if he should have the bad taste to prefer our prose to that of the secretary of the town hall of Villers-Cotterêts, let him take it up with that gentleman from Corbeil. (It should be said that this incident, which happened in 1848, is an interpolation in this chapter, which was written in 1847.)

"In the year one thousand seven hundred and ninety-two, the first of the French Republic, on the 28th of the month of November, at eight o'clock in the evening, after the publication of a bann made at the principal door of the town hall on Sunday the 18th of the present month, and posted since that time in the place appointed for the purpose, of the future marriage between Citizen Thomas-Alexandre Davy de la Pailleterie, aged thirty years and eight months, colonel of the Hussars of the Midi, born at La Guinodée, at Trou-Jérémie, in America, son of the late Alexandre-Antoine Davy de la Pailleterie, formerly commissary of artillery, who died at Saint-Germain-en-Laye in June 1786, and of the late Marie-Cessette Dumas, who died at La Guinodée, near Trou-Jérémie, in America, in 1772, his father and mother, of the one part; and Citizeness Marie-Louise-Élisabeth Labouret, daughter of full age of Citizen Claude Labouret, commanding the National Guard of Villers-Cotterêts and proprietor of the Hôtel de l'Écu, and of Marie-Joseph Prévot, her father and mother, of the other part;

"The said parties being domiciled, the bridegroom in garrison at Amiens, and the bride in this town. The whole done in the presence of the citizens and citizenesses:

"Louis-Brigitte-Auguste Espagne, lieutenant-colonel of the 7th Regiment of Hussars in garrison at Cambrai, native of Auch, department of the Gers;

"Françoise-Élisabeth Retou, stepmother of the bridegroom, widow of the late Antoine-Alexandre Davy de la Pailleterie, residing at Saint-Germain-en-Laye.

Alexandre Dumas (1802–1870)

When Alexandre Dumas sat down in 1847 to write his memoirs, he did not begin with himself. He began with his father, the General, and gave him twenty chapters, quoting the birth certificates and the army orders in full to silence the men who disputed his name. The Mémoires have been in English since 1907, in a six-volume Edwardian translation and in abridgements since; this book gives the General's whole story in the English of today. Classics in Modern English gives the French classics that English readers know only in old or abridged translations, whole and in the English of today. None of its books is a first translation; each is complete, and new.
