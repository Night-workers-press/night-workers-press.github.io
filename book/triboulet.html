The bookLe livre

"Here, Triboulet!" The king calls his jester the way one calls a dog, and the twisted little man barks back, because that is the joke and the joke is his living. Under it: a father, a daughter, and a court where being amusing is the only protection there is. Zévaco's cruellest and most tender novel.

« Ici, Triboulet ! » Le roi appelle son fou comme on appelle un chien, et le petit homme contrefait aboie en retour, parce que c'est la plaisanterie et que la plaisanterie le fait vivre. Dessous : un père, une fille, et une cour où être drôle est la seule protection. Le roman le plus cruel et le plus tendre de Zévaco.

CHAPTER I

Le Roi

* * *

"Here, Triboulet!"

King François the First, in a voice brimming with mirth, flung out this brief and disdainful summons.

The twisted, hunchbacked, misshapen creature thus addressed gave a start; his eyes flashed with a lightning-stroke of anguished hatred. Then his tormented face split, of a sudden, into a sneering grin, and he advanced, imitating the furious baying of a mastiff.

"Come now, buffoon — what is the meaning of this barking?" demanded the King, his brows knit together.

"Your Majesty does me the honour of addressing me as one of his dogs; I answer him as a dog would. It is my way of making myself understood, Sire!"

And Triboulet bowed, bent double. The few gentlemen who were present burst into wild hoots of laughter.

"On thy belly!" cried one of them. "A dog lies down, Triboulet!"

"A dog bites, too, sometimes, Monsieur de la Châtaigneraie. Witness the snap that Jarnac gave you — in the form of a slap!"

"Wretched insolent cur!" roared La Châtaigneraie.

"Peace!" commanded the King, laughing. "Now then, master fool, speak without disguise: how dost thou find me to-day?"

Standing before the immense mirror — a gift from the Venetian Republic — King François the First contemplated and admired himself, whilst two bustling valets finished adjusting his toque of black velvet with its white plume, his doublet of cherry satin, and his mantle of furs.

"Sire," replied Triboulet, "thou art as handsome as the lord Phœbus!"

"Why Phœbus?" enquired the monarch.

"Because, like the head of Phœbus, Your Majesty's head is surrounded by rays — only, the rays are formed by the white hairs of your beard and your hair!"

Triboulet retreated, shaking his marotte and grinding out his snickering laugh. The gentlemen murmured, indignant at such audacity; but the King had laughed, and so they laughed louder than the King, louder than Triboulet.

François the First drew up his tall frame with its athlete's shoulders and broad chest, made for heavy armour.

He turned to his gentlemen:

"And thou, Essé — how dost thou find me?"

"Never has Your Majesty seemed more vigorous; you grow younger by the day!"

"Count! Count!" yelped Triboulet. "You will make the King believe he is falling back into infancy. It will come to that, but he is only fifty yet, devil take it!"

"And thou, Sansac?" asked the King.

"Your Majesty remains a model of elegance…"

"Yes," interrupted the fool, "and yet you do not strap a hump to your belly the better to imitate the prominent elegance of the royal paunch! I, at least, have one on my back!"

The courtiers darted hateful glances at him, which he parried with grimaces. The King fell to laughing.

"Sire," cried La Châtaigneraie then, with vexation, "will Your Majesty deign to explain whence comes this fine gaiety to-day?"

"By God!" cried Triboulet shrilly. "The King is thinking of the peace his cousin the Emperor has imposed upon him: he loses only Flanders and Aragon, Artois and the Milanese. Nothing to weep over, I should think!"

"Buffoon!"

"No?… That is not it?… Perhaps the King is thinking of the massacres being carried out for Our Mother the Church… Provence drowned in blood!… That, too, fills me with merriment!"

"Silence!" growled the King, all pallor before the spectres the fool had just conjured.

And he hastened to resume:

"Gentlemen, a great expedition this evening!… Ah! I am fifty years old! Ah! They say I grow old!" he added feverishly, as though to deafen his own thoughts. "We shall see! After Marignan, they said: Brave as François! I would have them say still, and always: Young as François! Gallant as François! By Our Lady, let us laugh, my friends, since life is so sweet and the women so beautiful in our land of France…"

"God's day, my friends! Love! Ah, the divine music of that word: I love!… If you knew how beautiful she is in her innocence, and how her seventeen springtimes set upon her angel's brow a halo of purity!… And it is that which inflames me and sends torrents of fire through my veins! It is that purity which shines in her gaze, it is all that virginity which tempts me, draws me, drives me to madness!…"

Before this sudden confession bursting from the lips of François the First, the courtiers fell silent, uneasy… Who was this young maiden the King loved? The monarch was now pacing in agitation. Once more, the great mirror caught his gaze.

"No, I am not fifty! I am so young! I feel it in the mighty beating of my heart, in the love that rages in my head. I love, and I will have her love me!…"

"And if she will not love thee — she?" asked Triboulet, with a sneer in which there lurked a muffled anguish.

"She will love me! For such is my good pleasure… This evening!… At ten o'clock… You will be there… You will aid me…"

"Certainly, Sire!" cried d'Essé. "But what will the fair Madame Ferron say when she learns…"

"La Ferron! She wearies me! She bores me to death! I want no more of her! She has become a chain upon me!"

"A fine ferronnière!" exclaimed Triboulet.

"Triboulet, the jest is priceless!" cried the King, beaming. "It must be given to Marot, that he may set it in some ballade… La belle Ferronnière!… Charming!"

"I shall give the jest to Marot," said Triboulet. "But you will sign the ballade, Sire!"

"Triboulet, thou wilt be part of the expedition this evening?" resumed François, who feigned not to have heard this allusion to his plagiarisms.

"By God, my prince! It would be a fine thing to see the King of France commit a folly that was not countersigned by his fool!"

Withdrawn into the embrasure of a window, Triboulet watched night fall upon the half-finished constructions of the new Louvre. And within himself, the jester brooded:

"He said: a young maiden of seventeen… Who can this child be?… I am afraid!…"

An expression of dread, of grief, of mortal anguish froze upon his tormented visage. What fearsome riddles were churning in that wretched heart?

"As for la Ferron," François the First continued, "as for Madeleine Ferron, I go to her this very hour… And I have in store for her such a surprise that never again shall there be any possibility of refastening the ferronnière!…"

"Let us hear the surprise?" asked Sansac.

At that moment the door of the royal chamber opened. A man clad in black, his countenance livid, appeared.

"Behold the Comte de Monclar!" declaimed Triboulet, who, turning round, resumed his mask of sardonic mirth. "Behold the grand audiencier, Grand Prévôt of Paris, austere master of our police, justly feared by all the truands, cutpurses, ragamuffins, and minions of Galilee!"

The Comte de Monclar had advanced toward the King, before whom he stood in a deep bow.

"Speak, Monsieur," said François the First.

"Sire, I come to submit the list of audience requests, that Your Majesty may designate those of his subjects he will deign to receive. First, the sieur Étienne Dolet, printer at the sign of the Dolouère d'Or."

"I will not receive him," said the monarch harshly. "You will keep close watch upon this man, who has strange dealings with the new sects that poison my kingdom… What else?"

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
