Chapter Two

The Unsigned Commission

The Baron Hector de Nossac was a young man of twenty-six, of excellent birth, well made, light of mind, of proven courage, and enjoying at court the reputation of a man who did well with women. Never was a reputation better deserved. The baron was handsome, magnificent, inconstant, a gambler and a quarreller, and he had a settled weakness for Spanish snuff and the wine of Aÿ.

The Duchess du Maine had enrolled him in the Order of the Honey Bee; he had dipped into the Cellamare conspiracy, and Dubois had had him shut up in the Bastille. On the death of the worthy cardinal, Simiane reconciled him with the Regent, and the Regent gave him a regiment. One glance from Madame de Phalaris set him at odds with the Duke d’Orléans again, and the Duke d’Orléans took his commission away. An uncle of a kind they no longer make died exactly on cue, the day after his disgrace, leaving him 200,000 livres a year.

The baron spent the said income in six months, and something more besides. Then he thought of getting back into favour, and, reckoning that for this it was absolutely necessary to provide himself with a suitable mistress, he fixed his choice on the Duchess d’A—, who, at the threshold of the story we are about to tell you, was on the point of obtaining for him the governorship of the province of Normandy. Now, the day on which we have just seen the Baron de Nossac chatting with the Duchess d’A— from his bed was precisely 2 December 1723.

While the duchess was reaching her carriage, which waited for her at the little garden door, another carriage came in through the main gate. A gentleman of high bearing, though very thin, got down from it and had himself taken up to the baron at once. This gentleman was Monsieur de Simiane.

“Ah, there you are, my dear,” said the baron negligently.

“Yes,” Simiane answered quickly.

“Good God, what a hunted look you have. What has happened to you, marquis? Where have you come from? Has some husband of poor breeding had you thrashed by his people?”

“My dear fellow,” said Simiane, without answering Nossac’s rather impertinent question, “it is high time you married.”

“So much the worse, my dear. I shall not marry. I have my governorship.”

“Do you think so, baron?”

“I am quite sure of it.”

“And I am quite sure of the contrary. The Regent has not had time to sign your letters.”

Monsieur de Nossac started.

“What does that mean, marquis, and what is this has not had time?”

“Just that. The Regent died in the night.”

The baron cried out.

“Of apoplexy.”

“You are dreaming, marquis. It is impossible. The Duchess d’A— has just left this house and knew nothing about it.”

“There are a good many others who don’t know it either. Nobody will know until tomorrow. And here — I’ll wager you that before tonight the Duchess d’A— will be under arrest.”

“Why, marquis?”

“Because she is the sworn enemy of Madame de Prie.”

“Well?”

“Where on earth have you been living, my dear fellow?” exclaimed Simiane. “Don’t you know that the Marquise de Prie is the mistress of the Duke de Bourbon?”

“Yes, of course.”

“Then I shall teach you something else. The Duke de Bourbon is First Minister.”

The baron went pale.

“Monseigneur de Fréjus,” Simiane went on, “has generously stood aside. That prelate is preserved in piety and is never in a hurry. But rest easy — he will lose nothing by waiting.”

“So that my governorship —”

“Mourn it and be done, that is the wisest thing.”

“And this marriage?”

“You must either give it up or conclude it this instant.”

“Why?”

“Because Monsieur Borelli, the salt-tax farmer, who has got wind of your governorship and believes he is making a golden bargain by giving you his daughter today, will go back on it tomorrow, when he learns you are in disgrace.”

“But my dear marquis, a man does not marry from one day to the next.”

“He marries from one night to the next morning. Consent, and you will be married this evening.”

“Really?”

“I will see to it. I shall give old Borelli to understand that his dignity requires him to appear to give you his daughter out of complete disinterest, and before your appointment to the governorship of Normandy.”

“Bravo.”

“So I may make all the arrangements?”

The baron consulted the clock.

“Wait ten minutes,” he said. “If my commission has not arrived by two o’clock, you shall have my word.”

“Very good.”

“So the Regent’s death will not be known today?”

“No, there are measures to be taken. You will be married this evening, at midnight, and you can carry your wife off, if you like, to any castle you please.”

“Not at all. I shall stay in Paris.”

“The wedding can be at the father’s house, on the Île Saint-Louis, without ceremony —”

“Not at all. I want a splendid celebration. I want the thing done in broad daylight.”

“In the broad dark, at any rate.”

“So be it. You will take charge of the invitations. Those who do not come will tell me how to conduct myself towards them in future.”

“Rest easy about that. Marrying beneath oneself is fashionable enough for everybody to come. Besides, your wife is quite beautiful —”

“Is she indeed? Not that it greatly matters to me, considering the use I intend to make of her.”

“She has presence, and a beauty that will look out of place nowhere. We shall get her a stool at court once the squall has blown over.”

Two o’clock struck. The door opened.

“Ah, good God,” cried the baron, “here is my commission.”

The baron was mistaken. It was simply the Duke d’A—’s valet, come to inform him confidentially that the duchess had been arrested in her carriage an hour before, at the moment when she was returning to her house.

“Poor duchess,” said the baron philosophically.

“What are you saying, my dear?” asked Simiane.

“I am saying, marquis,” the baron answered phlegmatically, “that you may make all the arrangements. I shall marry Mademoiselle Borelli this evening.”

Chapter Three

The Hour Before Midnight

Mademoiselle Hélène Borelli, daughter of the salt-tax farmer of that name, was twenty-three years old, with a Greek head, great black eyes fringed with long lashes — velvet eyes, as the phrase goes — a fine figure, on the tall side, the hands of a statue, and a skin of dazzling whiteness, so utterly without lustre that when she sat still you would readily have taken her for a marble madonna.

At two in the afternoon the Baron de Nossac had not yet seen his wife; at four he was presented to her; at six he dined with her at his future father-in-law’s; and at eleven he was climbing into a carriage to drive to Saint-Germain-l’Auxerrois, where the little Abbé de Morfrans, his cousin, would celebrate the marriage mass.

“Well,” Simiane asked the baron as he was leading his betrothed to his carriage, “how do you find her?”

“Upon my word, my dear,” said the baron with some conceit, “she is quite beautiful, and I believe I shall love her for a good month straight off.”

ContextContexte

Ponson du Terrail, beyond Rocambole

Rocambole made Ponson du Terrail famous and then swallowed the rest of his work. He wrote dozens of other novels — the Second Empire demi-monde, forest melodramas, police memoirs, Regency intrigue, Orientalist serials — and almost none of them survived in English. Lost French Thrillers recovers them, in the order they can be edited, with the same care given to the Rocambole cycle.

Rocambole a rendu Ponson du Terrail célèbre, puis a englouti le reste de son œuvre. Il a écrit des dizaines d'autres romans — le demi-monde du Second Empire, des mélodrames forestiers, des mémoires de police, des intrigues de la Régence, des feuilletons orientalistes — et presque aucun n'a survécu en anglais. Lost French Thrillers les exhume, dans l'ordre où ils peuvent être établis, avec le soin donné au cycle de Rocambole.
