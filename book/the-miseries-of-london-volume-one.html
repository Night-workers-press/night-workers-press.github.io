PROLOGUE

The Baby-Farmer

Chapter One

The black plume of smoke from the Penny-Boat trailed away into the reddish fog hanging over the Thames, a fog that a pale shaft of the setting sun kept breaking through.

The Penny-Boat was a small steamer named for its fare: a penny — two sous, in French money.

Fifty such boats crisscrossed this vast river at every hour of the day, and it was into its murky waters that the colossal city of London sank its muddy feet.

As always, the Penny-Boat was packed with passengers — gentlemen and ladies aft, the roughs, meaning the common people, up at the bow.

There, at the front of the boat, men and women were watching a woman of twenty-four or twenty-five who held a child of about ten by the hand — some with curiosity, some with pity, a few with something closer to desire. Their clothes were poor, and their luggage poorer still.

The woman wore an old hat, an old checked shawl, coarse blue wool stockings, and shoes still coated in the dust of a long road.

The child's legs were bare below the knee, his head bare too, apart from a thick, tousled head of chestnut hair; his mother had wound around his rumpled little jacket a scrap of plaid that must once have been red and green but had faded to nothing but yellow and grey.

Why did these two unfortunates draw such attention on that crowded deck, amid all the bustle of the crossing, over the shriek of the trains that kept rattling back and forth across the Thames, from Cannon Street to London Bridge, and from London Bridge to Charing Cross?

Some smartly dressed gentlemen had even come forward from the stern to join the common folk clustered around the pair, and their astonishment and curiosity were every bit as great — perhaps even shading into open admiration — as that of everyone else who couldn't take their eyes off the mother and child.

For in her rags, the mother was lovelier than any of the ladies to be seen of a morning riding their thoroughbreds in Hyde Park or the gardens of Kensington, and never had a painter in love with the ideal dreamed up a cherub's face prettier than the child's.

The mother was pale-skinned, red-lipped, with deep blue eyes and hair black as ebony.

The child bore a strange mark: amid his chestnut hair, almost black, a thin, fine tuft of red hair ran down toward the middle of his forehead.

Both of them, mother and child, stared with uneasy wonder at the enormous city rising on both banks of the river, with its countless churches, its vast stations, its colossal bridges, and its blackened, smoke-stained houses.

Where had they come from? No one knew.

They had boarded at Greenwich, having walked there.

Sighing, the mother had dug into her purse — where two or three shillings jingled against a bit of copper change — for the four pence needed to buy their tickets.

Then she had sat down on the deck, taking her son onto her lap.

For a long while she spoke to no one.

But finally, as the Penny-Boat drew alongside the East India Docks, she asked whether what she saw ahead of her was London.

"Yes and no," answered a stout, red-headed man, a Scottish fish merchant on his way up to London Bridge. "Depends how you look at it, love. London's everywhere, and it never ends. Where are you headed?"

The young woman hesitated a moment.

"I'm going," she said at last, "to a part of town where there's a church called St Giles, and a street called Lawrence Street."

"Ah, I know it," said the Scotsman. "St Giles — that's a Catholic church."

"Yes."

"You're Irish, then?"

"Yes," the young woman said again.

The fish merchant was a decent, rather talkative sort; he had an eye for a pretty woman, and though he liked to think of himself as something of a gentleman, when he went into a public house he never drank standing at the bar with the well-dressed crowd — he'd go and smoke his pipe in the parlour, where he could sit and talk at his ease.

"You've a fair way to go yet, love," he said. "Get off at Charing Cross, find the Strand, and keep going straight on. Lawrence Street's an ugly little street and St Giles a poor sort of church, but there are some fine streets to get you there. Once you're past Piccadilly, you won't be far off. Are you going to family?"

"No, I don't know a soul in London. But I was told there's an Irishman in Lawrence Street named Patrick who'd take me and my child in."

"Every Irishman's called Patrick, love," the fish merchant said, "and if that's all you've got to go on, you're likely to end up sleeping under the stars."

The Irishwoman lifted her eyes to the sky, resigned.

"God is good," she said. "He won't abandon us."

The stout Scotsman went on: "You've come to London to work, then?"

"I don't know," she said.

Given the state of the young woman's clothes, it was an odd enough answer.

"In London," the Scotsman said, "it's only the lords who don't work."

"I have a duty to carry out," the Irishwoman said. "Tomorrow's the twenty-seventh of October, isn't it?"

"That it is."

"Tomorrow, at eight o'clock, I must be at St Giles's church, by the altar, and present my son to the priest saying Mass."

"Why's that, then?" the Scotsman asked, all innocence.

"His father made me promise, on his deathbed."

The Irishwoman gave this answer, no less mysterious than the last, without noticing that a small crowd had gathered around her, her son, and the fish merchant — among them a gentleman and a woman who were watching her with something like hunger. Just then the Penny-Boat drew up at London Bridge.

"Well, love," the Scotsman said, "my wife's a good woman, and if you'd like to come to us, we'll give you a proper cup of tea, some sandwiches, and a slice of smoked salmon, for you and your boy. Then you can sleep at ours, and tomorrow you'll have all the time in the world to get yourself to St Giles."

The Scotsman made the offer warmly, his ruddy face full of honest goodwill.

The Irishwoman hesitated, glancing at her poor child, worn out with tiredness.

"No, no," she said finally. "Thank you, a thousand times, but I must go where I've been told to go."

"Farewell, then," said the Scotsman, "and God keep you."

And he jumped down onto the landing stage.

The Penny-Boat got underway again. It passed the Cannon Street pier, then went under Blackfriars Bridge — "the Black Friars," as the English call it — touched briefly at Temple Pier, then pushed on again to the southwest.

Just then the fog tore open under a shaft of sunlight, and mother and child found themselves gazing at the magnificent spectacle spread before them.

To the right stood Somerset House; to the left, the black houses of Southwark; ahead of them, Waterloo Bridge, and further still, Westminster Bridge, and, half-blurred by the fog, the old Abbey and the Houses of Parliament with their foundations sunk in the water, and, quite lost in the mist on the far bank of the Thames, Lambeth Palace, the grand residence of the Archbishops of Canterbury.

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris.
