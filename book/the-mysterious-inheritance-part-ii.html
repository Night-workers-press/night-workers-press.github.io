From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter Twenty-nine

The Duel

Let's go back a step, and leave Mademoiselle de Balder to read and reread, in bewilderment, the strange letter she'd found on the guéridon of that unfamiliar room.

Armand, as we recall, had taken Bastien to the Rue Culture-Sainte-Catherine.

"My old friend," he said, "people in love are selfish, and forgetful because of it. If I'd left you on the Rue Meslay, we'd have spent the evening at Mademoiselle de Balder's, and the hours would have slipped by so quickly that, like last night, we'd have heard midnight strike before we knew it. But when a man has to be at the Bois de Boulogne at seven the next morning to defend his life, he needs a night's sleep."

"Bah, Monsieur Armand," Bastien replied, "I know all about that. In my day, in the Old Guard, we fought every morning, and it never stopped us from having a merry supper around midnight, every evening the wine held out."

"But that was thirty years ago."

"Maybe even thirty-five."

"You were a young man then."

"Well, I'm still solid, believe me."

Armand shook his head and said, somewhat wistfully:

"Were you any good with a sword?"

"Not especially, if I'm honest. In the Emperor's day, you understand, we fought every day on the battlefield, and there was no time to go to the fencing hall. But when you hold your sword with your heart..."

"Nonsense," Armand murmured, lost in thought.

And he added, almost to himself:

"Englishmen, generally speaking, don't fight much—they despise dueling, they hold it in contempt. But the ones who make an exception to that rule—and every exception becomes an eccentricity—must feel an almost fanatical devotion to it, precisely because their countrymen think nothing of it and loathe it. That must be the case with this Sir Williams, since he absolutely insists on meeting over such a trifle."

"Well," said Bastien, who'd caught Monsieur de Kergaz's aside, "since he absolutely insists, I'll try to teach him a lesson."

Armand led Bastien up to the second floor of the mansion, where he'd set up a large room as a fencing salon—he'd once been passionate about the sport—and took down foils and masks, telling the old soldier:

"Get your hand back in practice a little. It's always a good precaution."

The count and his old friend fenced for nearly an hour.

"Your form is good," Armand said at last, "your wrist is steady and light enough, but your legs lack spring. You'll have to kill your man on the first pass, or you're a dead man yourself."

"We'll try," Bastien replied calmly. He ate a hearty dinner, went to bed with the composure of an old soldier from whom death had always backed away, and slept straight through until morning.

Armand, who had spent the night on a couch, woke him at six and said:

"Come on. It's a good hour's ride to the Bois, and we still need to be first there. France cannot be late."

Bastien dressed briskly, but with the meticulous care of the old-time officers who used to have their hair powdered and call for their dress uniforms before storming a breach.

He put on a white piqué waistcoat over a fine cambric shirt fastened with a large diamond pin—a keepsake from Armand's unfortunate mother.

Over the waistcoat he buttoned a blue frock coat, his rosette gleaming in the buttonhole; then he pulled on polished boots and a pair of black cashmere trousers, slightly loose and cut in the hussar style, which completed his military bearing.

Armand was dressed entirely in black, and like Bastien, he wore his decoration.

King Louis-Philippe had seen fit to decorate Armand the sculptor, winner of the Prix de Rome, and the Comte de Kergaz was far from disowning the artist in himself.

A pair of dueling swords, brought back from Italy and tempered to perfection, were placed in the carriage's weapons case, and they set off. The Comte de Kergaz's carriage rolled up the Avenue des Champs-Élysées at a brisk trot without meeting another vehicle—so deserted is the most fashionable quarter of Paris at that early hour—but at the barrier they were joined by a light American-style carriage drawn by a single horse and driven by a young man.

"There's Sir Williams," said Bastien, pointing to the young man beside whom Ralph O... sat, while Arthur G... occupied the rear seat.

Armand studied with curiosity this man Bastien had mistaken for Andréa, and he too gave a start and said quickly:

"Are you absolutely certain it isn't him?"

"Oh, certainly, yes," said Bastien. "I'm convinced of it. But the resemblance is uncanny."

The baronet and his seconds greeted Armand and Bastien; then, being well-bred, they drew their tilbury up alongside the calèche, unwilling either to pass their opponents or to fall behind them. The two carriages went down the Avenue de Neuilly side by side and reached the Porte Maillot, where a horseman was waiting for them across the road.

This horseman was a cavalry squadron commander from a hussar regiment then garrisoned on the Quai d'Orsay, a man Monsieur de Kergaz knew well and had asked, the day before, by note, to serve as Bastien's second witness.

The squadron commander dismounted, Armand and Sir Williams climbed down from their carriages, and the six men set off on foot into the Bois, where, a hundred meters from the Pavillon d'Armenonville, they found a suitable thicket for the encounter. The ground was good—bare of grass and covered with fine sand.

While Sir Williams and Bastien, having bowed to each other once more, kept their distance, Ralph O... and the squadron commander settled the brief terms of the fight; and Monsieur de Kergaz, who kept his searching gaze fixed on Sir Williams, said to Arthur G..., his second witness:

"We find ourselves, monsieur, at a moment serious enough that we might speak freely and honestly, setting aside anything personal or hurtful."

"I agree with you, monsieur."

"Will you permit me a question?"

"Speak, monsieur, I'm listening."

"Have you known Sir Williams long?"

"Only two months."

"Are you quite certain he's truly a baronet, and of Irish origin?"

"I've seen his family papers, monsieur."

"How strange," Armand murmured. "I would swear he's my brother..."

"Monsieur," Arthur G... replied, "you must understand, though, that even if that were true, I have no right—having seen papers, titles, letters of recommendation in the name of Sir Williams, baronet and Irish gentleman—to claim he's one and the same as your brother, the Vicomte Andréa. Besides, it would be too late now."

"Just so, monsieur," Armand observed coolly. "It was purely for my own information that I asked."

The two young men bowed to each other, signaling that their conversation would end amicably, and they went to join Ralph O... and the squadron commander.

"The cause of this meeting is trivial," the latter was saying, "and besides, there's an enormous difference in age between the two opponents. That strikes me as more than enough reason not to give this affair too serious a character."

"That's my view as well, monsieur," Ralph O... replied.

"I think, then, that these gentlemen should fight to first blood."

"That's entirely sufficient."

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.
