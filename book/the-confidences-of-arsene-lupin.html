The bookLe livre

The book in which Lupin talks. Nine stories, most of them told to the friend who writes his adventures down, set in the years before The Hollow Needle and 813: a sunbeam on a wall that turns out to be a dying man spelling out a message, a wedding ring that will not come off a finger, eighteen diamonds waiting a hundred years in a sundial. Ganimard gets the two best hours of his life, and the last story, Arsène Lupin's Marriage, begins as clowning and is not funny at all. The nine stories Leblanc collected, in the order he collected them.

Le livre où Lupin parle. Neuf nouvelles, presque toutes racontées à l'ami qui écrit ses aventures, situées dans les années d'avant L'Aiguille creuse et 813 : un reflet de soleil sur un mur qui se révèle un mourant épelant un message, une alliance qui ne veut pas quitter un doigt, dix-huit diamants qui attendent cent ans dans un cadran solaire. Ganimard y vit les deux plus belles heures de sa vie, et la dernière histoire, Le Mariage d'Arsène Lupin, commence en farce et n'a rien de drôle. Les neuf nouvelles que Leblanc a réunies, dans l'ordre où il les a réunies.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

The Play of the Sun

“Lupin, do tell me something.”

“Eh! What do you want me to tell you? My whole life is public property!” answered Lupin, dozing on the couch in my study.

“Nobody knows it at all!” I cried. “People know from this or that letter of yours in the newspapers that you were mixed up in one affair, that you set another one going… But your own part in it, the real substance of the story, the way the drama ended, of all that they know nothing.”

“Bah! A heap of gossip with nothing in it.”

“Nothing in it! Your present of fifty thousand francs to Nicolas Dugrival’s wife! Nothing in it, the mysterious way you read the riddle of the Three Pictures!”

“A strange riddle, I admit,” said Lupin. “I’ll suggest a title for you: The Sign of the Shadow.”

“And your successes in society?” I went on. “The intrigues of the gallant Arsène! And the secret of your good deeds? All those stories you have so often hinted at to me, the ones you called The Wedding Ring, Death on the Prowl and the rest. So many confidences overdue, my poor Lupin! Come, a little courage…”

This was in the days when Lupin, famous already, had not yet fought his greatest battles; the days before the great adventures of the Hollow Needle and of 813. He was not yet thinking of appropriating the age-old treasure of the kings of France, or of burgling Europe under the Kaiser’s nose. He was content with more modest raids and more reasonable profits, spending himself in daily effort, doing evil from one day to the next, and doing good as well, out of temperament and out of sheer amateurishness, like a Don Quixote who enjoys himself and is easily moved.

As he said nothing, I tried again:

“Lupin, I beg of you!”

To my astonishment, he answered:

“Take a pencil, my dear fellow, and a sheet of paper.”

I obeyed at once, delighted at the thought that he was at last going to dictate to me a few of those pages into which he knows how to put such spirit and such fancy, and which I, alas, am obliged to spoil with my heavy explanations and my tedious elaborations.

“Ready?” he said.

“Ready.”

“Write down: 1 – 2 – 15 – 22 – 5 – 1 – 12.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Write, I tell you.”

He was sitting on the couch with his eyes turned towards the open window, and his fingers were rolling a cigarette of oriental tobacco.

He said:

“Write down: 12 – 25 – 15 – 21…”

There was a pause. Then he went on:

“13.”

And, after a silence:

“21 – 19…”

Had he gone mad? I looked at him, and little by little I saw that his eyes were no longer the indifferent eyes of a minute before, but attentive, and that they seemed to be following, somewhere out in space, a performance that held them.

Meanwhile he went on dictating, with intervals between the figures:

“20 – 1 – 22 – 15…”

Through the window one could see nothing but a piece of blue sky on the right and the front of the house opposite, an old private house whose shutters were closed as usual. There was nothing out of the way about it, no detail that struck me as new among those I had been looking at for years.

“9 – 4 – 5 – 4.”

And suddenly I understood. Or rather I thought I understood. For how could one believe that a man like Lupin, so reasonable underneath his mask of irony, would waste his time on such childishness? And yet there was no room for doubt. What he was counting was the intermittent flashes of a ray of sunlight playing on the blackened front of the old house, at the level of the second floor.

“1 – 14 – 7…” said Lupin.

The reflection vanished for a few seconds, then struck the wall again, and again, at regular intervals, and vanished once more.

Instinctively I had counted, and I said aloud:

“5…”

“You’ve got it? Not before time,” Lupin sniggered.

He went to the window and leaned out, as though to make sure of the exact line the beam of light was following. Then he went back and lay down again on the sofa, saying:

“Your turn now. Count.”

I obeyed, so completely did the fellow look as though he knew where he was going. Besides, I could not help admitting that there was something distinctly curious in the regularity of those flashes on the wall, in those appearances and disappearances that followed one another like the signals of a lighthouse.

They clearly came from a house on the same side of the street as ours, since the sun was falling obliquely through my windows. One might have thought that somebody was opening and shutting a casement by turns, or rather amusing himself by throwing back rays of light with a little pocket mirror.

“It’s a child playing,” I cried after a while, rather irritated by the stupid task imposed on me.

“Carry on.”

And I counted. And I set down figures. And the sun went on dancing in front of me, with a really mathematical precision.

“Well?” said Lupin, after a longer silence.

“Frankly, it seems to be over. There has been nothing for several minutes.”

We waited, and as no more light played in the air, I made a joke of it:

“It strikes me we have wasted our time. A few figures on a piece of paper: a thin haul.”

Without stirring from his couch, Lupin said:

“Be so good, my dear fellow, as to replace each of those figures by the corresponding letter of the alphabet, counting A as 1, B as 2, and so on.”

“But that’s idiotic.”

“Utterly idiotic. But one does so many idiotic things in life. One more…”

I resigned myself to the stupid job, and wrote down the first letters: A-B-O-V-E.

“A word!” I cried. “There’s a word forming.”

“Go on, then, my dear fellow.”

And I went on, and the letters that followed made other words, which I separated from one another as I went. And, to my great astonishment, a whole sentence lined itself up under my eyes.

“Got it?” said Lupin after a moment.

“Got it! Though there are spelling mistakes in it.”

“Never mind about those, I beg. Read it slowly.”

So I read out the unfinished sentence, which I give here exactly as it appeared to me:

“Above all you must avoide danger, shun atacks, meet the ennemy forces only with the greatest prudance, and…”

I began to laugh.

“And there we are! Light has dawned! Why, we are dazzled with it! But really, Lupin, confess that this string of advice, told off like beads by some cook, doesn’t get you very far.”

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
