From the opening pagesLes premières pages

CHAPTER

II

❦

W

e ask the reader to follow us into the King's small cabinet. This small cabinet adjoined the little bedchamber where we have already glimpsed him. In the royal apartments, these two rooms formed a kind of intimate retreat to which he admitted only his oldest and most tried friends.

Henri IV was there alone with Sully, and this was the morning after the day on which the minister had received the visit of Pardaillan and, thereafter, from the hands of Saêtta, the paper written in Italian containing the directions to the treasure.

Sully had first attempted to have the King accept the idea suggested by Pardaillan — which was, as may be recalled, to appear to yield to the Queen's desire and to fix a firm date for the coronation ceremony.

But the King was not a man to be satisfied with vague explanations. Sully, driven into a corner, was obliged to resign himself to placing him fully in possession of the veiled warning given by Pardaillan.

At the first words, Henri had gone pale and sunk back into his armchair. The fear of assassination, as we have said, was the canker gnawing at him.

When the minister had finished his explanations, the King struck both thighs in anger, and rising, exclaimed:

— God's truth, my friend, they will kill me — it is certain! I shall not come out of this city alive!

— They will not kill you, Sire, if you follow the counsel you have been given.

— And then? When I have gained until next spring, shall I be any better off?

— Well, Sire, I will say as M. de Pardaillan says: you will have gained nearly a year. That is a great deal, it seems to me. Between now and then, and with money, we shall be ready to set your great design in motion. In the spring, Sire, you take the field, and you escape the daggers of your would-be assassins. And since the issue of the campaign is not in doubt, you return victorious from Germany — so great, so haloed with the prestige of glory, that none will dare attempt anything against you again.

Henri IV, according to his custom, had begun to pace the cabinet in long strides. And while listening to his minister, he reflected. He perceived that in the proposal set before him he had nothing to lose. He was a man of prompt decisions:

— Very well, then, said he. Besides, I see no other way out of it. But to advance my projects by some months, as you say, money is needed. Will you find it?

— I shall find what is needed, Sully assured him, and more besides. Will Your Majesty cast an eye over this paper?

As he spoke, Sully handed Henri the paper that Saêtta had given him. Henri IV was better read than most of his gentlemen. He spoke Spanish and Italian with fluency. He was therefore able to read the paper handed to him without needing to resort to a translator, as Sully had been obliged to do.

— What is this treasury? said he, returning the paper after reading it. And in what way does it concern us?

— This treasury amounts to ten millions, Sire.

— Zounds! The sum is a respectable one.

Sully recounted briefly what he knew of the history of Fausta's treasure, and concluded:

— For want of better, these ten millions will be of real assistance to us in hastening our military preparations.

— But, the King observed, these millions do not belong to us.

— Pardon, Sire, said Sully coolly, for more than twenty years these millions have lain buried on your territory, without the owner giving any sign of life. Neither you nor your predecessors have, to my knowledge, made any engagement in the matter. What lies upon the King's lands belongs to the King. We have jurists to demonstrate it.

Henri IV, like Sully — though not in the same fashion — was interested.

That figure of ten millions, which, let us not forget, had a value far more considerable than in our own day, had not failed to impress him. He pressed the matter no further.

Sully therefore obtained licence to act as he saw fit to bring Jehan le Brave's millions into the royal coffers. And since, once he had taken a decision, the Béarnais liked to go straight to the mark, he resolved to settle the coronation affair on the spot, and sent for the Queen.

— Madame, said he roundly, when the Queen, somewhat ill at ease, had sat down, you asked to speak with me. I imagine it is to speak to me again of the ceremony of your coronation.

That was true. Marie de Médicis, acting on Concini's suggestions, had asked for the very audience the King was now referring to. She supposed he was going to refuse, as always. She supposed it all the more because Sully was present at the conversation. Instantly she became aggressive:

— Indeed, Sire, I wish to speak with you on that subject. But I see it will be as it has been on every previous occasion. The Queen can obtain nothing from the King. She is less well served than —

Henri saw the conjugal scene coming, with his mistresses about to be flung at his head. He interrupted at the right moment:

— Well, madame, you are mistaken. It pleases me to grant today what I have refused until now.

— What! stammered Marie de Médicis, quite taken aback. You consent?

— I have just reached, with my cousin Sully, some very grave decisions. It is possible — it is not certain, mind you — it is possible that I shall take the field next spring. During the King's absence, you will be Regent of the kingdom, madame. And I have reflected that it is necessary to secure your authority as far as lies in my power. Despite the great expense which this ceremony will entail, it has a utility that overrides all else. It is for that reason I consent — and I do more than consent: as of this moment I fix the date as the twentieth of September.

Marie de Médicis did not know what the Concinis' true intention was in urging her to press for her coronation. In her case, it was the woman more than the sovereign who desired this sumptuous ceremony in which she was to play the principal part. It was the woman who, momentarily setting etiquette aside, sprang upon the King, seized both his hands, and cried, sincerely moved, all radiance:

— You are good, Henri! You make me very happy!

— Yes, my dear, replied the King with a shade of melancholy, I am good. Perhaps you will acknowledge it fully — when I am no longer here.

Already the dry, profoundly selfish nature of Marie de Médicis was reasserting itself.

— Since the King seems so well disposed towards me, said she, I shall profit by it to make him another request.

— What is it? said Henri, on his guard.

— Sire, I have need of money.

ContextContexte

Michel Zévaco (1860–1918)

Zévaco was a schoolmaster, then a cavalry officer, then an anarchist journalist who fought duels over his editorials and went to prison for his convictions. He came to the novel in his forties and conquered it at once: from 1900 his serials ran in the great Paris dailies and were read by millions. Sartre, remembering his childhood in Les Mots, called him a writer of genius. He has been read in Persian, Turkish, Arabic, Romanian and Spanish. In English, for more than a century, he was effectively unavailable. The Zévaco Project is the first attempt ever made to translate his complete novels — one volume at a time, until the canon is closed.

Zévaco fut maître d'école, puis officier de cavalerie, puis journaliste anarchiste — un homme qui se battait en duel pour ses éditoriaux et fit de la prison pour ses convictions. Il vint au roman passé la quarantaine et le conquit aussitôt : à partir de 1900, ses feuilletons paraissaient dans les grands quotidiens parisiens et se lisaient par millions. Sartre, se souvenant de son enfance dans Les Mots, le tenait pour un écrivain de génie. On l'a lu en persan, en turc, en arabe, en roumain, en espagnol. En anglais, pendant plus d'un siècle, il fut pratiquement introuvable. Le Projet Zévaco est la première tentative jamais faite de traduire son œuvre romanesque complète — un volume après l'autre, jusqu'à la clôture du corpus.
