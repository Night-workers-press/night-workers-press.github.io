The bookLe livre

Spring 1863. Before leaving his village outside Edo for Kyoto, Hijikata Toshizō, the youngest son of a well-off farming family, copies his poems into a notebook, dates it, titles it after his name as a poet, Hōgyoku, and leaves it at home. A few years later he is vice-commander of the Shinsengumi; he is killed at Hakodate in June 1869, a week before the last fortress of the shogunate surrenders. The notebook holds forty-one hokku, one of them struck out by his own hand: plum blossom, snow, the spring moon. The Japanese is printed vertically, with a romanization, a plain English translation and a short note for every poem, and a foreword on the winter of 1868–69 in Ezo, when Hijikata served alongside the French officers.

Printemps 1863. Avant de quitter son village des environs d'Edo pour Kyoto, Hijikata Toshizō, cadet d'une famille de paysans aisés, recopie ses poèmes dans un cahier, le date, l'intitule d'après son nom de poète, Hōgyoku, et le laisse à la maison. Quelques années plus tard, il est vice-commandant du Shinsengumi ; il est tué à Hakodate en juin 1869, une semaine avant la reddition de la dernière forteresse du shogunat. Le cahier contient quarante et un hokku, dont un qu'il a barré lui-même : des pruniers, de la neige, la lune de printemps. Le japonais est imprimé verticalement, avec une transcription, une traduction anglaise sans fard et une courte note pour chaque poème, et un avant-propos sur l'hiver 1868-1869 à Ezo, quand Hijikata servait aux côtés des officiers français.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Foreword — The Notebook and the Column

There were ten of them. On the roll of the three thousand men of the Ezo government, a Hakodate scribe wrote their names down by ear, in katakana: ブリユ子ー for Brunet, ブッヘー for Bouffier, ホルタン for Fortant, コルラーシ for Collache. It took the Japanese historian Sawa Mamoru years to work out what they had really been called. These ten Frenchmen, instructors from the military mission of 1867 or sailors who had left their ship, spent the winter of 1868-1869 in Ezo, today’s Hokkaidō, with the last loyalists of the Shōgun.

In that camp was a man everyone knew by reputation and nobody really knew: Hijikata Toshizō, former vice-commander of the Shinsengumi. He left no memoirs and no speeches. He left a photograph, a few letters, and a notebook of forty-one poems. This book publishes the notebook. Before opening it, here is where his story crosses the story of the ten.

Tama, 1863

Early in 1863, in the village of Ishida, a day’s walk west of Edo, the youngest of the ten children of a well-off farming family is getting ready to leave for Kyoto. He is twenty-seven. With his friend Kondō Isami and the pupils of a small Edo fencing school, he has just joined the Rōshigumi, a band of masterless samurai raised by the shogunate to escort the Shōgun to the imperial capital. The band leaves Edo in the spring.

Before he goes, he copies out his hokku in a notebook. He dates it, ‘Spring, Year of the Boar, third year of Bunkyū’, titles it Hōgyoku Hokku-shū, The Hokku of Hōgyoku, and signs it with his personal name, Yoshitoyo. Hōgyoku, ‘abundant jewel’, is his name as a poet: the first of its two characters is the one in Yoshitoyo. He leaves the notebook at home and sets out.

From Kyoto to Ezo

What comes next, every Japanese reader knows. In Kyoto the Shinsengumi is born, the corps that hunts down the shogunate’s enemies in the capital. Hijikata is its vice-commander, the man who enforces its code to the point of ordered suicide, and they call him the Demon. Then the shogunate falls. In the spring of 1868 Kondō is captured and beheaded. Hijikata fights his way north with those still fighting, from Utsunomiya to Aizu, from Aizu to Sendai, where Enomoto Takeaki’s fleet gathers up the last of them and carries them to Ezo.

This is where the French come in. Captain Jules Brunet and the riding master André Cazeneuve have joined the fleet; three NCOs of the mission, the artillery sergeant Arthur Fortant and the infantry sergeants Jean Marlin and François Bouffier, reach Sendai on foot with their pupils. According to the diary of Ōtori Keisuke, when the French are shared out among the columns, Bouffier goes to Hijikata’s; Brunet, Marlin and Fortant stay at Hakodate. The sergeant and the vice-commander have not a word of language in common. We know they marched together through the snow. We know nothing more.

In January 1869 two naval cadets arrive as well, Eugène Collache and Henri de Nicol, who have left their resignations on their bunks. On 27 January the officers of Ezo vote to choose their leaders. Out of 856 votes Hijikata receives 73 and comes sixth; he becomes deputy minister of the army.

F…

In the spring, word comes that the imperial fleet has gathered at Miyako, on the northern coast of Honshū, with the ironclad the United States has just delivered to the new government, the Kōtetsu. At the end of April 1869 a council meets at Hakodate. Collache was there, and he left an account of it in the narrative he published in 1874:

‘The question was seriously debated and discussed in all its aspects between H. de N..., M..., F..., the Japanese admiral Araï Ikinoské, the commander of our corvette the Kaïten, and myself.’

H. de N. is Henri de Nicol, M. is Marlin, and F., in all likelihood, Fortant: he is the only one of the ten whose name begins with that letter. The council resolves to surprise the enemy fleet at anchor and carry its ships by boarding. Boarding needs swords. Soldiers of the land army are taken aboard, ‘who with their terrible sabres would make excellent boarders’, Collache writes. Hijikata goes aboard the Kaiten, with Nicol and Admiral Arai Ikunosuke.

On 6 May 1869, in Miyako Bay, the attack fails in less than an hour. Collache’s Takao, too slow, arrives too late and ends up aground; Collache surrenders after a few days on the run in the mountains. The Kaiten returns to Hakodate with its dead. An artillery sergeant, an infantry sergeant and two cadets of twenty-one and twenty-two had sat on the council that sent Hijikata into his only sea battle.

June 1869

On 20 May the imperial army lands on the island. Hijikata holds the pass at Futamata until the other positions give way and he is called back. In those weeks, tradition says, he entrusts his young page, Ichimura Tetsunosuke, with his photograph, a lock of his hair and a poem, with orders to take them to his family in Tama. The photograph arrived. It is the one the whole world knows.

At the beginning of June, Brunet orders the French to leave Hakodate. On 10 June nine of them climb into the boats of the French aviso Coëtlogon, Bouffier and Fortant among them; Collache is already a prisoner. Here ends what French eyes saw of Hijikata.

He has ten days left. On 20 June, at dawn, the general assault begins. The fort at Benten is cut off from the rest of the town; Hijikata rides out with a handful of men to relieve it. At the Ippongi barrier he is shot in the stomach and falls from his horse. The witness whose account we have, Ōno Unaka, writes himself that he arrived too late: when he was called, Hijikata was already dead. The exact spot is disputed. The body was carried off and buried in secret; nobody knows where. Goryōkaku surrenders on 27 June.

Two Graves We Do Not Have

The French sail for Saigon and then, most of them, for France. Not all. In the spring of 1870 Fortant, Marlin and Bouffier land again at Yokohama: Tajima Masachika, who wrote the French of the Ezo government and now serves the new regime, has them brought back for the military school at Osaka. Marlin dies there in 1872. In 1875, of sixteen French instructors, the only two whose contracts are not renewed are Bouffier and Fortant, the two veterans of Hakodate.

They stay on. In 1878, in Tsukiji, Fortant opens a trading house, Fortant & Co., in which Bouffier has put his money without signing the deed. A year later it goes bankrupt; the billiard table is sold at auction. Fortant takes it all on himself, and nothing falls on Bouffier. Sawa Mamoru, who pieced these lives together, uses for him the Japanese phrase 武士の商法, ‘samurai trading’, the trade of warriors who ruin themselves in a shop.

ContextContexte

Hijikata Toshizō (1835–1869)

Hijikata Toshizō was vice-commander of the Shinsengumi, the man they called the Demon, and he followed the shogunate's cause to its end in Ezo, where he served alongside the French officers who had joined its last army, and was killed at Hakodate in June 1869. His forty-one hokku are not great poems, and Japan has never been shy of saying so; they are the one place where we hear the man before the legend. The book is a companion to the third volume of The Ten of Hakodate, the Night Workers graphic novel, and it stands on its own.

Hijikata Toshizō fut le vice-commandant du Shinsengumi, celui qu'on surnommait le Démon, et il suivit la cause du shogunat jusqu'à son terme à Ezo, où il servit aux côtés des officiers français qui avaient rejoint sa dernière armée, avant d'être tué à Hakodate en juin 1869. Ses quarante et un hokku ne sont pas de grands poèmes, et le Japon ne s'est jamais privé de le dire ; c'est le seul endroit où l'on entend l'homme avant la légende. Le livre accompagne le troisième tome des Dix de Hakodate, la bande dessinée de Night Workers, et se lit aussi seul.
