Officially Dead

A novel after Balzac

by Edgard Bronce-Ceraypar Edgard Bronce-Ceray

The bookLe livre

Balzac's Colonel Chabert came back from Eylau to find his wife remarried, his fortune spent, and every institution in France with an interest in his staying dead. Two centuries later the question has only changed address. Bobby Carducci, paralegal, Downtown Brooklyn, stale bagel, second-floor office with a weeping ceiling — and a man in the waiting room who is legally deceased. A novel about what happens when the living world has no room left for you.

Le colonel Chabert de Balzac revenait d'Eylau pour découvrir sa femme remariée, sa fortune dissipée, et toutes les institutions de France intéressées à ce qu'il reste mort. Deux siècles plus tard, la question n'a fait que changer d'adresse. Bobby Carducci, assistant juridique, Downtown Brooklyn, un bagel rassis, un bureau au premier étage dont le plafond suinte — et dans la salle d'attente un homme légalement décédé. Un roman sur ce qui arrive quand le monde des vivants n'a plus de place pour vous.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

C H A P T E R O N E

———

The bagel was stale. Bobby Carducci knew this the way he knew most things worth knowing — too late to do anything about it. He'd bought it from the cart on Court Street at seven-forty, like he did every morning, and now it sat on his desk in its wax paper like a small beige crime, hard enough to chip a tooth. He chewed anyway. You didn't throw out a three-dollar bagel in Brooklyn. Not in this economy, and not on a paralegal's salary.

The office of Sarah Darvell, Attorney at Law, occupied the second floor of a building on Schermerhorn Street that had once been something better — a bank, possibly, or a dentist's office, back when Downtown Brooklyn still had dentists who weren't also selling Invisalign to Instagram models. The ceiling was high and stained in places where the pipes above had wept through the winters. The carpet was the color of a headache. There were five desks pushed together in an open arrangement that some designer had probably called "collaborative" and that everyone else called "I can hear you eating." Filing cabinets lined the walls, dense with the compressed misery of landlord-tenant disputes, personal injury claims, and the occasional immigration case that Sarah took on because she couldn't help herself.

The fluorescent tubes overhead had a flicker. Not a dramatic flicker — nothing that would make you think the building was haunted or that the wiring was about to kill you. Just a faint, neurological pulse at the edge of perception, the kind of thing you stopped noticing after a week and that slowly, over months, gave you the feeling that something was fundamentally wrong with the world without your being able to say what.

Bobby Carducci had worked here for eleven years. He'd stopped noticing the flicker nine years ago. The wrongness, he attributed to other things.

"He's back," said Simon.

Simon Pak was the intern. Twenty-two years old, Columbia pre-law, parents in Fort Lee, a kid who had somehow gotten it into his head that working for a solo practitioner in a second-floor office in Downtown Brooklyn would look scrappy and authentic on his law school application. He wore the kind of glasses that cost four hundred dollars and were designed to look like they cost twelve, and he had a habit of narrating the office's daily life as though he were livestreaming it, which, on some days, he was.

"Parka Guy," Simon said, not looking up from his phone. "Eleven o'clock. Right on schedule."

Through the window that faced the street — smudged, rain-tracked, with a dead fly on the sill that nobody had moved since September — Bobby could see the man crossing from the opposite sidewalk. He walked with a deliberateness that had nothing to do with confidence. It was the walk of someone who had learned to move through space without attracting attention and who had not yet unlearned it, even though nobody here was watching. Or nobody who mattered.

"That's five," said Priya, one of the two associates. She was at her desk, drafting a motion to dismiss that she already knew would be denied, eating almonds from a bag that crinkled with each handful. "Five times in two weeks."

"He's got persistence, I'll give him that," said Danny, the other associate. Danny Reiss, two years out of Brooklyn Law, the kind of guy who wore a tie even though Sarah never asked him to, because he believed that if you dressed like you worked at a real firm, the universe would eventually provide one. "Most of our actual clients don't come back five times."

"Most of our actual clients can pay," said Bobby.

The door at the bottom of the stairs opened. They could hear it because the buzzer had been broken since March and the lock since before that, so the door just opened when you pushed it, which Sarah said she'd fix and which Bobby knew she wouldn't, because fixing the door would cost money they didn't have and because, in some unspoken way, a door that opened for anyone was consistent with the kind of practice Sarah wanted to run, even if it also opened for people she'd rather it didn't.

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow, measured. The particular gait of a man who was not in a hurry because he had nowhere else to go.

Simon put down his phone and swiveled his chair to face the door with the theatrical readiness of someone expecting entertainment. Bobby kept chewing. Priya stopped crinkling.

The man came in.

He was maybe fifty, maybe forty-five — the kind of face that made age a guess because something had happened to it that wasn't aging. Thin, but not the thinness of someone who didn't eat enough. The thinness of someone whose body had consumed itself from the inside over a long period of time and had arrived at a hard remainder, like a piece of driftwood after the ocean had taken everything soft. His skin had the grayish undertone of someone who hadn't slept well in years. His hands were large, rough, cracked at the knuckles, and he held them at his sides with a stillness that seemed practiced, as though he'd trained himself not to fidget.

He wore the parka. It was olive drab, military-issue, the kind they sold at surplus stores or that you kept from your service because nobody asked for it back and because throwing it away would have meant something you weren't ready to deal with. It had been washed too many times. The zipper pull was missing, replaced with a bent paperclip. Beneath it, a flannel shirt, buttoned wrong — one hole off, so the collar sat crooked. Jeans. Boots that had once been good boots, the leather cracked now, one sole separating at the toe like a mouth trying to speak.

He stood just inside the door, scanning the room the way people scan rooms when they've spent time in places where the layout of a room could save your life or end it. His eyes moved left to right, clocked the exits — front door, back hallway — registered each person, and then settled, with a kind of weary hope, on Bobby.

"I'm here to see Mrs. Darvell," he said.

His voice was quiet. Not shy. Quiet the way a machine is quiet when it's been running at full capacity for so long that something inside has burned out and now it just hums.

"Ms.," Simon corrected, without looking up. "She's not married. It's Ms. Darvell."

The man absorbed this information with the same unhurried stillness. He didn't seem offended or embarrassed. He just filed it.

"Ms. Darvell. I need to see her."

Bobby put down the bagel. He'd had this conversation before — four times, to be exact — and each time it had ended the same way: the man leaving, Bobby feeling vaguely shitty about it, and the office returning to its low-grade hum of work and complaint.

"She's in court this morning," Bobby said. "She's got a hearing in the Eastern District, then a meeting with a client in Red Hook. She won't be back until—" He checked the time. "I don't know. Four? Five? Could be later."
