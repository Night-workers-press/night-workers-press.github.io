Midnight strikes along the boulevard des Italiens; the sky is clear, the moon is full, and the street is as crowded as noon with carriages, dominos and maskers. Chapter One

Midnight had just struck on every clock along the boulevard des Italiens.

It was January 1853, a Saturday, the night of the Opera ball. The air was cold and dry, the sky clear, and the moon shone at its fullest brilliance.

The boulevard was as crowded as if it were broad daylight. Carriages crossed paths at a brisk trot, pedestrians packed the sidewalks, and dominos and maskers of every description drifted happily through the crowd.

It was the hour when the Opera, crowned in a wreath of light, threw open its doors—the hour when Musard's hundred-voice orchestra struck up its first note.

Seated outside the Café Riche, at the corner of the rue Le Peletier, two young men sat talking, bundled warmly in their sable-lined vitchouras, just a few steps from their pony-chaise. Its magnificent Irish trotter was being held in check, with some difficulty, by a groom barely three and a half feet tall, dressed in a sky-blue overcoat with a wide fox-fur collar and small pleated boots with white turned-down tops.

"My dear Gontran," said one of the young men, "you have the strangest notion of dragging me to the Opera ball—a genuinely disreputable place nobody's set foot in for at least fifteen years, where you meet nothing but women who either aren't part of society anymore or never were."

"My dear Arthur," the other replied, "have you read many novels?"

"A fair few."

"Every novel begins at the Opera ball—the ones people write and invent, first of all, and then the ones that unfold in real life."

"That's a peculiar theory."

"It happens to be true."

"Are you planning to open the first chapter of some story of that kind tonight?"

"Perhaps."

"You have an appointment?"

"Yes."

"With whom?"

"I don't know. Read this instead."

The young man his friend called Gontran drew from his pocket a small jonquil-colored morocco wallet, and from it a rather thick, unsigned letter, which he handed to his friend Viscount Arthur de Chenevières.

Arthur unfolded it slowly, called for a candle, and before reading it made this observation:

"Handwriting has a character all its own, no less than people do. A firm, full, rounded hand betrays a cold, calm, resolute man. An elongated, faintly trembling script generally gives away the hand of a woman in the grip of some emotion. A woman writes quite differently to her dressmaker or her man of business than she does when arranging a first rendezvous with the man she loves..."

"That's true, my friend."

"Now," Arthur continued, "the hand that wrote this letter clearly belongs to a woman."

"Obviously."

"But it wasn't trembling."

"No, it wasn't."

"So you're not the one who's loved."

Baron Gontran de Neubourg smiled.

"Read it," he said, "and you'll see there's no question of love between my anonymous correspondent and me."

Arthur read half aloud:

"One evening in December of last year—about six weeks ago now—Baron Gontran de Neubourg, walking along the boulevard opposite the Café Anglais, ran into three friends smoking their cigars in the moonlight as they left their club, where they had been gambling heavily.

"Those three friends were the Viscount Arthur de Chenevières, Lord Blakstone, and the Marquis Albert de Verne."

"Well," Arthur broke off, "that's odd enough—this opening sounds exactly like the first chapter of a serial novel."

"Keep reading," said the baron.

Monsieur de Chenevières went on:

"Baron Gontran de Neubourg was walking alone, lost in thought, and had his friends not stopped him, there is no doubt he would have passed them without even noticing.

"'Where are you off to, Baron?' asked the viscount.

"'Nowhere in particular.'

"'Come now.'

"'I'm just walking.'

"'Without any purpose?'

"'I'm daydreaming... that's purpose enough. Good evening, gentlemen. Where have you come from?'

"'The club.'

"'Where are you headed?'

"'Just walking, same as you. Except instead of daydreaming, we're talking.'

"'About what?'

"'Lord Blakstone claims he has the spleen.'

"'Lord Blakstone is quite right—he's English, and the sky is clear tonight. An Englishman without fog is a body without a soul.'

"'De Verne here,' the viscount went on, 'is bored. He just prefers a French word for it.'

"'And you?' the baron asked.

"'I'm the same as de Verne.'

"'Gentlemen,' the baron said then, 'the oldest among us is thirty—that's me. The youngest is twenty-four—that's Arthur. The poorest has an income of a hundred thousand livres a year—that's me again. The richest has a hundred and fifty thousand pounds sterling a year—that's Lord Blakstone.'

"'Correct,' the Englishman said with his usual composure.

"'And yet,' the baron continued, 'we all lead the same life, and you could set out the shape of any one of our days like this:

"'We rise at eleven, breakfast at noon. At two we're seen in the Bois—you and I on horseback, Lord Blakstone in his pony-chaise, de Verne in his phaeton. At five we play whist. Between nine and eleven at night we're at the Opera, and from eleven to midnight we're making the rounds of two or three drawing rooms in the Faubourg Saint-Germain or the rue d'Anjou-Saint-Honoré, and then we finish the night at the club, ready to start it all over again tomorrow.'

"'And the day after that,' said the Marquis de Verne, who had said nothing until then.

"'Now,' Gontran went on, 'de Verne is the son of that brilliant cavalry general who made his name in the retreat from Russia. You, Viscount, count ancestors among the Crusaders. And Lord Blakstone descends from a Scottish clan chief who held Robert the Bruce and his entire army at bay in his old manor in the Cheviot hills, with nothing but a garrison of shepherds and farmhands.'

"'And you,' the viscount added, 'you, my dear Neubourg, come from Palatine stock, and your great-grandfather settled in France after the famous Thirty Years' War. One of your ancestors rode alone into the city of Mainz, helmet on his head and sword in hand, and nailed his gauntlet to the gate of Prince Frederick of Prussia.'

"'True enough,' the baron said simply."

The Viscount de Chenevières broke off his reading a second time and said to Baron Gontran de Neubourg:

"Your anonymous correspondent is a woman you know, my friend, and you must have given her all these details yourself—which are, by the way, entirely accurate."

"I haven't breathed a word of that conversation to anyone," Monsieur de Neubourg replied, "and I swear to you the handwriting on that letter is completely unknown to me."

"Go on, then."

The viscount continued:

"The four young men looked at each other in silence for a few moments.

"'Gentlemen,' Baron Gontran de Neubourg said at last, 'do you know that I feel thoroughly ill at ease in these cramped modern clothes, which bear so little resemblance to the armor of our ancestors—that I'm suffocating in this age of money and self-interest we live in—and that I sincerely miss the Round Table and its twelve knights?'

"'So do I,' said the Marquis de Verne.

"'I feel exactly the same,' added the Viscount de Chenevières.

"'And I,' said Lord Blakstone, 'sometimes believe I truly am my own ancestor, and that it was I who defended Galwy Manor against Robert the Bruce.'

ContextContexte

Pierre-Alexis Ponson du Terrail (1829–1871)

Rocambole is the reason French has the word rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail wrote him across nineteen volumes between 1857 and 1870 — a child of the Paris underworld who becomes a criminal prince, is broken, sent to the galleys, and returns as an avenger. He was the most widely read character of his century and the direct ancestor of every serial anti-hero since, Arsène Lupin included. English readers have never had him whole: a few volumes were translated in the 1860s and then abandoned. This is the first complete English Rocambole.

Rocambole est la raison pour laquelle le français possède le mot rocambolesque. Ponson du Terrail l'a écrit sur dix-neuf volumes entre 1857 et 1870 — enfant des bas-fonds parisiens devenu prince du crime, brisé, envoyé au bagne, puis revenu en justicier. Il fut le personnage le plus lu de son siècle et l'ancêtre direct de tous les anti-héros de feuilleton, Arsène Lupin compris. Le lectorat anglophone ne l'a jamais eu en entier : quelques volumes furent traduits dans les années 1860, puis abandonnés. Voici le premier Rocambole intégral en anglais.
