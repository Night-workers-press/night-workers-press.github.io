The bookLe livre

Paris, April 1915. Seven maimed soldiers wait in the dark under the trees of a small square to protect the nurse they call Little Mother Coralie. Within the hour a car has tried to carry her off, a man has been strangled with a thin red silk cord, and Captain Patrice Belval, who has lost a leg and made a philosophy of it, is on the trail of three hundred million francs in gold, hidden somewhere between a garden in Passy and the Seine. Halfway through, a stranger walks in and takes the book over. The only Lupin novel set inside the First World War, and written while it was being fought.

Paris, avril 1915. Sept soldats mutilés attendent dans l'ombre, sous les arbres d'une petite place, pour protéger l'infirmière qu'ils appellent maman Coralie. Moins d'une heure plus tard, une voiture a tenté de l'enlever, un homme a été étranglé avec un mince cordon de soie rouge, et le capitaine Patrice Belval, qui a perdu une jambe et en a fait une philosophie, est sur la piste de trois cents millions en or, cachés quelque part entre un jardin de Passy et la Seine. À mi-parcours, un inconnu entre en scène et prend le livre en main. Le seul roman de Lupin situé dans la Première Guerre mondiale, et écrit pendant qu'elle se livrait.

From the opening pagesLes premières pages

Chapter I — Little Mother Coralie

A little before the half hour after six struck, as the evening shadows were thickening, two soldiers reached the small crossroads planted with trees that is formed, opposite the Galliera Museum, by the meeting of the Rue de Chaillot and the Rue Pierre-Charron.

One of them wore the horizon-blue greatcoat of the infantry; the other, a Senegalese, the beige wool kit, wide breeches and close-fitting jacket, that has been issued since the war began to the zouaves and the African troops. One of them had only one leg left, the left; the other only one arm, the right.

They walked round the open space, at the centre of which stands a pretty group of Silenus figures, and stopped. The infantryman threw away his cigarette. The Senegalese picked it up, pulled a few quick mouthfuls out of it, pinched it out between thumb and forefinger and put it in his pocket.

All this without a word.

Almost at the same moment, out of the Rue Galliera, came two more soldiers, and it would have been impossible to say what branch of the service they belonged to, their military dress being made up of the most mismatched civilian oddments. One of them, however, sported the red cap of the zouaves, the other a gunner’s cap. The first walked on crutches, the second with sticks.

Those two took up their position beside the news kiosk that stands at the edge of the pavement.

By the Rue Pierre-Charron, the Rue de Brignoles and the Rue de Chaillot, three more came, one at a time: a light infantryman with one arm, a sapper who limped, a marine with one hip that seemed to be twisted out of place. Each went straight to a tree, and each leaned against it.

Between them not a word was exchanged. None of these seven maimed men seemed to know his companions, or to take any notice of them, or even to be aware that they were there.

Standing behind their trees, or behind the kiosk, or behind the group of Silenus figures, they did not move. And the few passers-by who crossed, on that evening of the 3rd of April 1915, this quiet crossroads, barely lit by its hooded street lamps, did not linger long enough to notice their motionless outlines.

The half hour after six struck.

At that moment the door of one of the houses that look out on the little square opened. A man came out of that house, closed the door behind him, crossed the Rue de Chaillot and walked round the open space.

He was an officer, dressed in khaki. Under his red forage cap, trimmed with three gold braids, a wide bandage of linen was wound about his head, hiding his forehead and the back of his neck. The man was tall and very thin. His right leg ended in a wooden peg fitted with a rubber washer. He leaned on a stick.

Having left the square, he stepped down into the roadway of the Rue Pierre-Charron. There he turned round and looked carefully, from several different points.

That minute examination brought him back to one of the trees on the open space. With the tip of his stick he gently touched a stomach that was showing. The stomach drew itself in.

The officer walked on.

This time he went off for good, by the Rue Pierre-Charron, towards the centre of Paris. In this way he reached the Avenue des Champs-Élysées, which he went up on the left-hand pavement.

Two hundred paces further on there was a large private house, converted, as a banner across it announced, into a hospital.

The officer took up a position some way off, where he could not be seen by the people coming out of it, and waited.

A quarter to seven struck, then seven.

A few more minutes went by.

Five people left the house. Then two more. At last a lady appeared on the threshold of the entrance hall, a nurse in a long blue cloak marked with the red cross.

“Here she is,” murmured the officer.

She took the road he had taken himself and reached the Rue Pierre-Charron, which she followed along the right-hand pavement, making her way towards the crossing with the Rue de Chaillot.

She went lightly, with a supple, even step. The wind that met her quick walk filled out the long blue veil floating round her shoulders. In spite of the fullness of the cloak, you could make out the rhythm of her hips and the youth in the way she moved.

The officer stayed behind her and walked along with an absent air, twirling his stick, like a man out for a stroll.

At that moment there was nobody else to be seen in that part of the street but the two of them.

But just as she had crossed the Avenue Marceau, and well before he reached it himself, a car that had been standing along the avenue pulled out and began to run in the same direction as the young woman, keeping an interval between them that did not change.

It was a taxi. And the officer noticed two things: first, that there were two men inside it; and second, that one of these men, whose face he was able to make out for a moment, cut across by a heavy moustache and topped by a grey felt hat, was leaning out of the door almost the whole time and talking to the driver.

The nurse, meanwhile, walked on without turning round. The officer had changed pavements and was quickening his pace, all the more because it seemed to him that the car was picking up speed as the young woman came nearer the crossroads.

From where he was, the officer took in nearly the whole of the little square at a glance, and, sharp as his eyes were, he could make out nothing in the shadows that would have betrayed the presence of the seven maimed men. Nor was there a passer-by. Nor a carriage. Only on the horizon, in the darkness of the wide avenues that crossed one another, two trams with their blinds down disturbed the silence.

Nor did the young woman, supposing that she paid any attention to what was going on in the street, appear to see anything that could have alarmed her. She gave not the slightest sign of hesitating. And the manoeuvring of the car behind her could not have struck her either, for she did not turn round once.

The car, however, was gaining on her. As they came up to the square there were ten to fifteen metres at most between it and the nurse, and when she, still absorbed in her thoughts, reached the first of the trees, the car came closer still and, leaving the middle of the roadway, began to run along the kerb, while on the side away from the pavement, the left, that is to say, the man who had been leaning out had opened the door and was climbing down onto the step.

ContextContexte

Maurice Leblanc (1864–1941)

Leblanc was a Norman from Rouen with a dozen years of careful, modestly selling fiction behind him when, in 1905, the publisher Pierre Lafitte asked him for a story for his new magazine, Je sais tout, and he wrote one about a thief arrested on an ocean liner. Two years later Arsène Lupin was the most famous criminal in France, and Leblanc spent the rest of his life in his company. English readers have known the canon almost from the start, but only through translations made in Leblanc's lifetime, quickly and with liberties. The Lupin Project gives the whole canon in modern English, complete and unabridged, one translator and one voice. It closes a circle for the house: Leblanc built Lupin out of newspaper reports on Alexandre Marius Jacob, whose memoirs gave Night Workers its name.

Leblanc était un Normand de Rouen, auteur depuis une douzaine d'années de romans soignés et peu vendus, quand l'éditeur Pierre Lafitte lui demanda en 1905 une nouvelle pour sa jeune revue, Je sais tout ; il écrivit celle d'un voleur arrêté sur un transatlantique. Deux ans plus tard, Arsène Lupin était le criminel le plus célèbre de France, et Leblanc passa le reste de sa vie en sa compagnie. Le lectorat anglophone connaît le canon presque depuis le début, mais seulement par des traductions faites du vivant de Leblanc, vite et avec des libertés. The Lupin Project donne le canon entier en anglais moderne, complet et sans coupure, un traducteur et une voix. Il boucle une boucle pour la maison : Leblanc a bâti Lupin à partir des comptes rendus de presse sur Alexandre Marius Jacob, dont les mémoires ont donné son nom à Night Workers.
